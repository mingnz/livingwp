# AI Adoption in the AEC Sector in New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper

New Zealand’s AEC sector is broadening from pilots into bounded use of AI agents, infrastructure intelligence, civil-design automation and off-site construction knowledge systems. Evidence remains uneven, mostly self-reported and without a sector-wide adoption baseline.

## Introduction

This edition reviews publicly available evidence published between 1 November 2025 and 1 November 2026.

AI adoption across New Zealand’s architecture, engineering and construction system is increasing, but remains uneven across organisation size, discipline and project stage.

The strongest evidence is concentrated in:

- Large-language-model assistants for technical documents, procedures, tender material and organisational knowledge.
- Machine learning for infrastructure condition assessment, flood forecasting, building operation and asset renewal.
- Computer vision, LiDAR and spatial intelligence for traffic and site safety.
- AI-supported civil land-development design.
- AI-based knowledge management for off-site and modular construction.
- Professional education, workforce capability and AI governance.

The most significant new evidence during the period is the University of Auckland and BRANZ research on an LLM-based knowledge system for off-site construction. The proof-of-concept used structured domain knowledge, ontology-based reasoning and LLMs to improve the retrieval and reuse of modular-construction knowledge. It provides stronger evidence than previously available for AI in New Zealand prefabricated and off-site construction research. (BRANZ, “Knowledge sharing for off-site construction with artificial intelligence in New Zealand,” Yang Zou and Zhen Zhang, 1 August 2026.) ([branz.co.nz](https://www.branz.co.nz/design-build/publications/knowledge-sharing-for-off-site-construction-with-artificial-intelligence-in-new-zealand?utm_source=openai))

Operational evidence remains strongest for bounded, information-rich use cases:

- First-pass document and procedure review.
- Tender and proposal information retrieval.
- Field reporting and voice-based information capture.
- Stormwater blockage detection and pipe-condition prediction.
- Civil grading, drainage and land-development design.
- Flood-risk assessment and infrastructure resilience.
- Computer-vision and LiDAR monitoring.
- Smart-building control research.

No independently measured, sector-wide New Zealand adoption rate was identified. The AEC Working Group survey launched on 30 July 2026 remains an important attempt to establish a baseline, but publicly reported findings were not located by 1 November 2026. (AI Forum NZ AEC Working Group, “AI in AEC Survey: Share Your Perspective,” 30 July 2026; “Knowledge Hub Archive.”) ([aec.aiforum.org.nz](https://aec.aiforum.org.nz/knowledgehub/ai-in-aec-survey-share-your-perspective/?utm_source=openai))

The evidence base is still dominated by:

- Organisation-authored case studies.
- Vendor-supported case studies.
- Professional webinars and knowledge-hub outputs.
- University research, theses and course material.
- Government and professional-body guidance.

Reported productivity, cost and time benefits should therefore be treated as indicative unless independently benchmarked.

Public evidence remains limited for:

- Scaled AI authoring in architectural practice.
- Autonomous structural or civil engineering design in New Zealand.
- AI-enabled construction supply-chain optimisation.
- AI-enabled prefabricated manufacturing in live New Zealand production environments.
- Autonomous general construction.
- Independently measured productivity, safety or carbon outcomes attributable specifically to AI.

## Policy and Frameworks

The AI Blueprint for Aotearoa, refreshed in May 2026, is the principal national ecosystem framework relevant to the AEC Working Group. It sets a direction to 2030 for innovative, responsible and inclusive AI, with emphasis on adoption, capability, talent, trust, social licence, data sovereignty and sustainable AI. (AI Forum New Zealand, “AI Blueprint for Aotearoa: a refreshed vision to 2030,” 6 May 2026.) ([aiforum.org.nz](https://aiforum.org.nz/2026/05/06/ai-blueprint-for-aotearoa-a-refreshed-vision-to-2030/?utm_source=openai))

The Blueprint is organised around five strategic pillars:

- New opportunities.
- Increasing capabilities and scaling innovation.
- Enhancing adoption and managing risks.
- Building talent.
- Global reach.

The 2026 refresh adds stronger emphasis on:

- Social licence and public trust.
- Sustainable AI, including energy, water, sovereignty and long-term capability.
- Indigenous AI and Te Ao Māori perspectives.
- Data governance and authenticity.
- Workforce education and responsible adoption.

These themes are directly relevant to AEC because built-environment AI can affect public infrastructure, worker safety, property and land information, environmental decisions, cultural data and long-lived assets.

The AEC Working Group provides a sector mechanism for connecting national AI priorities with architecture, engineering, construction, infrastructure and public-sector practice. Its stated activities include sector research, case studies, AI literacy, governance, capability development and collaboration between industry, government, academia, Māori experts and communities. (AI Forum NZ AEC Working Group, “AEC Working Group.”) ([aec.aiforum.org.nz](https://aec.aiforum.org.nz/?utm_source=openai))

The Public Service AI Work Programme to 2027 remains relevant to councils, infrastructure owners, regulators and public agencies using AI in planning, asset management, consenting, compliance or service delivery. It is organised around:

- Common-use AI tools.
- Safe and responsible AI.
- Customers and partnerships.
- AI workforce capability.

The programme includes public-sector AI registries, shared patterns and tools, sandbox environments, workforce training, AI assurance and measurement of use. (Government Digital Delivery Agency, “Public Service AI Work Programme.”) ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/standards-and-guidance/technology-and-architecture/artificial-intelligence/public-service-ai-work-programme?utm_source=openai))

The 2026 cross-agency public-sector survey reported:

- 545 AI use cases across 59 organisations.
- 432 cases from 42 public-service organisations.
- 113 cases from 17 wider public-sector organisations.
- 167 cases in operational phases.
- Generative AI as the most common technology, followed by natural language processing, agentic AI and machine learning.

The survey is not AEC-specific, but it indicates that public-sector AI use is moving beyond planning and experimentation. The reported barriers included capability, cost, security, privacy, reliability, public acceptance and data sovereignty. (Government Digital Delivery Agency, “Report: 2026 cross-agency survey of use cases for artificial intelligence,” 19 August 2026.) ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/dmsdocument/264~report-2026-cross-agency-survey-for-artificial-intelligence-ai-use-cases/html?utm_source=openai))

The Ministry for Regulation’s Responsible AI in Action guidance is relevant to regulators and agencies involved in building, planning, infrastructure and environmental decisions. It supports the use of AI for information processing, pattern recognition and decision support, while retaining human responsibility for regulatory decisions, legal interpretation and accountability. (Ministry for Regulation, “Helping regulators use AI with confidence,” 27 May 2026; “Responsible AI in action.”) ([regulation.govt.nz](https://www.regulation.govt.nz/news/helping-regulators-use-ai-with-confidence/?utm_source=openai))

Standards New Zealand consulted on the potential adoption of more than 60 international standards covering:

- AI governance and risk management.
- AI data quality.
- AI lifecycle processes.
- Transparency and explainability.
- Bias and trustworthiness.
- Information security and privacy.
- Biometric technologies.

The AI standards consultation closed on 8 October 2026. This signals a move towards greater alignment with international AI governance and assurance practice. (Standards New Zealand, “Help shape the future of technology standards in New Zealand,” 20 August 2026; “P5002D Artificial Intelligence Standards.”) ([standards.govt.nz](https://www.standards.govt.nz/news-and-updates/help-shape-the-future-of-technology-standards-in-new-zealand?utm_source=openai))

The Biometric Processing Privacy Code 2025 is relevant to AI-enabled facial recognition, voice recognition, gait analysis, worker identification and site-access systems. It came into force on 3 November 2025, with the transition period for existing processing ending on 3 August 2026. (Office of the Privacy Commissioner, “Biometric Processing Privacy Code 2025.”) ([privacy.org.nz](https://www.privacy.org.nz/privacy-principles/codes-of-practice/biometric-processing-privacy-code/?utm_source=openai))

Engineering New Zealand’s AI programme frames AI as an assistant rather than a professional decision-maker. Its material emphasises:

- Verification of key outputs.
- Human responsibility for engineering decisions.
- Protection of confidential project and client information.
- Caution when applying AI to code compliance and safety assessments.
- Continued reliance on physical inspection, engineering judgement and professional sign-off.

The programme also includes AI learning opportunities, case studies, governance material and guidance on responsible use in engineering practice. (Engineering New Zealand, “Engineering and AI.”) ([engineeringnz.org](https://www.engineeringnz.org/programmes/engineering-and-ai/))

The AEC Working Group’s governance outputs identify recurring issues involving:

- Accountability for AI-assisted work.
- Data sovereignty and confidentiality.
- Intellectual property and copyright.
- Contracts, insurance and procurement.
- Māori data governance and cultural participation.
- Human review of technical and safety-critical outputs.
- The risk of AI-generated claims, variations or compliance material overwhelming contractual processes.

(AI Forum NZ AEC Working Group, “Potholes in the AI Expressway: AI vs Construction Contracts,” 18 September 2026.) ([aec.aiforum.org.nz](https://aec.aiforum.org.nz/knowledgehub/podcast-potholes-in-the-ai-expressway-ai-vs-construction-contracts/?utm_source=openai))

The current governance environment is therefore distributed rather than contained in one AEC-specific instrument. It combines:

- National AI policy.
- Public-sector assurance.
- Privacy and biometric requirements.
- Professional obligations.
- Contractual and intellectual-property arrangements.
- Māori data governance and cultural participation.
- Cybersecurity and information-management controls.
- Existing building, engineering, environmental and health-and-safety responsibilities.

## Research Overview

### Industry-led research and reports

The AEC Working Group knowledge hub remains the most concentrated public source of New Zealand-specific material on AI in the built environment. Its coverage during the period included:

- Critical-infrastructure AI agents.
- Stormwater asset intelligence.
- Construction lifecycle data.
- LiDAR and traffic safety.
- Civil-design automation.
- Tender and proposal workflows.
- Contractual and governance risk.
- Professional education and capability.

Much of this material is early-stage, organisation-reported or based on webinars rather than independent evaluation. (AI Forum NZ AEC Working Group, “Knowledge Hub Archive.”) ([aec.aiforum.org.nz](https://aec.aiforum.org.nz/knowledgehub/?filter_category=case-studies))

Engineering New Zealand’s Engineering and AI programme has expanded the professional capability base through:

- AI case studies.
- AI governance material.
- Professional-practice guidance.
- Workshops and webinars.
- Demonstrations covering engineering workflows from problem definition through design review.

The programme indicates that capability development is advancing faster than independently measured sector deployment. (Engineering New Zealand, “Engineering and AI.”) ([engineeringnz.org](https://www.engineeringnz.org/programmes/engineering-and-ai/))

The 2026 New Zealand construction-sector evidence continues to associate AI with productivity pressure, labour constraints, tendering, administration and decision-making. However, public industry reports generally measure sentiment or intention rather than verified AI outcomes.

### NZ university capabilities and recent studies

Publicly visible university activity during the period is concentrated in:

- Off-site construction knowledge management.
- Flood forecasting and flood-risk assessment.
- Earthquake and structural-damage classification.
- Smart-building control.
- Civil-engineering education in machine learning and generative AI.
- Computer vision and infrastructure monitoring.
- Climate and hazard resilience.

The University of Auckland and BRANZ report on off-site construction is the most directly relevant new study. It reviewed knowledge-management barriers in modular and prefabricated construction, then developed a prototype using:

- Large language models.
- Ontology-based reasoning.
- Semantic-web technologies.
- A domain-specific knowledge base.
- Project-specific document retrieval.

The prototype encoded representative modular-connection design options and linked them to supporting documents. Testing with 26 architecture, engineering and construction professionals produced an average usability score of 82.8. The research reported improvements in faithfulness, relevance and recall when structured domain knowledge was combined with LLM retrieval. It remains a proof of concept and has not been demonstrated as a live commercial system across New Zealand projects. (Yang Zou and Zhen Zhang, “Knowledge sharing for off-site construction with artificial intelligence in New Zealand,” BRANZ, 1 August 2026.) ([branz.co.nz](https://www.branz.co.nz/design-build/publications/knowledge-sharing-for-off-site-construction-with-artificial-intelligence-in-new-zealand?utm_source=openai))

The University of Canterbury’s 2026 Practical Modelling and AI course applies machine learning and generative AI to structural, transport, water, construction-management and geotechnical examples. It teaches verification, validation, calibration, uncertainty analysis and the risks of black-box use. This is evidence of AI capability being incorporated into civil-engineering education rather than evidence of commercial deployment. (University of Canterbury, “ENCI604 Practical Modelling and AI,” 2026.) ([courseinfo.canterbury.ac.nz](https://courseinfo.canterbury.ac.nz/GetCourseDetails.aspx?course=ENCI604&occurrence=26S1%28C%29&year=2026&utm_source=openai))

A University of Canterbury doctoral project uses physics-informed machine learning for flood forecasting in Canterbury catchments in partnership with Environment Canterbury. The research connects AI methods with local water systems, hazard resilience and public decision-making. (University of Canterbury, “UC PhDs to tackle urgent environmental challenges,” 16 July 2026.) ([branz.co.nz](https://www.branz.co.nz/design-build/articles/ai-system-could-help-accelerate-off-site-construction-in-new-zealand?utm_source=openai))

A 2026 University of Canterbury thesis developed hybrid hydrodynamic and machine-learning models for rapid pluvial flood assessment in the Wairewa rural catchment. It demonstrates the use of AI to complement physical models in New Zealand hazard and infrastructure-resilience research. It is not evidence of routine operational deployment. (Andrea Pozo Estívariz, “Advancing methods of rapid flood risk assessment using climate informed hybrid approaches of hydraulic modelling and machine learning,” University of Canterbury, 2026.) ([branz.co.nz](https://www.branz.co.nz/design-build/articles/ai-system-could-help-accelerate-off-site-construction-in-new-zealand?utm_source=openai))

A University of Canterbury-led study on wildfire forecasting used machine learning to analyse weather-station data and detect fire-risk patterns. The evaluated case studies were in Australia, but the researchers identified potential applicability to New Zealand because comparable weather-station networks already exist. The study is therefore best treated as transferable research capability rather than domestic AEC adoption. (University of Canterbury, “AI tool predicts wildfire danger faster than current systems,” 25 March 2026.) ([canterbury.ac.nz](https://www.canterbury.ac.nz/news-and-events/news/2026/ai-tool-predicts-wildfire-danger-faster-than-current-systems-))

A 2026 paper involving Massey University’s School of Built Environment examined machine learning and reinforcement learning for natural-ventilation control in smart buildings. The study was evaluated in a building in Cambridge, Massachusetts, not New Zealand. It demonstrated control of indoor carbon dioxide and temperature targets and highlighted the importance of sensors, actuators, weather data, software integration and safe operating boundaries. (Wei Zhang, Leslie Norford and Wentao Wu, “Development of microservices with machine learning algorithms for natural ventilation control in smart buildings,” Building and Environment, 1 May 2026.) ([sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S036013232600226X?utm_source=openai))

The University of Auckland’s construction-informatics teaching and Smart Digital Lab activity provides additional capability in:

- Infrastructure inspection.
- Occupational health and safety.
- Production planning and control.
- Quake and tsunami evacuation.

These university capabilities demonstrate a broad research base, but public evidence of sustained commercial deployment in New Zealand remains limited. ## Case Studies

### Transpower: bounded AI agents in critical infrastructure

- Context: Transpower faced increasing engineering-methodology reviews and the need to provide rapid access to operational procedures across a complex electricity-transmission system.
- AI method: GPT-based Microsoft 365 Copilot agents operating within an internal environment and connected to approved SharePoint-hosted sources.
- Application: First-pass assessment of engineering-methodology documents and conversational retrieval of operational procedures.
- Observed outcomes: The engineering-review agent reportedly saves approximately 30–45 minutes per review. The procedure agent became the most-used reported AI agent in the relevant operational context.
- Governance pattern: Agents are restricted to approved knowledge sources, while engineers and control-room staff validate outputs.
- Limitation: The results are organisation-reported and have not been independently evaluated in the public case study.

The case demonstrates a bounded-assistant model for critical infrastructure. AI identifies missing information, points users to relevant material and improves retrieval, while professional and operational accountability remains human. (AI Forum NZ AEC Working Group, “Transpower’s AI Agents in Critical Infrastructure,” 16 July 2026.) ([aec.aiforum.org.nz](https://aec.aiforum.org.nz/knowledgehub/case-study-transpowers-ai-agents-in-critical-infrastructure/?utm_source=openai))

### Auckland Council, Mott MacDonald and Lynker Analytics: stormwater asset intelligence

- Context: Auckland Council’s Healthy Waters team needed to integrate fragmented monitoring and asset information across stormwater operations.
- AI method: Image-processing AI for blockage detection and machine learning for pipe-condition prediction.
- Application: Monitoring critical stormwater structures, identifying blockages and flooding risks, and forecasting pipe-condition grades over time.
- Observed outcomes: The case reports more efficient monitoring, faster response and improved targeting of asset-renewal planning.
- Data lesson: The applications depended on sustained work to identify, validate, organise and improve data across siloed systems.
- Limitation: Public evidence does not include independently verified accuracy, cost or lifecycle-performance measures.

This case is significant because it applies AI to public infrastructure operations rather than only to project delivery. It also demonstrates that data preparation and asset-information management were prerequisites for AI use. (AI Forum NZ AEC Working Group, “Data-driven decisions for better city planning and management,” 12 December 2025.) ([aec.aiforum.org.nz](https://aec.aiforum.org.nz/knowledgehub/data-driven-decisions-for-better-city-planning-and-management/?utm_source=openai))

### University of Auckland and BRANZ: AI knowledge system for off-site construction

- Context: New Zealand off-site construction and modular building projects face fragmented knowledge, small-firm capability constraints and technical information stored in PDFs, images and other unstructured formats.
- AI method: A hybrid system combining LLMs, semantic-web technologies, ontology-based reasoning and a domain-specific knowledge base.
- Application: Retrieval and reuse of modular-connection design knowledge, comparison of design options and access to supporting technical documents.
- Observed outcomes: The strongest tested configuration improved reported faithfulness by 38%, relevance by 31% and recall by 120% compared with conventional approaches. User testing with 26 AEC professionals produced an average usability score of 82.8.
- Research lesson: Structured domain knowledge improved the reliability and completeness of LLM responses.
- Limitation: The system was a proof of concept and has not yet been tested across larger, diverse project and regulatory datasets.

This is the clearest recent New Zealand evidence that AI may support off-site construction by improving the reuse of technical and project knowledge. It does not establish AI-enabled factory production or commercial deployment at scale. (BRANZ, “AI system could help accelerate off-site construction in New Zealand,” 20 October 2026; Yang Zou and Zhen Zhang, “Knowledge sharing for off-site construction with artificial intelligence in New Zealand,” 1 August 2026.) ([branz.co.nz](https://www.branz.co.nz/design-build/publications/knowledge-sharing-for-off-site-construction-with-artificial-intelligence-in-new-zealand?utm_source=openai))

### HEB Construction: AI agents for tender biographies and CVs

- Context: HEB Construction had employee resumes and biographies distributed across folders, systems and document versions. Tender teams spent significant time preparing project-specific profiles.
- AI method: Two AI agents built on the Boomi Enterprise Platform. One agent decomposed CVs into structured employee information, while a conversational agent retrieved profiles and generated tailored tender biographies.
- Application: Tender-response preparation and reuse of workforce information.
- Observed outcomes: HEB reported faster turnaround, more consistent drafts and hours saved on each proposal.
- Data lesson: The AI workflow depended on the creation of trusted employee records before generation.
- Governance pattern: HEB stated that people retained accountability and control over the output.
- Limitation: The evidence is based on a vendor-supported announcement and does not provide independently measured productivity or tender-performance results.

The case illustrates a recurring New Zealand pattern: AI is being applied to internal knowledge retrieval, document reuse and proposal work before being applied to safety-critical or autonomous site decisions. (Boomi and HEB Construction, “HEB Construction Activates Talent Data for Accurate, Bid-Ready Tender Responses,” 1 September 2026.) ([streetinsider.com](https://www.streetinsider.com/Business%2BWire/HEB%2BConstruction%2BActivates%2BTalent%2BData%2Bfor%2BAccurate%2C%2BBid-Ready%2BTender%2BResponses/27014193.html))

### Allsite.ai and Civix: AI-assisted civil land-development design

- Context: Land-development design requires repeated grading, roading, drainage and servicing iterations under local engineering and site constraints.
- AI method: AI design agents integrated with Civil 3D and spatial-data workflows.
- Application: Automated or semi-automated surface grading, road design, pipe-network design and design iteration.
- Observed outcomes: Allsite.ai and associated case material report rapid design iterations and use on New Zealand projects, including the Mangawhai Central development. Public company material also reports new AI design-agent releases during 2026.
- Engineering pattern: The AI produces design alternatives within existing civil-engineering workflows, with engineers retaining review and quality-control responsibilities.
- Limitation: Reported performance and project outcomes are primarily company-authored. Public independent evaluation of accuracy, safety, cost and regulatory compliance remains limited.

This case is an important signal for AI entering civil engineering through bounded design automation rather than autonomous professional sign-off. (Allsite.ai, “Allsite.ai” and “Release Notes,” 2026; AI Forum NZ AEC Working Group, “AI-Automated Civil Engineering Design,” 29 May 2026.) ([allsite.ai](https://www.allsite.ai/?utm_source=openai))

### Fitzgerald Construction: AI-assisted tender intelligence in development

- Context: Fitzgerald Construction receives a high proportion of work through tenders, with uneven surges in tender volume and limited internal capacity for reviewing large document packs.
- AI method: A planned AI data-intelligence platform connected to previous tenders, project records, pricing information, staff CVs and subcontractor knowledge.
- Application: Tender triage, requirement identification, risk review, information retrieval, subcontractor identification and checking of draft responses.
- Observed outcomes: No verified outcomes were available at the time of publication. The project established proposed measures covering triage time, response capacity, response quality, tender wins and margin performance.
- Business lesson: The project is framed as a measurable experiment rather than an assumption that AI activity itself creates value.
- Limitation: The system was still being developed, and the expected benefits had not yet been tested.

The case is relevant because it shows a smaller New Zealand contractor seeking to apply AI to a commercially important but information-heavy workflow without claiming completed transformation. (First Focus, “AI in Construction: Fitzgerald Case Study,” 6 October 2026.) ([firstfocus.co.nz](https://firstfocus.co.nz/insights/ai-in-construction-fitzgerald-case-study/))

## Trends and Outlook

- AI adoption is concentrating in narrow, repeatable and information-rich tasks. Document review, procedure retrieval, tendering, asset monitoring, field reporting and civil-design iteration are more visible than fully autonomous design or construction. (AI Forum NZ AEC Working Group, “Transpower’s AI Agents in Critical Infrastructure”; First Focus, “AI in Construction: Fitzgerald Case Study.”) ([aec.aiforum.org.nz](https://aec.aiforum.org.nz/knowledgehub/case-study-transpowers-ai-agents-in-critical-infrastructure/?utm_source=openai))

- Internal knowledge is becoming a primary AI asset. Transpower, HEB, Fitzgerald and the BRANZ off-site-construction research all use AI to structure, retrieve or reuse organisational and project knowledge. This suggests that the first competitive advantage may come from better information access rather than from access to a more powerful general-purpose model. ([aec.aiforum.org.nz](https://aec.aiforum.org.nz/knowledgehub/case-study-transpowers-ai-agents-in-critical-infrastructure/?utm_source=openai))

- Structured domain knowledge is emerging as a reliability layer. The BRANZ research found that ontology-based reasoning and project-specific knowledge improved LLM performance relative to conventional retrieval approaches. This is consistent with the broader AEC pattern of connecting AI to approved, bounded and traceable information sources. ([branz.co.nz](https://www.branz.co.nz/design-build/articles/ai-system-could-help-accelerate-off-site-construction-in-new-zealand?utm_source=openai))

- Civil engineering is becoming one of the most visible design-automation domains in New Zealand. Allsite.ai and related civil-design activity show AI being used for grading, roads, drainage and servicing within established engineering software. The evidence remains mostly company-reported, but the use case is more operationally specific than general architectural image generation. ([allsite.ai](https://www.allsite.ai/?utm_source=openai))

- Off-site construction has moved from a gap in the evidence base to a visible research area. The University of Auckland and BRANZ work addresses knowledge fragmentation and design collaboration, but public evidence of AI-controlled fabrication, robotic manufacturing or scaled factory deployment remains limited. ([branz.co.nz](https://www.branz.co.nz/design-build/publications/knowledge-sharing-for-off-site-construction-with-artificial-intelligence-in-new-zealand?utm_source=openai))

- Public infrastructure remains a leading application context. Stormwater, electricity transmission, flood forecasting, traffic safety and asset renewal provide recurring data, defined problems and visible consequences from better or worse decisions. ([aec.aiforum.org.nz](https://aec.aiforum.org.nz/knowledgehub/case-study-transpowers-ai-agents-in-critical-infrastructure/?utm_source=openai))

- Architecture remains less visible in public New Zealand evidence than infrastructure analytics, engineering support and civil-design automation. No independently evaluated New Zealand case of scaled AI use in architectural production authoring was identified in the reviewed period.

- Professional and educational capability is growing faster than independently measured deployment. University courses, Engineering New Zealand programmes, the AEC Working Group and the University of Waikato AI Day are building AI literacy and governance awareness, but there is limited public evidence of sustained, measured production use across the sector. ([courseinfo.canterbury.ac.nz](https://courseinfo.canterbury.ac.nz/GetCourseDetails.aspx?course=ENCI604&occurrence=26S1%28C%29&year=2026&utm_source=openai))

- Publicly visible New Zealand AI-based startup and specialist activity is concentrated in:

  - Civil land-development design automation.
  - Structural and component design support.
  - AI agents and enterprise knowledge retrieval.
  - Tender and proposal intelligence.
  - Field-data capture and voice interfaces.
  - Computer vision, LiDAR and spatial intelligence.
  - Asset-condition prediction.
  - AI governance and workflow services.

- BEDC is a New Zealand-founded business using AI-supported design services for residential construction, including drawing interpretation, material take-off, estimation and structural design workflows. Its public material describes human-in-the-loop review and operations across New Zealand and international markets. The available evidence is largely company- or partner-authored, and New Zealand-specific deployment outcomes are not independently established. (ASB, “From code to construction: How BEDC is building smarter”; BEDC, “Team.”) ([asb.co.nz](https://www.asb.co.nz/businesshub/resources/case-studies/from-code-to-construction--how-bedc-is-building-smarter.html))

- The main risks emerging from New Zealand evidence are:

  - Incorrect or hallucinated technical information.
  - Misinterpretation of standards, project requirements or procedures.
  - Privacy, surveillance and biometric-processing risks.
  - Leakage of confidential client, project or infrastructure information.
  - Shadow AI through unapproved accounts or tools.
  - Unclear accountability for AI-assisted outputs.
  - Weak independent measurement of claimed benefits.
  - Cultural misrepresentation or inappropriate use of Māori data and mātauranga Māori.
  - Uneven capability between large organisations and smaller firms.
  - Deskilling or reduced learning opportunities if routine tasks are automated without appropriate supervision.
  - Contractual, insurance and intellectual-property uncertainty.
  - AI-generated claims, variations or compliance responses overwhelming project teams.

  These risks are identified across Engineering New Zealand guidance, AEC Working Group material, the construction-contracts series and New Zealand case studies. ([engineeringnz.org](https://www.engineeringnz.org/programmes/engineering-and-ai/))

- Governance is increasingly treated as an adoption condition rather than a later compliance activity. The recurring themes are human accountability, defined boundaries, approved data sources, traceability, review, privacy, cultural legitimacy and security. ([aec.aiforum.org.nz](https://aec.aiforum.org.nz/knowledgehub/case-study-transpowers-ai-agents-in-critical-infrastructure/?utm_source=openai))

- AI is beginning to influence business models through augmentation of existing AEC services. Visible models include:

  - AI-enabled civil-design services.
  - Internal knowledge agents.
  - Infrastructure monitoring and asset analytics.
  - Tender and proposal intelligence.
  - Field-data and reporting services.
  - Recurring digital-assurance and information-management services.

- An emerging business-model signal is a shift from selling labour hours alone towards combining professional judgement with reusable data, workflow agents, monitoring and assurance. This remains an early pattern rather than evidence of sector-wide displacement or transformation.

- The near-term New Zealand outlook is one of governed augmentation. AI is most likely to reduce information friction, improve technical search and checking, increase the frequency of infrastructure observations and support earlier project decisions before enabling broad autonomous delivery.

- Evidence for construction robotics employing AI methods remains limited in New Zealand. Robotics, BIM and reality capture should not automatically be counted as AI adoption unless AI methods and AI-attributable outcomes are explicit.

## A Global Perspective

Global evidence indicates that AEC AI adoption is increasing, but the conversion of pilots into routine, measured practice remains difficult.

The RICS AI in Commercial Property and Construction Report 2026, based on more than 3,100 professionals worldwide, reported:

- Approximately two-thirds of construction respondents using AI in some form.
- 39% of construction organisations in early-stage pilots.
- 19% reporting regular use, compared with 12% in the previous year.
- 37% planning to increase AI investment.
- Skills, integration, data quality, privacy and security remaining significant barriers.
- Nearly two-thirds of construction organisations reporting low AI preparedness.

The report suggests that the global challenge is shifting from initial experimentation to embedding AI in professional practice. (RICS, “Global AI in commercial property and construction 2026: adoption, readiness and the road ahead”; reported 18 August 2026.) ([academy.rics.org](https://academy.rics.org/global-ai-in-commercial-property-and-construction-2026-adoption-readiness-and-the-road-ahead-webinar?utm_source=openai))

The earlier RICS AI in Construction 2025 report provides a lower baseline:

- 45% of organisations reporting no AI implementation.
- 34% in early pilot phases.
- Less than 12% reporting regular use in specific processes.
- Approximately 1.5% reporting use across multiple processes.
- Less than 1% reporting fully embedded organisation-wide use.
- Lack of skilled personnel, integration and data quality among the main barriers.

Together, the two reports indicate increased use but continuing difficulty in moving from isolated pilots to scaled organisational capability. (RICS, “Artificial intelligence in construction report 2025,” September 2025.) ([rics.org](https://www.rics.org/news-insights/artificial-intelligence-in-construction-report?utm_source=openai))

### Global application areas

The principal global application areas are:

- Design optioneering and engineering analysis.
- Cost estimation, quantity take-off and tendering.
- Contract, claims and project-document intelligence.
- Computer-vision progress and quality monitoring.
- Safety and compliance monitoring.
- Predictive maintenance and asset inspection.
- Building-energy optimisation and facilities management.
- Construction robotics and semi-autonomous equipment.

In the United States, the Associated General Contractors’ 2026 outlook survey reported that 61% of respondents either used AI or planned to increase AI investment. Reported uses included office and administrative functions, estimating, design and preconstruction, and recruitment or training. (Associated General Contractors, “2026 Construction Hiring and Business Outlook Report.”) ([agc.org](https://www.agc.org/sites/default/files/users/user21902/2026%20Construction%20Hiring%20and%20Business%20Outlook%20Report_Final2.pdf))

The RIBA 2026 AI survey reported that 74% of architecture practices used AI in at least some projects. Applications covered early design visualisation, project management and routine business activities. RIBA also emphasised professional judgement, ethics, inclusion, environmental effects, standards and a people-first approach. (Royal Institute of British Architects, “RIBA AI Report 2026.”) ([riba.org](https://www.riba.org/work/insights-and-resources/ai-report/))

### Global startup activity

Global AI-based AEC startup activity is moving beyond general-purpose productivity assistants towards domain-specific workflows and physical systems.

Visible categories include:

- Estimating, quantity take-off and cost planning.
- Design generation and design optioneering.
- Contract, claims and project-document analysis.
- Computer-vision progress and quality monitoring.
- Procurement and project controls.
- Facilities-management agents.
- Asset inspection and predictive maintenance.
- Surveying, layout and reality capture.
- Autonomous and semi-autonomous equipment.
- Robotics for finishing, rebar, earthmoving, inspection and site logistics.

Construction robotics is becoming more commercially visible, but remains small relative to total construction expenditure. The Zacua Ventures, Hilti Ventures and 94 Ventures report identifies repeatable deployments in layout, rebar, solar groundworks, scanning, earthmoving and other bounded tasks. It reports that successful systems tend to focus on narrow, high-utilisation workflows rather than attempting to automate the whole site. (Zacua Ventures, Hilti Ventures and 94 Ventures, “Construction Robotics Report 2026,” 5 March 2026.) ([zacuaventures.com](https://zacuaventures.com/construction-robotics-report-2026/))

The report also identifies a transition from scripted robotics towards systems using:

- Deep-learning perception.
- Data-driven planning.
- Embodied AI.
- Robot foundation models.
- Language and vision interfaces.

However, the report states that construction robotics represented less than 0.03% of global construction spend in 2025. Deployment remains concentrated in suitable tasks and projects. ([zacuaventures.com](https://zacuaventures.com/construction-robotics-report-2026/))

### Internal capabilities in major AEC firms

Large AEC organisations are increasingly treating AI capability as an internal strategic asset.

AECOM’s 2025 annual report describes:

- An AI for Engineering platform.
- AI agents embedded in the project and programme lifecycle.
- AI use in proposal drafting, bidding, programme management, engineering workflows and design optimisation.
- A 2025 governance policy separating permissible, high-risk and prohibited uses.
- Additional risk-review processes for AI-related issues.

The disclosure is company-authored and includes forward-looking claims, but it provides clear evidence of a major global AEC firm building internal AI platforms, governance and specialist capability. (AECOM, “2025 Annual Report.”) ([aecom.com](https://aecom.com/wp-content/uploads/documents/reports/2025/AECOM_2025_Annual_Report.pdf))

The global direction is therefore not only towards buying AI software. Large firms are also developing:

- Internal AI and data teams.
- Enterprise knowledge systems.
- Model-evaluation and assurance capability.
- AI governance policies.
- Proprietary workflow data.
- Domain-specific agents.
- Integration between AI and existing project systems.

### Global impacts, risks and learnings

Visible global impacts include:

- Faster document retrieval, reporting and first-draft production.
- More frequent construction-progress and asset-condition observation.
- Increased use of AI in design comparison and optioneering.
- Wider access to organisational knowledge.
- Growth of specialised robotics for repetitive, measurable tasks.
- Formation of internal AI teams and governance systems.
- Greater emphasis on proprietary data and workflow integration.

The principal global learnings are:

- Adoption begins with narrow workflows before enterprise-wide transformation.
- Data quality and integration are larger constraints than model availability.
- Physical construction environments remain difficult for autonomous systems because of variability, clutter, changing site conditions and safety requirements.
- Human oversight remains necessary for professional, safety-critical and regulatory decisions.
- Reported benefits are difficult to isolate when AI is combined with BIM, automation, robotics, sensing and process redesign.
- Large firms have advantages in data, internal capability and investment.
- Smaller firms may face greater barriers in skills, integration and governance.
- Commercial, contractual and insurance models are adapting more slowly than AI capability.
- Startups increasingly seek to control workflow data and become embedded in project or asset operating systems.

The global business-model signal is the emergence of AI-enabled professional and operational services delivered through software, data, monitoring, workflow agents and assurance relationships. The available evidence does not support a conclusion that traditional AEC firms are being replaced.

## Conclusion

AI adoption in New Zealand’s AEC sector is increasing and becoming more operational, but remains uneven, early-stage and poorly measured at sector level.

The strongest verified activity is in:

- AI agents for technical documents and procedures.
- Infrastructure and asset-condition intelligence.
- Civil land-development design automation.
- Tender and proposal information management.
- Computer vision, LiDAR and spatial safety monitoring.
- Environmental, flood and resilience decision support.
- AI knowledge management for off-site construction.
- Engineering education and professional capability development.

The Transpower case remains the clearest example of controlled operational use in a high-consequence environment. Its use of approved knowledge sources, human validation and bounded agents reflects the governance model emerging across the sector. ([aec.aiforum.org.nz](https://aec.aiforum.org.nz/knowledgehub/case-study-transpowers-ai-agents-in-critical-infrastructure/?utm_source=openai))

The BRANZ and University of Auckland off-site-construction research adds a significant new dimension. It demonstrates that AI can improve access to fragmented technical knowledge when combined with structured domain models and expert-defined relationships. However, it remains a proof of concept rather than evidence of AI-enabled commercial manufacturing. ([branz.co.nz](https://www.branz.co.nz/design-build/publications/knowledge-sharing-for-off-site-construction-with-artificial-intelligence-in-new-zealand?utm_source=openai))

The Auckland Council, HEB, Fitzgerald, Allsite.ai and university examples show a broader pattern. New Zealand organisations are applying AI where:

- Data is available or can be structured.
- Processes are repetitive and information-heavy.
- Risks are visible.
- Professional review can remain in the workflow.
- The value of better information can be connected to resilience, safety, cost, capacity or asset management.

Most reported benefits remain self-reported, early-stage or difficult to separate from wider digital transformation. Public evidence remains weakest for:

- Scaled AI authoring in architecture.
- Autonomous structural or civil engineering design.
- AI-enabled offsite manufacturing.
- Supply-chain and logistics optimisation.
- Autonomous general construction.
- Independently benchmarked productivity or safety improvement.

The principal strategic insights are:

- AI is entering AEC through augmentation rather than wholesale automation.
- Internal knowledge, data quality and information boundaries are central adoption conditions.
- Structured domain knowledge may improve the reliability of LLM use in technical AEC settings.
- Professional accountability remains human even when AI performs substantial analytical or drafting work.
- Governance, privacy, Māori data sovereignty, cultural legitimacy and cybersecurity are practical prerequisites for adoption.
- Smaller firms face greater constraints in capability, data readiness and access to specialist expertise.
- New Zealand’s strongest opportunities are connected to local infrastructure, environmental conditions, hazards, public assets and culturally informed decision-making.
- AI capability is becoming an organisational asset involving data, workflows, people, governance and reusable knowledge rather than simply access to a software tool.
- Commercial, contractual and insurance arrangements may become significant constraints as AI moves from drafting and analysis towards operational agents.

The next system-level steps indicated by the evidence are:

- Publish and analyse the AEC Working Group’s sector survey findings.
- Increase the number of independently evaluated New Zealand AEC case studies.
- Separate AI-attributable outcomes from benefits produced by wider digitalisation, robotics or process redesign.
- Strengthen common approaches to AI assurance, traceability, human review and accountability.
- Continue AI literacy and domain-specific education across engineering, architecture, construction and asset management.
- Develop clearer expectations for privacy, biometric processing, Māori data governance, mātauranga Māori and cultural participation.
- Improve project and asset-data quality, ownership, interoperability and continuity.
- Monitor the implications of AI for contracts, procurement, intellectual property, insurance and professional liability.
- Maintain a focus on bounded, measurable use cases before expanding into higher-consequence automation.

Overall, New Zealand AEC is moving from awareness towards governed augmentation. The most likely near-term effect is a gradual reduction in information friction, improved technical review and retrieval, stronger infrastructure visibility, and better decision support within professionally accountable workflows.
