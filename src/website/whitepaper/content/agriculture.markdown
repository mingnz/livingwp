# AI in Agriculture in Aotearoa New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper
*Updated 29 May 2026*

## Introduction

AI in New Zealand agriculture is moving from isolated tools to embedded operating systems. The strongest adoption remains in dairy, where AI is already used in virtual fencing, animal movement, heat and health monitoring, mastitis detection, pasture measurement, and advisory delivery. Horticulture is advancing on a different curve, with concentration in orchard robotics, crop intelligence, disease-risk modelling, and AI-assisted quality forecasting rather than broad whole-farm copilots. Public-sector and research-led AI is also becoming more important in emissions measurement, weed and pest analysis, and biosecurity workflows. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/news/halter-raises-220m-in-series-e-to-accelerate-global-expansion-of-virtual-fencing?utm_source=openai))

This matters in a sector with outsized economic weight. MPI’s latest Situation and Outlook for Primary Industries says food and fibre export revenue is forecast to reach NZ$62.0 billion in the year to 30 June 2026, including NZ$27.4 billion from dairy and NZ$9.2 billion from horticulture. At the policy level, MBIE’s July 2025 AI strategy explicitly favours adoption and application over frontier-model development, and it points to agriculture as a practical domain where New Zealand can tailor AI to local production systems. ([mpi.govt.nz](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/resources-and-forms/economic-intelligence/situation-and-outlook-for-primary-industries/?utm_source=openai))

## Executive Snapshot

- **Dairy is still the commercial centre of gravity.** Halter, Aimer, Bovonic, Herd-i, and DairyNZ together show that the most mature AI use cases are livestock control, pasture allocation, health detection, reproduction support, and trusted knowledge retrieval. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/news/halter-raises-220m-in-series-e-to-accelerate-global-expansion-of-virtual-fencing?utm_source=openai))
- **Adoption is real, but it is not evenly distributed.** DairyNZ’s research says current GenAI use among dairy farmers is still low overall, with strongest near-term value in decision support, task enhancement, communications, and analysis of farm-specific data rather than autonomous farm management. ([dairynz.co.nz](https://www.dairynz.co.nz/media/m11h0z1l/opportunities-of-ai-for-nz-dairy-farmers-dec2025-perrin-ag-final-report.pdf))
- **Horticulture is narrower but strategically significant.** The clearest New Zealand patterns are orchard robotics, disease-risk prediction, precision crop analysis, and post-harvest quality intelligence. ([roboticsplus.co.nz](https://www.roboticsplus.co.nz/home?utm_source=openai))
- **Public-good AI is becoming sector infrastructure.** The on-farm emissions calculator, AI-enabled weed-risk assessment, laser weed control, and AI-supported import health work show AI moving into the operating layer of regulation, science, and sector services. ([agresearch.co.nz](https://www.agresearch.co.nz/news/new-emissions-calculator-gives-farmers-a-clearer-picture-of-on-farm-greenhouse-gases/?utm_source=openai))
- **The market is shifting from “AI tools” to workflow fit.** The strongest New Zealand examples all sit inside a daily workflow farmers already value: moving stock, detecting mastitis, planning grazing, classifying disease risk, or retrieving trusted guidance. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/en-nz/news/halter-launches-world-first-virtual-fencing-via-satellite?utm_source=openai))
- **Capability support is improving, but national research infrastructure is still not fully settled.** The Government has expanded practical SME AI support, while the national AI Research Platform decision had still not been announced on MBIE’s platform page as of late May 2026. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/more-practical-ai-support-for-small-businesses?utm_source=openai))

## What Changed Since 2 April 2026

- **Halter’s scale story strengthened materially.** In March 2026 the company said it serves more than 2,000 farmers and ranchers across New Zealand, Australia, and the US, with one million smart collars sold; in late April it launched direct-to-satellite connectivity for beef cattle in New Zealand through One NZ Satellite powered by Starlink. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/news/halter-raises-220m-in-series-e-to-accelerate-global-expansion-of-virtual-fencing?utm_source=openai))
- **Aimer moved from product scaling to publicly backed expansion.** In May 2026 Aimer said MPI is investing NZ$600,000 in a NZ$1.675 million project to scale its pasture AI tools and develop “Ask AIMER,” a chat-based assistant inside the app; the company says AIMER is already used on more than 650 farms with more than 10,000 pasture measurements recorded each week. ([aimer-farming.com](https://www.aimer-farming.com/blog-articles/aimer-farming-secures-government-co-investment-in-new-ai-tools?utm_source=openai))
- **Government support for practical AI adoption broadened.** On 15 May 2026 MBIE expanded the AI Advisory Pilot from 50 to up to 150 businesses, extended it to 31 January 2027, and kept co-funding of up to 50% capped at NZ$15,000. That is economy-wide rather than agriculture-specific, but it matters for ag SMEs, contractors, processors, and service firms. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/more-practical-ai-support-for-small-businesses?utm_source=openai))
- **The national AI platform remains unresolved.** The agriculture-oriented BioAI proposal is still in contention, but MBIE’s current platform page says only that phase-two proposals were due on 31 March 2026, assessment was in mid-April, and a further announcement timeline would be provided “in due course.” ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/refocusing-the-science-innovation-and-technology-system/public-research-organisations/new-zealand-institute-for-advanced-technology/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform?utm_source=openai))

## Current News and Market Developments

### 1) Livestock AI has moved further into scale economics

Halter is now the clearest New Zealand example of AI embedded in routine livestock management at scale. The company’s March 2026 funding announcement says it serves more than 2,000 farmers and ranchers across New Zealand, Australia, and the US, has sold one million collars, and raised US$220 million in Series E funding at a US$2 billion valuation. That places it well beyond pilot status and into the category of a scaled farm operating system. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/news/halter-raises-220m-in-series-e-to-accelerate-global-expansion-of-virtual-fencing?utm_source=openai))

The April 2026 satellite launch matters because it expands the addressable New Zealand beef market, not just the product feature list. Halter says direct-to-satellite virtual fencing removes the need for on-farm towers, is available in New Zealand immediately for beef operations, and could expand access for New Zealand beef farms by at least 20%. Strategically, that pushes AI deeper into harder hill-country and remote-use cases that were previously connectivity-constrained. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/en-nz/news/halter-launches-world-first-virtual-fencing-via-satellite?utm_source=openai))

### 2) Pasture intelligence is becoming a funded category, not just a startup niche

Aimer remains one of the most New Zealand-specific AI stories because it is built around pasture-based dairy and beef systems rather than imported row-crop assumptions. Its core offer combines smartphone computer vision, pasture forecasting, feed wedges, and automated grazing and supplement planning. The company says AIMER Vision Gen3 improved accuracy by 5–10%, with around 93% of pasture covers falling within 200 kg DM/ha of a platemeter value. ([aimer-farming.com](https://www.aimer-farming.com/blog-articles/aimer-vision-gen3?utm_source=openai))

The more important change since April is institutional backing. Aimer’s May 2026 announcement says MPI is investing NZ$600,000 in a NZ$1.675 million project to scale the system across hundreds of dairy and beef farms and accelerate “Ask AIMER,” a chat-based farm-specific assistant. MPI’s project register says the project is intended to develop an AI agent to optimise emissions, productivity, and profit across pasture types with support from Ravensdown, Cropmark Seeds, and Fonterra. ([aimer-farming.com](https://www.aimer-farming.com/blog-articles/aimer-farming-secures-government-co-investment-in-new-ai-tools?utm_source=openai))

That is a meaningful signal: pasture AI is no longer just about measurement accuracy, but about turning measurement into recommendations, scenario analysis, and farm-specific decision support. ([aimer-farming.com](https://www.aimer-farming.com/blog-articles/aimer-farming-secures-government-co-investment-in-new-ai-tools?utm_source=openai))

### 3) Narrow animal-health AI continues to win on visible ROI

Bovonic’s QuadSense remains one of the strongest examples of narrow AI with clear on-farm economics. The company says more than 4,000 units have been installed across more than 160 farms in New Zealand and Ireland. Its 2025 validation survey of 33 New Zealand farms reported average annual benefits of NZ$29,547 and average payback in about six months. ([bovonic.com](https://bovonic.com/quadsense-built-for-farmers-proven-on-farms/?utm_source=openai))

Bovonic’s broader public materials also point to lower bulk somatic cell counts, labour savings, and reduced antibiotic use among participating farms. That matters because it shows how AI is being bought in practice: not as “AI transformation,” but as a specific fix for mastitis costs, time pressure, and milk-quality risk. ([bovonic.com](https://bovonic.com/?utm_source=openai))

### 4) Institutional AI is becoming part of farmer-facing delivery

DairyNZ has moved from AI commentary into AI deployment. Its DAiSY assistant now draws on more than 1,100 pages and 880 practical tools and resources from the DairyNZ website, responds only from DairyNZ content, and is positioned as a trusted retrieval layer for research-backed farm guidance. ([dairynz.co.nz](https://www.dairynz.co.nz/about-daisy/))

This is important because institution-led AI can lower one of agriculture’s biggest barriers: trust. A sector body using bounded, source-grounded AI is a very different proposition from farmers relying on general-purpose chatbots for operational advice. ([dairynz.co.nz](https://www.dairynz.co.nz/about-daisy/))

### 5) Horticulture AI is still selective, but it is strengthening around automation and crop intelligence

Robotics Plus remains the clearest scaled horticulture-AI platform from New Zealand. Following Yamaha Motor’s 2025 acquisition, the company continues to position its autonomous Prospr platform for orchard and vineyard tasks such as intelligent spraying and weed control, while retaining headquarters and IP development in Tauranga. Robotics Plus describes its systems as continuously improving through monitoring and AI learning. ([roboticsplus.co.nz](https://www.roboticsplus.co.nz/home?utm_source=openai))

Agovor is a useful second signal from the horticulture side. In February 2026 Hort Innovation said the New Zealand-founded company closed an AU$3 million pre-seed round to advance affordable autonomous robots for horticulture. Agovor’s own site positions its electric autonomous towing platform as a labour-saving system for row-based horticulture with remote monitoring and swappable smart attachments. ([horticulture.com.au](https://www.horticulture.com.au/hort-innovation/news-events/media-releases/2026/hort-innovation-venture-fund-announced-second-investment/?utm_source=openai))

Kiwifruit also shows that AI adoption in horticulture is not only about robots. Plant & Food Research reports that Zespri adopted machine learning in 2022 to estimate national kiwifruit supply, with more than 96% accuracy in trays per hectare for SunGold, Green, and organics. Momentum 2026 drew more than 600 growers, shareholders, post-harvest partners, and industry leaders, underscoring the scale and coordination of the sector that can support such tools. ([plantandfood.com](https://www.plantandfood.com/en-nz/article/smartening-up-food-systems?utm_source=openai))

### 6) Public-good AI is growing into sector infrastructure

The on-farm emissions calculator launched in October 2025 remains a significant piece of infrastructure. AgResearch says it is the first tool to apply the government-mandated emissions standard released in December 2024, and that it was developed by MPI with the Bioeconomy Science Institute and Ag Emissions Centre. Its real importance is standardisation: it creates a common base layer that other farm software and advisory tools can potentially build on. ([agresearch.co.nz](https://www.agresearch.co.nz/news/new-emissions-calculator-gives-farmers-a-clearer-picture-of-on-farm-greenhouse-gases/?utm_source=openai))

AgResearch and the Bioeconomy Science Institute are also pushing AI into weed control and biosecurity science. The Institute’s weed-risk model uses big data and AI to speed hazard assessment, and AgResearch’s Map and Zap uses AI-driven identification and laser control for weeds across pastoral, horticultural, and viticultural settings. ([agresearch.co.nz](https://www.agresearch.co.nz/news/big-data-and-ai-turbocharge-weed-risk-research-at-bioeconomy-science-institute/?utm_source=openai))

MPI is now using GenAI in biosecurity workflow reform as well. In July 2025 the Government said proof-of-concept trials showed AI could significantly reduce the time required for parts of the import health standards process, including pest risk analysis and drafting. That is not farm-gate AI, but it is still agricultural infrastructure because import-health responsiveness directly affects seed, plant, and food trade pathways. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/node/125575?utm_source=openai))

## Research Overview

### DairyNZ / Perrin Ag: GenAI value is practical, but mainstream adoption is still early

DairyNZ’s commissioned Perrin Ag report says current GenAI adoption among dairy farmers appears low, but it identifies credible use cases in decision support, contextual analysis, predictive reasoning, writing assistance, and team communications. The report also argues that the next leap will come from embedding AI into existing templates, spreadsheets, and operational workflows rather than expecting farmers to build new systems from scratch. ([dairynz.co.nz](https://www.dairynz.co.nz/media/m11h0z1l/opportunities-of-ai-for-nz-dairy-farmers-dec2025-perrin-ag-final-report.pdf))

The report is especially useful because it captures real on-farm experimentation, including scenario modelling, Monday reporting workflows, and custom GPTs built around farm policies and trusted sources. It also reinforces a recurring market lesson: value increases when AI is grounded in specific farm context and linked to live operational data. ([dairynz.co.nz](https://www.dairynz.co.nz/media/m11h0z1l/opportunities-of-ai-for-nz-dairy-farmers-dec2025-perrin-ag-final-report.pdf))

### Precision dairy research: virtual fencing changes farm systems, not just labour inputs

A 2026 JDS Communications paper on New Zealand pasture-based dairy systems found that virtual fencing adoption affects more than stock movement; it changes how farms are managed and adapted over time. The study draws on interviews with 20 New Zealand dairy farmers and describes virtual fencing as part of wider farm-system change rather than a simple equipment substitution. ([sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666910226000554?utm_source=openai))

That supports a broader inference about AI adoption in New Zealand dairy: the most important technologies are increasingly those that reshape management routines, labour allocation, and decision timing, not just those that automate single tasks. This is an inference from the adoption findings and the commercial examples above. ([sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666910226000554?utm_source=openai))

### AgResearch and the Bioeconomy Science Institute: AI is becoming shared research capability

AgResearch’s 2025 annual report said it had more than 50 AI-focused research projects under way, including work that used AI to analyse more than 20 years of livestock CT-scan data for methane, feed efficiency, welfare, and meat-quality traits. Since then, Bioeconomy Science Institute communications have reinforced that AI is being used not just in analysis but in weed hazard modelling and laser-based field systems. ([agresearch.co.nz](https://www.agresearch.co.nz/assets/Documents/AgR-AR-2025-FULL-v3.6.pdf?utm_source=openai))

Institutionally, this matters because the Bioeconomy Science Institute now sits at the centre of the agriculture-oriented BioAI proposal for the national AI Research Platform. MBIE’s published concept statement says BioAI is designed to drive practical innovation across agriculture, aquaculture, and forestry, though the final national platform decision had not yet been announced as of late May 2026. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/refocusing-the-science-innovation-and-technology-system/public-research-organisations/new-zealand-institute-for-advanced-technology/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform?utm_source=openai))

### Horticulture and plant-science research: disease prediction and supply forecasting are the strongest current use cases

Lincoln Agritech’s STELLA project in Hawke’s Bay is a strong example of AI applied to invisible crop risk. The project combines automated spore sampling, UAV and satellite imagery, environmental monitoring, and AI-powered risk models to improve early detection of bull’s-eye rot in apples. Lincoln Agritech links the work directly to export-risk reduction in a region central to New Zealand apple production. ([lincolnagritech.co.nz](https://lincolnagritech.co.nz/lincoln-agritech-enables-ai-driven-apple-disease-detection-in-new-zealand/?utm_source=openai))

Plant & Food Research’s review of “smartening up food systems” suggests a second strong pattern: crop intelligence and forecasting. Its examples include Zespri’s machine-learning-based national crop estimation and planned algorithmic work on grapevine trunk disease. Together, these show horticulture AI concentrating where quality, timing, and export performance justify data-intensive tools. ([plantandfood.com](https://www.plantandfood.com/en-nz/article/smartening-up-food-systems?utm_source=openai))

## Case Studies

### Halter: AI as a scaled farm operating system

Halter is the clearest proof that AI in New Zealand agriculture can reach genuine operational scale. Its combination of virtual fencing, remote shifting, animal monitoring, heat and behaviour insights, and now direct-to-satellite connectivity makes it a farm operating layer rather than a point solution. The March and April 2026 announcements show both commercial scale and technical expansion. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/news/halter-raises-220m-in-series-e-to-accelerate-global-expansion-of-virtual-fencing?utm_source=openai))

### Aimer Farming: pasture AI designed for New Zealand’s production model

Aimer matters because it fits the country’s pasture-first farming systems. Its smartphone-based measurement, forecasting, grazing plans, and emerging “Ask AIMER” assistant reduce hardware friction and push toward low-cost, farm-specific decision support. The new MPI-backed project is a strong sign that pasture intelligence is becoming a recognised strategic category. ([aimer-farming.com](https://www.aimer-farming.com/blog-articles/aimer-farming-secures-government-co-investment-in-new-ai-tools?utm_source=openai))

### Bovonic QuadSense: narrow AI with immediate economics

QuadSense shows why focused AI spreads faster than general-purpose systems in agriculture. Farmers can attach a direct value to earlier mastitis detection, lower SCC, labour savings, and reduced antibiotic use. Its reported six-month payback profile makes it one of the strongest commercial-adoption cases in the market. ([bovonic.com](https://bovonic.com/quadsense-built-for-farmers-proven-on-farms/?utm_source=openai))

### Robotics Plus and Agovor: horticulture automation where labour pressure is acute

Robotics Plus demonstrates the high-end, globally backed path: autonomous orchard and vineyard machinery developed in New Zealand and scaled with Yamaha. Agovor represents the lighter-weight, affordability-focused end of the same trend: smaller autonomous electric systems for repetitive row-crop tasks. Together they show horticulture AI splitting into premium autonomous platforms and more accessible modular field robots. ([roboticsplus.co.nz](https://www.roboticsplus.co.nz/home?utm_source=openai))

### Lincoln Agritech / STELLA: AI for export-risk management

STELLA is one of the strongest examples of AI protecting high-value export outcomes rather than just lifting labour productivity. Bull’s-eye rot is hard to detect early, and the project’s sensor-plus-model approach is designed to move intervention earlier in the orchard. That makes it strategically important for premium fruit supply chains. ([lincolnagritech.co.nz](https://lincolnagritech.co.nz/lincoln-agritech-enables-ai-driven-apple-disease-detection-in-new-zealand/?utm_source=openai))

## Core Trends

### 1) AI adoption is strongest where the workflow is already digitised

Dairy continues to lead because it already has collars, herd data, milk-quality data, reproductive records, and advisory infrastructure that AI can plug into. The combination of Halter, Bovonic, Herd-i, and DairyNZ is not accidental; it reflects a sector with the digital substrate needed to support AI use. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/news/halter-raises-220m-in-series-e-to-accelerate-global-expansion-of-virtual-fencing?utm_source=openai))

### 2) The market is shifting from tools to operating layers

The most successful products are not “AI features” bolted onto generic software. They are systems embedded in grazing management, mastitis screening, knowledge retrieval, weed control, and disease prediction. That is why operational fit now appears more important than technical novelty. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/en-nz/news/halter-launches-world-first-virtual-fencing-via-satellite?utm_source=openai))

### 3) Horticulture AI is more selective and more capital-concentrated than dairy AI

Compared with dairy, horticulture adoption is clustered in higher-value crops and packhouse or orchard environments where labour scarcity, quality control, and export risk justify robotics and crop-intelligence investment. The strongest examples are orchard autonomy, disease-risk modelling, and crop estimation rather than broad farm copilots. ([roboticsplus.co.nz](https://www.roboticsplus.co.nz/news/prospr-driving-a-new-future-for-viticulture-multi-use-autonomous-vehicle-on-show-at-unified-wine-and-grape-symposium?utm_source=openai))

### 4) Public-good AI is becoming part of national agricultural capability

The emissions calculator, weed-risk assessment, weed-zapping systems, import-health workflow reform, and the proposed BioAI platform all point in the same direction: AI is becoming part of agricultural infrastructure, not just private vendor software. ([agresearch.co.nz](https://www.agresearch.co.nz/news/new-emissions-calculator-gives-farmers-a-clearer-picture-of-on-farm-greenhouse-gases/?utm_source=openai))

### 5) Trust, source-grounding, and explainability remain essential

DairyNZ’s bounded assistant, the Perrin Ag findings, and the slow diffusion of GenAI all indicate that farmers will adopt AI faster when outputs are traceable, limited to trusted sources, and clearly linked to known farm decisions. In agriculture, “can I trust this?” still matters more than “is this clever?” ([dairynz.co.nz](https://www.dairynz.co.nz/about-daisy/))

## Constraints and Risks

The main barriers are now clearer than they were a year ago:

- **Trust and validation:** farmers need outputs that are reliable in real farm conditions and grounded in defensible sources. ([dairynz.co.nz](https://www.dairynz.co.nz/media/m11h0z1l/opportunities-of-ai-for-nz-dairy-farmers-dec2025-perrin-ag-final-report.pdf))
- **Interoperability:** value is still lost when data sits across separate apps, devices, and advisory systems. DairyNZ’s research points directly to the integration challenge. ([dairynz.co.nz](https://www.dairynz.co.nz/media/eh0nza1b/proceedings-4th-international-precision-dairy-farming-conference-new-zealand-2025.pdf?utm_source=openai))
- **SME capability gap:** MBIE says 68% of SMEs had no plans to evaluate or invest in AI in the cited 2024 Spark/NZIER survey, even as larger firms moved faster; the May 2026 AI Advisory Pilot expansion is a response to that gap, not proof that it has been solved. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/business-and-employment/economic-growth/digital-policy/new-zealands-ai-strategy-investing-with-confidence/new-content-page-4?utm_source=openai))
- **Proof of value:** products with visible ROI, like mastitis detection or labour-saving pasture tools, are diffusing faster than broad “AI platform” propositions. ([bovonic.com](https://bovonic.com/quadsense-built-for-farmers-proven-on-farms/?utm_source=openai))
- **National coordination remains incomplete:** the AI Research Platform is still pending final confirmation, so New Zealand’s long-term agriculture-AI research architecture is clearer than it was in 2025 but not fully settled yet. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/refocusing-the-science-innovation-and-technology-system/public-research-organisations/new-zealand-institute-for-advanced-technology/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform?utm_source=openai))

## Conclusion

As of **29 May 2026**, AI adoption in New Zealand agriculture is best described as **practical, selective, and increasingly infrastructure-like**. Dairy remains the most commercially advanced segment, led by scaled livestock systems, pasture intelligence, targeted animal-health tools, and trusted advisory interfaces. Horticulture is progressing through robotics, disease-risk prediction, and crop-quality intelligence rather than through broad whole-orchard copilots. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/news/halter-raises-220m-in-series-e-to-accelerate-global-expansion-of-virtual-fencing?utm_source=openai))

The most important change since the 2 April 2026 update is not one single launch. It is the tightening connection between **commercial deployment, public co-investment, institutional trust layers, and national science infrastructure**. Halter has expanded both scale and connectivity reach; Aimer has secured government co-investment for its next AI layer; DairyNZ has put a bounded AI assistant into production; MBIE has widened practical adoption support for SMEs; and the BioAI concept remains a live candidate for national research infrastructure. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/news/halter-raises-220m-in-series-e-to-accelerate-global-expansion-of-virtual-fencing?utm_source=openai))

The near-term winners in Aotearoa agriculture are likely to be the same kinds of systems already emerging: products that combine **workflow fit, trusted data grounding, measurable ROI, and interoperability with the realities of pasture-based and export-oriented production**. New Zealand agriculture is not chasing frontier AI for its own sake; it is building a more local, more applied stack around the problems farmers and growers will actually pay to solve. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/business-and-employment/economic-growth/digital-policy/new-zealands-ai-strategy-investing-with-confidence?utm_source=openai))
