# AI in Creative Industries in Aotearoa New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper  
**Updated: May 29, 2026**

## Introduction

AI adoption in Aotearoa New Zealand’s creative industries has continued to broaden since the previous update on **April 2, 2026**, but the story has become more specific. The sector now has three clearer layers: **measurable creator adoption**, **formal governance across public-facing creative institutions**, and **a new phase of commercial and workforce restructuring** in agencies, journalism, and screen production. The most important recent shift is that debate is no longer only about whether AI is entering creative work; it is now about **which kinds of work it is replacing, which parts of the value chain it is improving, and how trust, rights, and cultural integrity are being protected.** ([mch.govt.nz](https://www.mch.govt.nz/news/new-zealand-creatives-embrace-digital-tools?utm_source=openai))

That matters because New Zealand’s creative economy is structurally exposed to both the upside and downside of AI. In the year to **March 2025**, the arts and creative sector averaged **122,013 filled jobs**, accounted for **4.4% of all filled jobs**, generated **$18.521 billion in GDP in 2025**, contained **38,413 businesses**, and had a **28.4% self-employment rate**—well above the national rate of 15.2%. In other words, this is a sector dominated by small organisations, freelancers, and portfolio workers, where AI can improve productivity quickly but can also reshape entry-level pathways and bargaining power just as quickly. ([mch.govt.nz](https://www.mch.govt.nz/sites/default/files/2026-04/arts-creative-sector-profile.pdf))

## Executive Snapshot

- **Adoption is now established, not speculative.** Manatū Taonga reported on **March 18, 2026** that **seven in 10 creators use digital tools** in their creative or cultural practice, and **65% of those digital-tool users are using generative AI**. Use is strongest in idea support, with substantial use also in producing creative work. ([mch.govt.nz](https://www.mch.govt.nz/news/new-zealand-creatives-embrace-digital-tools?utm_source=openai))
- **The post-April story is increasingly about capability and labour structure.** Massey University’s May 2026 pilot study found no “bloodbath” of job losses yet, but it did find AI being used to replace low-value administrative tasks and some of the low-paid, freelance, and unpaid work that traditionally helps people get a foot in the door. ([massey.ac.nz](https://www.massey.ac.nz/about/news/pilot-study-shows-generative-ai-use-in-creative-industries-impacts-entry-level-roles/))
- **Governance is thickening across major cultural institutions.** NZFC, NZ On Air, TVNZ, and RNZ all now have public AI principles or assessment guidance that emphasise human oversight, transparency, rights management, and protections for te ao Māori and Indigenous cultural and intellectual property. ([nzfilm.co.nz](https://www.nzfilm.co.nz/about-us/ai-strategy))
- **Commercial creative is shifting from “tool use” to operating-model redesign.** Recent New Zealand agency and martech developments point to AI changing pricing, workflow measurement, discoverability, and service design—not just speeding up content production. ([campaignbrief.co.nz](https://campaignbrief.co.nz/2026/04/13/foundry-collective-launches-the-agency-group-built-for-the-ai-era/))
- **Trust remains the limiting factor in public-facing media.** JMAD’s 2026 trust data shows around **60% of New Zealanders remain uncomfortable** with news mostly produced by AI with some human oversight, while comfort is higher when journalism is mainly human with AI assistance. ([jmadresearch.com](https://www.jmadresearch.com/_files/ugd/a95e86_b1937c0c5f82498088e43cbb5552215d.pdf))

## What Changed Since the April 2, 2026 Update

- **The AI and Creativity Summit moved from upcoming to realised sector infrastructure.** The summit ran on **May 5–6, 2026** across Auckland and Wellington, with programming focused on news, synthetic media, copyright, attribution, social licence, and creative practice, alongside the One Minute AI Film Festival. That indicates AI convening is no longer peripheral; it is now a recurring part of the sector’s professional calendar. ([technewzealand.org.nz](https://technewzealand.org.nz/event/ai-and-creativity-summit-2026/?utm_source=openai))
- **A refreshed national AI Blueprint added sharper creative-industry priorities.** The AI Forum’s May 2026 Blueprint gives the creative industries a 2030 vision in which AI “powers the business of creativity,” while naming 2026 priorities around trust, adoption data, training, transparency, and stronger protection for practitioners and their work. ([aiforum.org.nz](https://aiforum.org.nz/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/NZT009-AI_Blueprint_Report-v05.pdf))
- **Workforce concerns became more concrete.** The Massey pilot study, presented at the summit on **May 6, 2026**, pushed the conversation beyond abstract fear by identifying pressure on entry-level pathways even while respondents reported few clear-cut examples of outright job destruction so far. ([massey.ac.nz](https://www.massey.ac.nz/about/news/pilot-study-shows-generative-ai-use-in-creative-industries-impacts-entry-level-roles/))
- **Commercial adoption became more visible in agency economics.** April and May brought new local examples of AI-enabled agency formation, workflow platforms, and AI-led transformation offerings, suggesting that in advertising and adjacent creative services the business model itself is now being redesigned around AI. ([campaignbrief.co.nz](https://campaignbrief.co.nz/2026/04/13/foundry-collective-launches-the-agency-group-built-for-the-ai-era/))
- **Government support for practical AI uptake widened.** On **May 15, 2026**, MBIE announced an expansion of the AI Advisory Pilot and broader business support via the Great New Zealand AI Roadshow. Given the creative sector’s high SME and self-employment profile, this matters even though it is economy-wide rather than arts-specific. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/more-practical-ai-support-for-small-businesses?utm_source=openai))

## Current News

### 1) Sector-wide convening and public debate

The **AI and Creativity Summit 2026** framed the current sector agenda around trust, misinformation, rights, cultural perspectives, skills, and real-world creative workflows. Its programme explicitly asked whether AI is expanding creative agency or quietly de-skilling practitioners, which closely matches the concerns now surfacing in both industry events and academic research. ([aicreativeindustries.nz](https://aicreativeindustries.nz/programme/))

The literary sector is also now publicly engaging with AI as a rights and authorship issue. The **Auckland Writers Festival** programmed **“Authors and AI: A Moment of Reckoning?”** on **May 15, 2026**, with Copyright Licensing New Zealand and author Catherine Chidgey on the panel, showing that AI has moved into mainstream publishing discourse in Aotearoa rather than remaining a specialist tech-policy topic. ([writersfestival.co.nz](https://www.writersfestival.co.nz/programmes/event/authors-and-ai-a-moment-of-reckoning/2223496/?utm_source=openai))

### 2) Practical support for experimentation

The **FutureFrames:NZ 2026** competition launched as a nationwide AI short-film initiative backed by the **New Zealand Film Commission** and **NZ On Air**, with workshops in May and awards in July. It is a notable signal because it combines experimentation, skills development, and industry-supported discussion of ethical use in a format designed for working filmmakers, not just policymakers. ([screenguild.co.nz](https://www.screenguild.co.nz/news/articles/futureframes-nz-2026-launches-short-films-big-shifts?utm_source=openai))

The **Creative Tech Accelerator** at Te Puna Creative Hub also remains one of the clearest practical capability-building examples in the country. Its first cohort was showcased in February and reported publicly in March, with a second cohort opening thereafter. The programme is explicitly designed around AI-enabled production-ready outcomes and community-based skills access. ([nzist.ac.nz](https://www.nzist.ac.nz/news/creative-tech-accelerator-showcases-future-ai-enabled-creativity?utm_source=openai))

## Research Overview

### Official adoption baseline

The strongest single adoption datapoint remains Manatū Taonga’s March 2026 release: **70% of creators use digital tools**, and among those users **65% use generative AI**. Nearly half use those tools to support or refine ideas, one in three use them to produce art and creative work, and 14% use them to share creations more widely. At the same time, more than a third of creators who do not use digital tools cite lack of technological skills or knowledge as a barrier. ([mch.govt.nz](https://www.mch.govt.nz/news/new-zealand-creatives-embrace-digital-tools?utm_source=openai))

### Workforce and skills research

Massey University’s May 2026 pilot study is important because it adds a workforce lens that the broader adoption survey does not. Interviewees reported caution in commercial deployment and did not identify widespread direct job loss yet, but the study found AI increasingly used for low-value administration and for work previously done by junior, freelance, or unpaid contributors. That suggests a likely pressure point around **creative career entry routes**, even before large-scale headcount reductions appear. This is an inference from the study’s findings, but it is a strong one. ([massey.ac.nz](https://www.massey.ac.nz/about/news/pilot-study-shows-generative-ai-use-in-creative-industries-impacts-entry-level-roles/))

### Journalism research

AUT’s **JMAD** published the first 2026 baseline report on AI, journalism, and news media in Aotearoa New Zealand, finding AI-assisted news production common across local newsrooms. JMAD’s trust research, published in April 2026, adds the audience side: roughly **60%** of New Zealanders remain uncomfortable with news mostly produced by AI with some human oversight, while **27%** are comfortable with news produced mainly by human journalists with some AI help. ([openrepository.aut.ac.nz](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/cbea9155-0239-4658-b706-28039c7b0bb6))

### Music and creator-rights research

In music, the APRA AMCOS regional evidence remains the most important reference point. It shows creators see AI as both useful and threatening: **54%** agreed AI can assist the human creative process, while **82%** worried it could stop them making a living, and APRA AMCOS estimated that **23% of music creators’ revenues could be at risk by 2028**. For Māori creators, cultural appropriation risk remains especially acute. ([apraamcos.co.nz](https://www.apraamcos.co.nz/about/supporting-the-industry/research-papers/aiandmusic?utm_source=openai))

## Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Stuff’s Democracy.AI

Stuff’s **Democracy.AI** remains the strongest operational case study in New Zealand journalism. It scans local government documents, helps surface stories, and supports drafting and summarisation, while final stories are edited and published with human checks and oversight. In 2026, the tool also won a global **INMA** award for Best Use of AI in a customer-facing product, adding external validation to an already strong local case. ([advertise.stuff.co.nz](https://advertise.stuff.co.nz/news/kiwi-designed-ai-tool-wins-global-media-award?utm_source=openai))

The strategic significance is bigger than workflow efficiency. Democracy.AI shows a New Zealand model of AI that is **public-interest oriented**: using automation to widen local accountability reporting rather than to generate generic content at scale. ([advertise.stuff.co.nz](https://advertise.stuff.co.nz/news/kiwi-designed-ai-tool-wins-global-media-award?utm_source=openai))

### Case Study 2: Screen-sector experimentation through FutureFrames:NZ

FutureFrames:NZ shows how the screen sector is trying to domesticate AI through guided experimentation rather than uncontrolled adoption. The competition uses hybrid filmmaking formats, hands-on workshops, mentorship, and public support from NZFC and NZ On Air, which effectively turns AI learning into a structured professional-development pathway. ([screenguild.co.nz](https://www.screenguild.co.nz/news/articles/futureframes-nz-2026-launches-short-films-big-shifts?utm_source=openai))

### Case Study 3: Creative capability pipeline via Creative Tech Accelerator

The Creative Tech Accelerator is one of the clearest examples of AI capability-building aimed at emerging talent and non-traditional pathways. Its design—linking Microsoft, NZIST, Seen Ventures, local government, and social development support—suggests a growing New Zealand preference for **place-based, applied AI training** rather than purely theoretical upskilling. ([nzist.ac.nz](https://www.nzist.ac.nz/news/creative-tech-accelerator-showcases-future-ai-enabled-creativity?utm_source=openai))

### Case Study 4: Commercial creative’s move to AI-native operations

Recent commercial moves such as **Foundry Collective**, **Manifest**, and **Pead’s GEO/AEO offering** show that the commercial frontier is shifting beyond content generation. Foundry positions AI as the engine inside an integrated strategy-and-performance system; Manifest is built around measuring human and AI-driven work in real time; and Pead is monetising a new problem altogether—how brands appear in AI-generated answers. Together, these examples indicate that in advertising and communications, AI is now reshaping **workflow design, pricing logic, and discoverability strategy**. ([campaignbrief.co.nz](https://campaignbrief.co.nz/2026/04/13/foundry-collective-launches-the-agency-group-built-for-the-ai-era/))

## Governance and Policy Environment

Public-sector and publicly funded creative institutions are converging around a recognisable New Zealand governance model: **human-first, transparent, rights-aware, and culturally specific**. NZ On Air requires applicants to disclose AI use fully, demonstrate capability and governance, and address cultural authenticity in relation to **te ao Māori and mātauranga Māori**. NZFC’s principles similarly prioritise human creativity, rights protection, Te Tiriti, ICIP, transparency, fairness, and accountability, and its production-investment guidelines now explicitly ask applicants to outline proposed AI use in funding applications. ([nzonair.govt.nz](https://www.nzonair.govt.nz/documents/1277/NZ_On_Air_-_AI_Content_Creator_Guidance.pdf))

In broadcasting, TVNZ’s March 2026 principles allow AI in recommendations, advertising, and some creative workflows, but require human validation, guardrails for commissioned content, and disclosure where AI plays a role in news. TVNZ states it does **not generate new editorial content for news**. RNZ’s updated 2026 principles take an even more conservative line: AI may assist with research, administration, summarisation, and production tasks, but RNZ says it generally will not knowingly publish material created by generative AI. ([corporate.tvnz.co.nz](https://corporate.tvnz.co.nz/assets/Uploads/TVNZ-AI-PRINCIPLES-FINAL.pdf))

The risk environment is also sharpening. The Office of the Privacy Commissioner’s **February 23, 2026** joint statement on AI-generated imagery highlighted non-consensual intimate imagery, defamatory depictions, and risks to children and other vulnerable groups. For creative industries, that makes synthetic media governance a live operational issue, not just an abstract ethics topic. ([privacy.org.nz](https://www.privacy.org.nz/tuhono-connect/statements-media-releases/joint-statement-on-ai-generated-imagery/?utm_source=openai))

## Key Trends

### Trend 1: Adoption is spreading fastest where AI removes drudgery
The most mature New Zealand examples still cluster around summarisation, transcription, document analysis, workflow acceleration, post-production support, translation, and ideation—not fully autonomous creative output. ([advertise.stuff.co.nz](https://advertise.stuff.co.nz/news/kiwi-designed-ai-tool-wins-global-media-award?utm_source=openai))

### Trend 2: The hardest labour issue is probably entry-level erosion
The Massey findings suggest the biggest immediate workforce effect may be the quiet removal of junior and speculative work rather than headline layoffs. That is especially important in a sector with high self-employment and many small firms. This is an inference, but it is strongly supported by the available evidence. ([massey.ac.nz](https://www.massey.ac.nz/about/news/pilot-study-shows-generative-ai-use-in-creative-industries-impacts-entry-level-roles/))

### Trend 3: Agency economics are moving from hours to outcomes
Commercial creative firms are starting to reorganise around AI-enabled efficiency, value-based pricing, workflow telemetry, and AI-era discoverability. The business-model shift is now as important as the creative-tool shift. ([campaignbrief.co.nz](https://campaignbrief.co.nz/2026/04/29/manifest-raises-2m-pre-seed-round-to-get-agencies-battle-ready-for-the-ai-era/))

### Trend 4: Human oversight is still the dominant legitimacy rule
Across TVNZ, RNZ, NZFC, and NZ On Air, the recurring principle is clear: AI may support work, but human accountability, verification, and disclosure remain central to acceptable use. ([rnz.co.nz](https://www.rnz.co.nz/assets/cms_uploads/000/000/534/RNZ_AI_Principles_2026.pdf?utm_source=openai))

### Trend 5: Rights, provenance, and Māori cultural integrity remain unresolved fault lines
Whether in screen, publishing, or music, the most persistent disputes concern training data, consent, attribution, remuneration, and protection of Māori cultural material and knowledge systems. ([nzfilm.co.nz](https://www.nzfilm.co.nz/about-us/ai-strategy))

## Conclusion

As of **May 29, 2026**, AI adoption in Aotearoa New Zealand’s creative industries is no longer best described as experimental. It is better described as **selectively operational, unevenly distributed, and increasingly governed**. The clearest evidence still points to strongest uptake in workflow-heavy environments such as journalism, screen post-production, commercial creative, and creative-tech training, while rights-sensitive domains such as music, publishing, and Indigenous cultural expression remain more contested. ([mch.govt.nz](https://www.mch.govt.nz/news/new-zealand-creatives-embrace-digital-tools?utm_source=openai))

Since the **April 2, 2026** edition, the sector has advanced in three ways: it has built stronger public forums for debate, produced more specific evidence about workforce impacts, and moved further into organisational redesign—especially in agencies and media. The overall pattern is now clear: New Zealand is not pursuing an “AI replaces creativity” model. It is building a **human-first, trust-sensitive, culturally bounded** model in which AI is acceptable when it improves productivity, expands access, or supports storytelling without undermining authorship, cultural integrity, or public trust. ([technewzealand.org.nz](https://technewzealand.org.nz/event/ai-and-creativity-summit-2026/?utm_source=openai))

The near-term state of play can be summarised simply: **adoption is broadening, governance is maturing, commercial structures are shifting, and the key unresolved issues are labour pathways, rights, provenance, and Māori data and cultural integrity.** ([aiforum.org.nz](https://aiforum.org.nz/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/NZT009-AI_Blueprint_Report-v05.pdf))
