# AI in Finance in Aotearoa New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper
**Snapshot date: May 29, 2026**

## Introduction

AI adoption in New Zealand finance is moving forward, but the shape of that progress remains **controlled rather than autonomous**. The strongest public evidence still points to supervised use in fraud prevention, customer communications, adviser support, internal workflow automation, analytics, and increasingly, payments infrastructure. Since the April 2, 2026 edition, the most important change is not a sudden leap to fully automated finance; it is the combination of **sharper regulatory risk language from the Reserve Bank, deeper open-banking implementation, and clearer evidence that AI-enabled finance products will scale first where data access, payment initiation, and human accountability are strongest**. ([rbnz.govt.nz](https://www.rbnz.govt.nz/financial-stability/financial-stability-report/financial-stability-reports/2026/may/financial-stability-report-may-2026/web-version))

## Executive Summary

- **The core pattern still holds:** AI in New Zealand finance is real, but mostly in narrow, governed use cases rather than unconstrained decision-making. The FMA’s sector baseline found AI use concentrated in fraud detection, risk, decision support, product development, and customer service ambitions; the FMA’s March 2026 advice review then showed AI being used primarily to extend adviser capacity rather than replace advisers. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/library/research/understanding-ai-in-financial-services/?utm_source=openai))
- **The biggest change since April 2 is the strengthening of the operating layer.** Open banking is moving from framework to implementation detail, with MBIE consulting in April and again on May 28, 2026 on business-channel and delegation issues, while Kiwibank’s payment-initiation milestone falls at the end of May / June 1, 2026. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/have-your-say/consultation-on-exposure-draft-amendment-open-banking-regulations-relating-to-business-banking-digital-channels?utm_source=openai))
- **Advice remains the clearest measured adoption frontier.** The FMA’s March 25, 2026 review remains the strongest quantified evidence of live AI-enabled finance in New Zealand: 40 Financial Advice Providers had a digital advice facility, and about 165,000 retail clients received digital advice in the prior year that resulted in acquiring a financial product. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/news/all-releases/media-releases/improve-new-zealanders-access-to-financial-advice/?utm_source=openai))
- **Payments are becoming the next practical AI-enablement layer.** API Centre records show multiple contracted third parties and bank partnerships in market, and BNZ/BlinkPay said on May 27, 2026 that they had demonstrated open-banking payments appearing in a recipient BNZ account in 2.6 seconds. ([apicentre.paymentsnz.co.nz](https://www.apicentre.paymentsnz.co.nz/join/api-standards-user/current-standards-users/))
- **Defensive AI still leads offensive AI.** ANZ’s anti-phishing collaboration remains one of the strongest production examples, with more than 5,000 phishing domains disrupted in two months and a 39% reduction in ANZ card-phishing cases over the same period. ([anz.com.au](https://www.anz.com.au/newsroom/new-zealand/2026/01/Banks-and-telcos-use-kiwi-tech-to-block-phishing-websites/?utm_source=openai))
- **Regulatory concern is now more explicit.** In its May 6, 2026 Financial Stability Report, RBNZ said AI could amplify cyber and operational risk, including dependency on a small number of third-party AI providers and the risk of biased, misleading, or fraudulent outputs. ([rbnz.govt.nz](https://www.rbnz.govt.nz/financial-stability/financial-stability-report/financial-stability-reports/2026/may/financial-stability-report-may-2026/web-version))

## What’s New Since April 2, 2026

### 1) RBNZ has sharpened the risk framing around AI

The clearest new development since the last edition is the Reserve Bank’s **May 6, 2026 Financial Stability Report**. RBNZ explicitly linked greater AI adoption to higher cyber and operational vulnerability, warning that dependence on a small number of third-party AI providers could create concentration risk and that AI-generated outputs could be biased, misleading, or fraudulent. It also said geopolitical shocks can intensify cyber risk as the financial sector becomes more digital. This does not amount to a retreat from AI, but it does mark a harder-edged supervisory stance: AI is now being discussed as a **financial-stability and operational-resilience issue**, not just an innovation topic. ([rbnz.govt.nz](https://www.rbnz.govt.nz/financial-stability/financial-stability-report/financial-stability-reports/2026/may/financial-stability-report-may-2026/web-version))

### 2) Open banking has moved deeper into implementation and edge-case design

Since early April, MBIE has shifted from broad rollout to **implementation refinement**. In April 2026 it consulted on draft amendments to extend regulated open banking to a wider range of business banking digital channels. On **May 28, 2026**, it opened a further technical consultation on delegation systems and professional trust accounts. That is strategically important for AI in finance because higher-value use cases in cashflow tools, treasury workflows, lender onboarding, and SME finance often depend on business-account permissions and delegated authority, not just retail payments. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/have-your-say/consultation-on-exposure-draft-amendment-open-banking-regulations-relating-to-business-banking-digital-channels?utm_source=openai))

### 3) Kiwibank is reaching the payments milestone, making open banking more nationally complete

MBIE’s regime requires Kiwibank to support open-banking payments from **June 1, 2026**, and Kiwibank’s current public materials say customers can make payments through open banking from **May 2026**, with account-information sharing from **November 2026**. The API Centre’s current implementation plan and standards-user register likewise show Kiwibank providing payment-initiation APIs by **May 30, 2026** and account-information APIs by **November 30, 2026**. This matters because Kiwibank’s inclusion broadens competitive coverage and makes AI-enabled payment and personal-finance experiences more feasible across a larger share of the market. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/open-banking-regulations-now-in-force?utm_source=openai))

### 4) BNZ and BlinkPay have demonstrated what the next phase can look like

On **May 27, 2026**, BNZ said BlinkPay had proven real-time payments can work in New Zealand using open banking, with test payments appearing in a recipient BNZ account in **2.6 seconds** from customer approval. This is not the same as an economy-wide instant-payments rail, but it is a concrete demonstration that bank-account payments are becoming more programmable and responsive. For AI in finance, that matters because recommendation engines and agents become more commercially useful when they can act on trusted, low-friction payment rails rather than stop at analysis. ([bnz.co.nz](https://www.bnz.co.nz/about-us/news/blinkpay-and-bnz-prove-real-time-payments-capability-using-open-banking?utm_source=openai))

## Current State of AI Adoption in New Zealand Finance

### Sector-wide position

New Zealand finance is still best described as **selectively operational**. The FMA’s 2024 research found all participating firms either used generative AI already or expected to soon, with current use concentrated in fraud detection, risk management, decision support, and product functions. The FMA’s March 2026 advice review then showed the sector moving from internal experimentation into more visible client-facing processes, while still emphasizing that the dominant model is adviser augmentation under supervision rather than adviser replacement. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/library/research/understanding-ai-in-financial-services/?utm_source=openai))

### Where adoption is strongest

The areas with the clearest public evidence of traction are:

- **Fraud, scam, and cyber defence.** This remains the most mature and easiest-to-justify AI category in New Zealand finance. ([anz.com.au](https://www.anz.com.au/newsroom/new-zealand/2026/01/Banks-and-telcos-use-kiwi-tech-to-block-phishing-websites/?utm_source=openai))
- **Financial advice and adviser operations.** Digital advice, summarisation, compliance support, record creation, and personal-finance engagement tools are the best-documented client-facing uses. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/news/all-releases/media-releases/improve-new-zealanders-access-to-financial-advice/?utm_source=openai))
- **Internal productivity and workflow support.** ANZ’s February 2026 Agentforce deployment is a prominent example of AI being used to automate routine tasks and surface customer context for frontline bankers. ([anz.com.au](https://www.anz.com.au/newsroom/media/2026/february/anz-launches-agentic-ai-powered-crm-to-transform-business-banking/?utm_source=openai))
- **Payments and open-banking-enabled services.** This is becoming the sector’s most important enabling layer for future AI products, especially where secure consented data access and payment initiation are required. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/open-banking-regulations-now-in-force?utm_source=openai))

### What has not changed

The centre of gravity is **still not autonomous lending, autonomous underwriting, or fully automated retail advice**. Public evidence continues to show AI being deployed first where outcomes are easier to review, humans remain accountable, and errors are easier to catch before they become conduct failures. That reading is consistent across the FMA’s financial-services research, the March 2026 advice review, and RBNZ’s stability framing. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/library/research/understanding-ai-in-financial-services/?utm_source=openai))

## Recent News and Market Developments

### Advice and wealth: hybrid advice is still the main scaling story

The March 2026 FMA review remains the strongest single data point for AI in finance because it documents real client-facing scale rather than aspiration. It found 40 FAPs with digital advice facilities, about 165,000 retail clients receiving digital advice that resulted in acquiring a financial product, and a range of live tools including AI-supported analysis, compliance checking, record-keeping, and proactive financial-wellbeing tools. In an FMA podcast published on **April 2, 2026**, the regulator said it plans roundtables, meetings, and seminars with the sector to explore how AI-supported advice can be delivered with more compliance certainty. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/news/all-releases/media-releases/improve-new-zealanders-access-to-financial-advice/?utm_source=openai))

### Banking and payments: the rails are becoming usable, not just available

Open banking is no longer just a policy headline. API Centre material shows the ecosystem now includes numerous contracted third parties, including Akahu, BlinkPay, POLi, Qippay, Volley, Worldline, and others, while BNZ says it has over **30 collaborations** across open-banking-related partners. The current register also shows Kiwibank joining the standards-based payments layer by the end of May 2026. This is important because AI products in finance need not only models, but **permissioned data access, authentication, and transaction execution**. ([apicentre.paymentsnz.co.nz](https://www.apicentre.paymentsnz.co.nz/join/api-standards-user/current-standards-users/))

A more forward-looking development is the growing evidence that action-taking systems are being tested under controlled conditions. Westpac NZ and Mastercard said in February 2026 they completed New Zealand’s first fully authenticated agentic transactions on Mastercard’s network, while BNZ and BlinkPay’s May 2026 announcement showed open-banking payments reaching a recipient account in seconds. Together, these do not mean agentic finance is mainstream, but they do show New Zealand finance beginning to experiment with AI and API-driven systems that can move from **advice to action** inside controlled payment environments. ([westpac.co.nz](https://www.westpac.co.nz/about-us/media/mastercard-completes-nzs-first-authenticated-agentic-transactions-with-westpac/?utm_source=openai))

### Fraud and scams: production proof still lives here

The ANZ-led phishing-disruption collaboration remains one of the strongest examples of operational AI-like detection and coordinated intelligence in New Zealand finance. ANZ said the collaboration with 2degrees, Kiwibank, TSB, One NZ, Spark, and Trade Me disrupted more than **5,000 phishing domains** in two months and reduced ANZ customer card-phishing cases by **39%** over the same period. This reinforces the broader pattern: the strongest ROI and the clearest board-level case for AI in New Zealand finance still comes from **defensive applications**. ([anz.com.au](https://www.anz.com.au/newsroom/new-zealand/2026/01/Banks-and-telcos-use-kiwi-tech-to-block-phishing-websites/?utm_source=openai))

The most relevant evidence stack for this topic now is:

- **RBNZ, May 6, 2026:** Financial Stability Report linking AI adoption to cyber, concentration, and operational-resilience risks. ([rbnz.govt.nz](https://www.rbnz.govt.nz/financial-stability/financial-stability-report/financial-stability-reports/2026/may/financial-stability-report-may-2026/web-version))

- **Payments NZ / API Centre, current implementation and standards-user material:** operational evidence on bank readiness, third-party participation, and ecosystem maturity. ([apicentre.paymentsnz.co.nz](https://www.apicentre.paymentsnz.co.nz/standards/implementation/minimum-open-banking-implementation-plan/?utm_source=openai))

### What this body of evidence now says

Taken together, the public evidence suggests New Zealand finance is following a distinct path:

- **AI scales fastest where reviewability is strongest.** Advice support, scam detection, summarisation, and workflow automation lead because they are easier to supervise. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/assets/Reports/Access-to-advice-Financial-advice-sector-challenges-and-opportunities.pdf?utm_source=openai))
- **Open banking is becoming the enabling substrate for the next phase.** AI value in finance increasingly depends on clean consent, identity, account access, and payment initiation. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/open-banking-regulations-now-in-force?utm_source=openai))
- **Governance has become product architecture.** The more finance firms move toward AI-assisted action, the more they need auditable flows, consent management, uptime standards, and third-party controls. ([rbnz.govt.nz](https://www.rbnz.govt.nz/financial-stability/financial-stability-report/financial-stability-reports/2026/may/financial-stability-report-may-2026/web-version))
- **New Zealand’s public evidence still favours augmentation over replacement.** The most visible deployments help staff, advisers, and customers complete tasks faster rather than handing over end-to-end authority. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/news/all-releases/media-releases/improve-new-zealanders-access-to-financial-advice/?utm_source=openai))

## Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Financial advice is moving into hybrid AI-supported delivery

The FMA’s March 2026 review shows digital advice is no longer marginal. With **40 FAPs** reporting a digital advice facility and about **165,000** retail clients receiving digital advice that resulted in acquiring a financial product, the regulator now has measurable evidence of client-facing adoption. The uses described include AI agents, compliance-checking tools, summarisation, record creation, and proactive personal-finance tools, but the FMA’s framing is clear: the predominant use is still to **support advisers**, not replace them. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/news/all-releases/media-releases/improve-new-zealanders-access-to-financial-advice/?utm_source=openai))

**Why it matters**
- It is the clearest quantified evidence that AI has moved beyond back-office experimentation into live New Zealand financial workflows. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/assets/Reports/Access-to-advice-Financial-advice-sector-challenges-and-opportunities.pdf?utm_source=openai))
- It suggests the likely winning model is **hybrid advice**: digital journeys, AI support, and human accountability. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/news/all-releases/media-releases/improve-new-zealanders-access-to-financial-advice/?utm_source=openai))

### Case Study 2: BNZ and BlinkPay show how AI-ready finance needs AI-ready rails

BNZ and BlinkPay said on **May 27, 2026** that open-banking payments were tested from customer approval to funds appearing in a BNZ account in **2.6 seconds**. On its own, that is a payments story. Strategically, it is also an AI story: intelligent assistants become far more useful when they can help a user decide **and** complete a transaction through trusted infrastructure. ([bnz.co.nz](https://www.bnz.co.nz/about-us/news/blinkpay-and-bnz-prove-real-time-payments-capability-using-open-banking?utm_source=openai))

**Why it matters**
- It points to the next stage of finance AI in New Zealand: systems that combine recommendation, orchestration, and rapid execution. ([bnz.co.nz](https://www.bnz.co.nz/about-us/news/blinkpay-and-bnz-prove-real-time-payments-capability-using-open-banking?utm_source=openai))
- It shows that the limiting factor for many future AI products may be less about model quality and more about access to secure payment and consent infrastructure. This is an inference from the payments and open-banking evidence. ([bnz.co.nz](https://www.bnz.co.nz/about-us/news/blinkpay-and-bnz-prove-real-time-payments-capability-using-open-banking?utm_source=openai))

### Case Study 3: ANZ’s anti-phishing collaboration shows why defensive AI still leads

ANZ’s phishing-disruption partnership shows that the most mature AI-like finance deployments are often **ecosystem deployments**, not isolated bank tools. The collaboration with banks, telcos, and digital platforms disrupted more than **5,000 malicious domains** in two months and ANZ said customer card-phishing cases fell **39%** over that same period. ([anz.com.au](https://www.anz.com.au/newsroom/new-zealand/2026/01/Banks-and-telcos-use-kiwi-tech-to-block-phishing-websites/?utm_source=openai))

**Why it matters**
- It provides one of the clearest examples of measurable operational value from AI-enabled detection and response. ([anz.com.au](https://www.anz.com.au/newsroom/new-zealand/2026/01/Banks-and-telcos-use-kiwi-tech-to-block-phishing-websites/?utm_source=openai))
- It reinforces that New Zealand finance still finds its strongest adoption path where AI improves resilience, not where it replaces judgment. ([anz.com.au](https://www.anz.com.au/newsroom/new-zealand/2026/01/Banks-and-telcos-use-kiwi-tech-to-block-phishing-websites/?utm_source=openai))

### Case Study 4: Westpac and Mastercard preview controlled agentic commerce

Westpac NZ and Mastercard’s February 2026 authenticated agentic transactions are still early, but strategically important. They suggest that AI-driven commerce in New Zealand finance is likely to enter the market first in **highly controlled, authenticated payment contexts** rather than through unconstrained autonomous agents acting across retail banking. ([westpac.co.nz](https://www.westpac.co.nz/about-us/media/mastercard-completes-nzs-first-authenticated-agentic-transactions-with-westpac/?utm_source=openai))

**Why it matters**
- It offers a realistic picture of how agentic finance may emerge locally: consented, traceable, standards-based, and perimeter-controlled. ([westpac.co.nz](https://www.westpac.co.nz/about-us/media/mastercard-completes-nzs-first-authenticated-agentic-transactions-with-westpac/?utm_source=openai))
- It aligns with the broader regulatory preference for human accountability and strong operational controls. ([rbnz.govt.nz](https://www.rbnz.govt.nz/financial-stability/financial-stability-report/financial-stability-reports/2026/may/financial-stability-report-may-2026/web-version))

## Key Trends

### Trend 1: Defensive AI still leads the market

Fraud detection, phishing disruption, cyber monitoring, and risk screening remain the most credible large-scale AI use cases in New Zealand finance. These areas offer measurable loss reduction and relatively clear governance. ([anz.com.au](https://www.anz.com.au/newsroom/new-zealand/2026/01/Banks-and-telcos-use-kiwi-tech-to-block-phishing-websites/?utm_source=openai))

### Trend 2: Advice is becoming the most visible customer-facing scaling channel

The FMA’s March 2026 evidence remains the strongest proof that AI in finance is reaching customers at scale, but mainly through hybrid advice models rather than autonomous robo-advice. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/news/all-releases/media-releases/improve-new-zealanders-access-to-financial-advice/?utm_source=openai))

### Trend 3: Open banking is becoming the action layer for future AI products

The combination of MBIE regulation, API Centre standards, active third-party contracts, Kiwibank’s rollout, and BNZ/BlinkPay’s live proof point means open banking is increasingly the practical infrastructure for AI-enabled payments, budgeting, cashflow tools, and smarter onboarding. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/open-banking-regulations-now-in-force?utm_source=openai))

### Trend 4: Governance is no longer separate from innovation

Performance standards, consent rules, business-channel edge cases, and third-party concentration are now part of the product problem, not just the compliance problem. The more AI tools move closer to customer action, the more these controls become commercially essential. ([apicentre.paymentsnz.co.nz](https://www.apicentre.paymentsnz.co.nz/news/articles/180000-open-banking-payments-in-aotearoa-last-month--whats-next/))

### Trend 5: AI concentration and cyber dependency are rising as strategic risks

RBNZ’s May 2026 language makes clear that concern has shifted from generic caution to specific worries about reliance on a small number of AI providers, biased or misleading outputs, and amplified cyber threats. ([rbnz.govt.nz](https://www.rbnz.govt.nz/financial-stability/financial-stability-report/financial-stability-reports/2026/may/financial-stability-report-may-2026/web-version))

## Constraints and Risks

### Trust and conduct risk

The FMA’s advice work continues to imply that customer-facing AI will grow most easily where products are simpler, outcomes are explainable, and humans remain visibly accountable. That makes conduct design, disclosure, and oversight central constraints on scaling. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/news/all-releases/media-releases/improve-new-zealanders-access-to-financial-advice/?utm_source=openai))

### Infrastructure and ecosystem dependency

New Zealand’s market is small, and many critical technology layers are shared. RBNZ’s concern about dependence on a small number of third-party AI providers sits alongside the practical dependence on shared cloud, API, and payments infrastructure. That creates correlated risk even as it accelerates innovation. ([rbnz.govt.nz](https://www.rbnz.govt.nz/financial-stability/financial-stability-report/financial-stability-reports/2026/may/financial-stability-report-may-2026/web-version))

### Incomplete payments modernisation

Open banking is improving quickly, but the wider payments stack is still in transition. BNZ/BlinkPay’s proof point is impressive precisely because New Zealand does not yet have a universal instant-payments environment for everyday retail use. This means some of the most advanced AI-finance experiences will remain constrained by rail availability and interoperability until the broader payments environment modernises further. This is an inference from the open-banking implementation evidence and BNZ/BlinkPay’s positioning of its test as a proof of capability. ([bnz.co.nz](https://www.bnz.co.nz/about-us/news/blinkpay-and-bnz-prove-real-time-payments-capability-using-open-banking?utm_source=openai))

## Overall Assessment

As of **May 29, 2026**, AI in New Zealand finance is best understood as **operationally real, infrastructure-dependent, and still tightly bounded by governance**. The strongest customer-facing evidence still sits in hybrid financial advice. The strongest operational evidence still sits in fraud and scam defence. And the most important enabling shift since the last edition is the steady construction of a more usable open-banking layer, now moving into business-account and delegated-authority detail while Kiwibank joins the payments phase. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/assets/Reports/Access-to-advice-Financial-advice-sector-challenges-and-opportunities.pdf?utm_source=openai))

The sector is therefore not moving toward “autonomous finance” in a broad sense. It is moving toward **AI plus rails**: AI plus regulated data access, AI plus secure payment initiation, AI plus adviser oversight, AI plus stronger resilience controls. That combination is much more consistent with the evidence than any narrative of wholesale replacement. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/news/all-releases/media-releases/improve-new-zealanders-access-to-financial-advice/?utm_source=openai))

In short: **New Zealand finance is entering a deeper implementation phase of AI.** Since April 2, 2026, the biggest story is not bigger models; it is better plumbing, harder-edged risk supervision, and clearer proof that the near-term winners will be firms that combine AI with trusted payments, consented data, and reviewable decisions. ([rbnz.govt.nz](https://www.rbnz.govt.nz/financial-stability/financial-stability-report/financial-stability-reports/2026/may/financial-stability-report-may-2026/web-version))
