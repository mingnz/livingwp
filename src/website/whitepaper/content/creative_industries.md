# AI in Creative Industries in Aotearoa New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper

**Updated: August 18, 2026**  
**Previous update: July 13, 2026**  
**Coverage: Publicly available information through August 18, 2026**

## Introduction

AI adoption across Aotearoa New Zealand’s creative industries is broadening, but the dominant model remains **human-led augmentation rather than full automation**.

The strongest national adoption baseline remains Manatū Taonga’s 2025 Cultural Participation Survey, released in March 2026. It found that **70% of creators use digital tools** in their creative or cultural practice, and **65% of those digital-tool users use generative AI**. Nearly half use AI to support or refine ideas, approximately one-third use it to produce creative work, and 14% use it to distribute work more widely. ([mch.govt.nz](https://www.mch.govt.nz/news/new-zealand-creatives-embrace-digital-tools?utm_source=openai))

Since the previous update, the evidence base has become more substantial. New research from Massey University examines the effects of generative AI on creative work and entry-level roles; AUT research provides a detailed case study of AI-assisted documentary podcast production; and sector discussions have moved further toward questions of workflow redesign, rights, authenticity, cultural sovereignty, and the distribution of economic value. ([massey.ac.nz](https://www.massey.ac.nz/about/news/pilot-study-shows-generative-ai-use-in-creative-industries-impacts-entry-level-roles/?utm_source=openai))

The current state can be summarised as follows:

> **AI is becoming normalised inside creative workflows, while its use in final public-facing output remains more contested.**

---

## Executive Snapshot

- **Adoption is already mainstream among digitally enabled creators.** Seven in 10 creators use digital tools, and 65% of those users also use generative AI. ([mch.govt.nz](https://www.mch.govt.nz/news/new-zealand-creatives-embrace-digital-tools?utm_source=openai))
- **Most current use is assistive.** Common applications include research, ideation, summarisation, transcription, audio restoration, script development, editing, content adaptation, audience analysis, and marketing optimisation. ([doi.org](https://doi.org/10.24135/ijcmr.v13iapril.167?utm_source=openai))
- **The strongest public evidence of operational deployment remains in journalism, marketing, podcasting, screen production, and visual effects.**
- **Entry-level creative work is emerging as a major concern.** Massey’s pilot research suggests that AI may affect traditional gateway roles even where practitioners cannot yet identify widespread direct job displacement. ([massey.ac.nz](https://www.massey.ac.nz/about/news/pilot-study-shows-generative-ai-use-in-creative-industries-impacts-entry-level-roles/?utm_source=openai))
- **Governance is moving from general principles into funding processes.** NZFC funding applications now require applicants to outline proposed AI use and address cultural authenticity, creative integrity, transparency, and ethical and industry impacts. ([nzfilm.co.nz](https://www.nzfilm.co.nz/assets/resources/Final-Published-HPTRM-Version-June-2026.pdf?utm_source=openai))
- **Māori cultural and intellectual property remains a distinctive Aotearoa issue.** National AI guidance identifies risks involving cultural misappropriation, data sovereignty, and the unauthorised use of mātauranga Māori. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/sites/default/files/2025-07/New%20Zealand%27s%20AI%20Strategy%20-%20Investing%20with%20confidence.pdf?utm_source=openai))
- **The commercial sector is shifting from content generation to AI-enabled operating models.** Marketing organisations are focusing on workflow redesign, brand integrity, AI-shaped discoverability, and the changing value of human creative judgment. ([marketing.org.nz](https://marketing.org.nz/advanced-ai/traininghub?utm_source=openai))
- **New Zealand’s creative-tech capability is being positioned as a national research opportunity.** The proposed Aotearoa Creative AI Research Institute, led by Wētā FX, remains a significant capability signal, although MBIE’s public material describes it as a proposal rather than a confirmed national institute. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/concepts-selected-for-ai-research-platform?utm_source=openai))

## What Changed Since July 13, 2026

### 1. New research has strengthened the evidence on workforce impacts

Massey University’s pilot study, presented in May and reported during the current update period, examined generative AI use across film, music, design, digital media, communications, and related fields. The research involved interviews with 10 creative practitioners in Aotearoa.

The study found:

- Practitioners had widely varying positions, from active resistance to enthusiastic daily use.
- AI was being used for tasks such as research, ideation, drafting, image generation, communication, and production support.
- Participants were concerned about the displacement of paid creative roles.
- Interviewees could not yet identify clear, widespread examples of AI directly eliminating specific jobs.
- The greatest immediate concern was the possible erosion of **entry-level and gateway roles**, where emerging practitioners traditionally gain experience. ([massey.ac.nz](https://www.massey.ac.nz/about/news/pilot-study-shows-generative-ai-use-in-creative-industries-impacts-entry-level-roles/?utm_source=openai))

This adds an important qualification to the adoption narrative: productivity gains may not automatically translate into stronger local employment or career pathways.

### 2. Research attention has moved from “AI tools” to the organisation of creativity

AUT’s “Life Cycle of an Idea” project is examining how AI changes the formation, development, circulation, and ownership of creative ideas. Its research questions focus on authorship, decision-making, collaboration, creative agency, and the potential standardisation of aesthetics. ([vcdrc.aut.ac.nz](https://vcdrc.aut.ac.nz/our-research/the-life-cycle-of-an-idea-creative-practice-in-transition%2C-exploring-design%2C-ai-and-the-reorganisation-of-creativity?utm_source=openai))

This represents a shift in the research agenda. The key question is no longer simply whether creatives use AI, but how AI changes:

- who originates ideas;
- who makes creative decisions;
- how creative work is evaluated;
- how credit and authorship are assigned;
- which kinds of work remain economically viable; and
- how emerging practitioners enter the industry.

### 3. Documentary and factual storytelling have become a more visible testing ground

AUT researchers and Doc Edge Industry 2026 placed AI and documentary filmmaking at the centre of sector discussion. The focus was not limited to production efficiency. It included authenticity, truth, voice replication, post-production, misinformation, copyright, privacy, and precarious work. ([aut.ac.nz](https://www.aut.ac.nz/news/stories/ai-an-existential-threat-to-documentary?utm_source=openai))

Documentary is particularly sensitive because its cultural value depends on an audience’s belief that people, events, voices, and evidence have been represented truthfully. AI therefore creates a sharper distinction between:

- **technical enhancement**, such as noise reduction or transcription;
- **editorial assistance**, such as thematic analysis or script development; and
- **synthetic reconstruction**, such as generated voices, images, scenes, or events.

The sector’s likely direction is controlled augmentation, with disclosure and human editorial responsibility becoming increasingly important.

### 4. Creative-sector AI activity is becoming more coordinated

The AI Forum’s 2026 Blueprint identifies the AI and Creative Industries Summit held on May 5–6 and the nationwide AI Hackathon Festival held from August 3–10 as key ecosystem events. The Blueprint places particular emphasis on social licence, trust, authenticity, education, and data sovereignty. ([aiforum.org.nz](https://aiforum.org.nz/2026/05/06/ai-blueprint-for-aotearoa-a-refreshed-vision-to-2030/?utm_source=openai))

The significance is institutional rather than purely technical. AI in the creative industries is becoming a recurring national policy and industry-development topic involving:

- creative practitioners;
- technology companies;
- universities;
- public funders;
- cultural institutions;
- media organisations; and
- Māori and community representatives.

# Current State by Subsector

## 1. Journalism, Publishing and Audio

Journalism remains one of the clearest examples of practical AI adoption in New Zealand creative work.

Publicly documented use cases include:

- scanning council documents and public records;
- generating story leads;
- rewriting financial announcements;
- homepage and content recommendation;
- drafting and voicing podcasts;
- transcription and summarisation;
- translation and accessibility support; and
- production analytics.

The earlier JMAD research identified several New Zealand examples, including Stuff’s Democracy.AI, NZME’s Bidi rewriting system, NZ Herald’s Polaris homepage curation, and BusinessDesk’s AI-assisted podcast production. These examples show that local news organisations are using AI primarily to increase newsroom capacity and accelerate routine production rather than remove editorial responsibility. ([jmadresearch.com](https://www.jmadresearch.com/_files/ugd/a95e86_b1937c0c5f82498088e43cbb5552215d.pdf?utm_source=openai))

### Current operating pattern

The sector is separating into two broad models:

**Commercial media**

- More willing to experiment with automation and personalisation.
- Strong focus on production speed, audience growth, and revenue.
- Greater willingness to use AI in content adaptation and distribution.

**Public-service media**

- More cautious about synthetic public-facing output.
- Stronger emphasis on editorial accountability, accuracy, transparency, and audience trust.
- More likely to permit AI for internal assistance than for autonomous publication.

### Strategic assessment

News organisations are likely to continue adopting AI in back-office and workflow functions. However, audience resistance to heavily automated journalism creates a practical limit on fully synthetic news production.

The competitive advantage will increasingly come from combining:

- trusted human journalism;
- AI-supported research and production;
- transparent disclosure;
- strong source verification; and
- distinctive local and culturally grounded reporting.

---

## 2. Screen, Film, Television and Documentary

Screen is New Zealand’s most developed sector in terms of formal AI governance.

NZFC’s AI principles place emphasis on:

- human talent and creativity;
- protection of likeness, persona, personal information, and intellectual property;
- respect for Te Tiriti o Waitangi;
- protection of Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property;
- transparency;
- consent and fair remuneration;
- risk assessment;
- accountability; and
- ongoing oversight. ([nzfilm.co.nz](https://www.nzfilm.co.nz/about-us/ai-strategy?utm_source=openai))

NZFC’s June 2026 production funding guidelines now require applicants to outline proposed AI use. Applications are assessed against cultural authenticity, creative integrity, transparency, ethical implications, intellectual property ownership, benefit sharing, and capacity-building. ([nzfilm.co.nz](https://www.nzfilm.co.nz/assets/resources/Final-Published-HPTRM-Version-June-2026.pdf?utm_source=openai))

### Main use cases

Public evidence points to AI use or experimentation in:

- script and screenplay analysis;
- story development;
- concept art and pre-visualisation;
- visual effects;
- image restoration;
- editing and post-production;
- asset management;
- audience and market analysis;
- production administration; and
- virtual production.

Wētā FX has publicly demonstrated the use of machine learning in visual-effects tools, while the proposed Aotearoa Creative AI Research Institute would focus on computer vision, imaging, complex data models, digital twins, and AI rights management. ([technewzealand.org.nz](https://technewzealand.org.nz/reports/navigating-our-creative-future/?utm_source=openai))

### Documentary and authenticity

Documentary presents a distinct governance challenge. AI can assist with research, editing, audio repair, and thematic analysis, but the use of synthetic voices, reconstructed scenes, or generated evidence risks undermining the genre’s relationship with truth and lived experience. ([aut.ac.nz](https://www.aut.ac.nz/news/stories/ai-an-existential-threat-to-documentary?utm_source=openai))

### Strategic assessment

New Zealand screen is not moving toward unrestricted synthetic production. Its emerging model is:

> **AI may be used where it improves production, but human authorship, cultural legitimacy, consent, and accountability must remain visible.**

The most important development is the integration of these expectations into funding decisions rather than leaving them as voluntary principles.

## 3. Advertising, Marketing and Commercial Creative

Advertising and marketing remain among the fastest-moving areas of AI adoption.

IAB New Zealand describes the market as separating into two tiers:

1. **High-volume, lower-stakes work**, including social content, performance creative, and routine variations.
2. **Brand, broadcast, and high-craft work**, where senior human creative judgment remains central. ([iab.org.nz](https://www.iab.org.nz/news-resources/the-future-of-creativity-ai-event-wrap?utm_source=openai))

Marketing Association activity in August 2026 shows that the industry is now addressing AI as an operating-model issue. Topics include:

- how marketing roles are being redefined;
- how teams should work with AI;
- how to manage tool sprawl;
- how to preserve brand integrity;
- how to maintain strategic oversight; and
- how to integrate AI across planning, content, analytics, and reporting. ([marketing.org.nz](https://marketing.org.nz/advanced-ai/traininghub?utm_source=openai))

### Emerging commercial pattern

The most significant shift is from AI as a standalone content-generation tool to AI as a layer across the marketing system.

This includes:

- campaign planning;
- creative development;
- rapid content variation;
- customer segmentation;
- synthetic audience modelling;
- media optimisation;
- performance reporting;
- search and recommendation visibility; and
- automated customer journeys.

IAB New Zealand has also highlighted the emergence of synthetic audience panels, in which AI-generated personas are built from existing data sources to simulate consumer responses. This may accelerate early-stage testing, but it does not eliminate the need for real audience research, cultural insight, or human interpretation. ([iab.org.nz](https://www.iab.org.nz/news-resources/the-future-of-creativity-ai-event-wrap?utm_source=openai))

### Strategic assessment

Commercial creative will likely see the fastest growth in AI-assisted production. However, the value of human creativity is shifting rather than disappearing.

Human value is concentrating around:

- distinctive ideas;
- cultural interpretation;
- brand judgment;
- taste and quality control;
- narrative coherence;
- strategic positioning;
- ethical decision-making; and
- accountability for public outcomes.

## 4. Music

Music remains the subsector where adoption, rights, consent, and economic risk are most tightly connected.

The regional APRA AMCOS research cited in the previous update found that:

- 54% of surveyed creators believed AI could assist the human creative process;
- 82% were concerned AI could threaten their ability to earn a living; and
- without an effective licensing framework, a significant share of music creator revenue could be exposed by 2028. ([mch.govt.nz](https://www.mch.govt.nz/news/new-zealand-creatives-embrace-digital-tools?utm_source=openai))

The central issues remain:

- unauthorised training on recorded music;
- imitation of an artist’s voice or style;
- synthetic performers;
- ownership of AI-generated compositions;
- consent and remuneration;
- attribution and credit;
- Māori cultural expression; and
- the bargaining position of independent creators.

The Government has asked for advice on a possible copyright framework for generative AI by **March 31, 2027**. This does not yet establish a licensing system, but it places generative-AI copyright within the formal government policy programme. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/creative-and-cultural-sector-gets-further-copyright-support?utm_source=openai))

### Strategic assessment

Music adoption is likely to remain uneven:

- creators may use AI privately for experimentation, arrangement, editing, mastering, and ideation;
- commercial release of synthetic or AI-derived music will remain more sensitive;
- rights organisations will continue to focus on consent, training-data transparency, attribution, and compensation; and
- Māori creators will require safeguards against the unauthorised extraction or imitation of culturally significant musical material.

---

## 5. Games, Animation, VFX and Creative Technology

Games, animation, VFX, virtual production, and immersive media represent New Zealand’s strongest long-term AI capability frontier.

The sector benefits from:

- technically sophisticated production pipelines;
- export-oriented business models;
- demand for productivity improvements;
- reusable digital assets;
- simulation and procedural generation;
- large-scale testing requirements; and
- a comparatively strong connection between creative and technology skills.

The proposed Aotearoa Creative AI Research Institute is particularly significant because it links creative-sector needs with national research priorities in computer vision, digital twins, imaging, and AI rights management. However, the public MBIE announcement describes the institute as one of several concepts selected for further development; it does not confirm that it has become the sole funded platform. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/concepts-selected-for-ai-research-platform?utm_source=openai))

AUT’s Portals/Traces project is another capability signal. It explores virtual production, extended reality, AI, photogrammetry, motion capture, interoperable workflows, and ethically governed data exchange across international partners. ([vcdrc.aut.ac.nz](https://vcdrc.aut.ac.nz/our-research/portalstraces?utm_source=openai))

### Likely applications

- character and environment development;
- animation assistance;
- procedural world-building;
- motion capture cleanup;
- visual-effects compositing;
- asset search and management;
- game testing;
- simulation;
- localisation;
- accessibility features;
- player and audience modelling; and
- virtual production.

### Strategic assessment

AI may improve the competitiveness of New Zealand’s creative-technology companies, but the benefits will depend on whether local firms retain:

- intellectual property;
- specialist talent;
- access to compute and data;
- commercial ownership of tools and workflows; and
- the ability to export high-value services rather than only supply labour to offshore platforms.

## 6. Podcasting and Audio Production

AUT research provides one of the most detailed New Zealand practice-led case studies.

In producing a five-episode documentary podcast, researcher Lewis Tennant tested AI across pre-production, production, and post-production. The study found that AI was useful for:

- exploratory research;
- generating initial leads;
- supporting script development;
- audio enhancement;
- editing assistance; and
- workflow experimentation.

However, the research also found that AI outputs were inconsistent and required substantial human checking. AI-generated research could not be relied upon as a primary source, while audio enhancement sometimes reduced the depth and richness of professional recordings. ([doi.org](https://doi.org/10.24135/ijcmr.v13iapril.167?utm_source=openai))

### Key finding

The case supports a broader sector pattern:

> **AI performs best when it assists specialist knowledge, but performs poorly when asked to replace editorial, factual, or aesthetic judgment.**

## 7. Arts Organisations, Funders and Cultural Institutions

AI is increasingly being incorporated into the public funding architecture of the creative sector.

Creative New Zealand advises applicants to:

- be transparent about AI use;
- lead with their own creative voice;
- check factual and ethical integrity;
- respect intellectual property;
- protect privacy; and
- take particular care with cultural integrity in Ngā Toi Māori and Pacific arts. ([mch.govt.nz](https://www.mch.govt.nz/news/new-zealand-creatives-embrace-digital-tools?utm_source=openai))

NZ On Air’s AI guidance applies to funded screen and music content. It treats AI guidance as a living document because the technology and its risks are changing rapidly. ([nzonair.govt.nz](https://www.nzonair.govt.nz/news/shorts-newsletter-16-june-2025/?utm_source=openai))

NZFC has gone further by linking AI disclosure to the assessment of funding applications. ([nzfilm.co.nz](https://www.nzfilm.co.nz/assets/resources/Final-Published-HPTRM-Version-June-2026.pdf?utm_source=openai))

### Institutional implications

Funders and cultural institutions are likely to become:

- purchasers of AI-enabled administrative tools;
- gatekeepers of disclosure expectations;
- arbiters of cultural and ethical risk;
- supporters of capability-building; and
- potential custodians of culturally sensitive data.

This makes public funding bodies important actors in defining acceptable AI practice across the sector.

# Research Overview

## National adoption evidence

Manatū Taonga’s survey provides the broadest current estimate of creative-sector adoption. It shows that generative AI is not confined to technology companies or large studios; it is already used by a substantial proportion of creators who use digital tools. ([mch.govt.nz](https://www.mch.govt.nz/news/new-zealand-creatives-embrace-digital-tools?utm_source=openai))

The main reported benefits are:

- improved efficiency;
- idea generation;
- assistance with creative development; and
- wider distribution.

A major constraint is capability. More than one-third of creators who do not use digital tools reported a lack of technological skills or knowledge as a barrier. ([mch.govt.nz](https://www.mch.govt.nz/news/new-zealand-creatives-embrace-digital-tools?utm_source=openai))

## Massey University pilot study

Massey’s research is important because it focuses on current practitioner experience in Aotearoa rather than relying primarily on overseas projections.

Its main findings indicate:

- adoption and resistance coexist;
- use is highly uneven between disciplines and organisations;
- practitioners remain uncertain about copyright and ownership;
- productivity gains may be captured outside New Zealand;
- entry-level roles may be particularly exposed; and
- direct job displacement is not yet clearly documented across the sample. ([massey.ac.nz](https://www.massey.ac.nz/about/news/pilot-study-shows-generative-ai-use-in-creative-industries-impacts-entry-level-roles/?utm_source=openai))

The sample is small, so it should not be treated as a sector-wide employment estimate. Its value lies in identifying issues requiring larger-scale research.

## AUT podcasting research

The podcasting study shows that AI can reduce friction in production but does not remove the need for human expertise. Its findings are consistent with the wider New Zealand pattern of cautious, workflow-focused adoption. ([doi.org](https://doi.org/10.24135/ijcmr.v13iapril.167?utm_source=openai))

## Research gaps

Despite improving evidence, important gaps remain:

- no comprehensive sector-wide measurement of AI adoption by creative subsector;
- limited data on small studios, freelancers, and independent creators;
- limited measurement of productivity outcomes;
- limited evidence on revenue and employment effects;
- little public information on AI use in New Zealand games studios;
- limited analysis of Māori data sovereignty and AI use in practice;
- limited information on the energy and infrastructure costs of creative AI; and
- limited evidence on whether AI increases or reduces international competitiveness.

# Policy, Rights and Trust Environment

## Copyright

The Government’s June 3, 2026 announcement confirms that a possible generative-AI copyright framework is under consideration, with a report-back deadline of March 31, 2027. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/creative-and-cultural-sector-gets-further-copyright-support?utm_source=openai))

The unresolved questions include:

- whether creators should be able to opt out of training;
- whether licensing should be voluntary, collective, or mandatory;
- how training data should be disclosed;
- how AI-generated outputs should be protected;
- how style imitation should be treated;
- how liability should be allocated; and
- how New Zealand law should interact with overseas platforms and datasets.

## Cultural and Indigenous rights

New Zealand’s national AI strategy recognises both the opportunities and risks associated with Māori data and cultural intellectual property. It identifies concerns including:

- unauthorised use of mātauranga Māori in training data;
- cultural misappropriation;
- loss of data sovereignty;
- bias and misrepresentation; and
- commercial exploitation without consent or benefit sharing. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/sites/default/files/2025-07/New%20Zealand%27s%20AI%20Strategy%20-%20Investing%20with%20confidence.pdf?utm_source=openai))

For creative industries, these are not abstract compliance issues. They affect:

- story development;
- design;
- music;
- imagery;
- language models;
- archives;
- digital characters;
- cultural symbols; and
- the use of community knowledge.

## Trust and authenticity

Trust is becoming a form of market infrastructure.

Creative organisations must increasingly be able to explain:

- whether AI was used;
- what role it played;
- whether human review occurred;
- whether rights were cleared;
- whose likeness or data was involved;
- whether the content has been altered; and
- who is accountable for the final result.

The pressure for transparency is particularly strong in journalism, documentary, public broadcasting, cultural heritage, and Māori and Pacific creative work.

# Key Trends

## 1. Adoption is broad, but not uniform

The national baseline indicates substantial uptake, but the experience of a large production company, advertising agency, independent musician, artist, or community arts organisation can be very different.

The largest barriers remain:

- skills;
- cost;
- access to suitable tools;
- uncertainty over rights;
- privacy and confidentiality;
- cultural safety; and
- lack of clear return on investment.

## 2. Workflow AI is ahead of synthetic-output AI

The most visible applications remain:

- summarisation;
- transcription;
- research assistance;
- editing;
- audio cleanup;
- document analysis;
- content adaptation;
- recommendation;
- marketing optimisation; and
- administrative automation.

Fully synthetic public-facing creative work exists, but it faces stronger resistance where audiences expect authenticity, human testimony, or cultural accountability.

## 3. Entry-level work is the critical labour-market fault line

If AI automates routine research, drafting, asset preparation, editing, and production support, the first impact may be on the roles through which new entrants develop professional experience.

This creates a strategic risk:

- experienced creatives may become more productive;
- junior roles may shrink;
- fewer people may acquire practical experience;
- the future senior talent pipeline may weaken.

The sector will need deliberate apprenticeships, supervised AI-enabled work, and new pathways into creative careers.

## 4. Creative judgment is becoming more valuable

As content generation becomes cheaper, differentiation depends more heavily on:

- originality;
- taste;
- cultural understanding;
- narrative judgment;
- editing;
- audience insight;
- ethical reasoning; and
- the ability to make meaningful choices among many machine-generated options.

## 5. Discoverability is becoming part of creative strategy

AI systems are increasingly involved in how audiences find, interpret, and select content, products, and brands.

This means creative organisations must consider not only how to make content, but also:

- how their work is represented in AI-generated answers;
- whether their content is discoverable through machine systems;
- how provenance is communicated;
- how brand meaning survives automated summarisation; and
- how local and culturally specific work avoids being flattened into generic categories.

## 6. New Zealand’s opportunity is concentrated in specialist capability

New Zealand is unlikely to compete with the largest countries on foundation-model scale. Its stronger opportunities lie in:

- visual effects;
- animation;
- virtual production;
- games;
- immersive media;
- creative software;
- rights management;
- culturally grounded datasets;
- Māori and Pacific creative technology; and
- high-value export services.

## 7. Governance is becoming part of competitiveness

The most credible New Zealand creative organisations will increasingly be those able to demonstrate:

- consent;
- provenance;
- secure data handling;
- cultural consultation;
- human accountability;
- transparent disclosure;
- fair remuneration; and
- reliable quality control.

Responsible adoption is therefore becoming more than a compliance obligation. It is a potential market differentiator.

# Strategic Implications for Industry Stakeholders

## For creative businesses

- Establish an internal register of approved AI tools.
- Do not upload confidential client, performer, script, or cultural material without clear authorisation.
- Record where AI has materially contributed to a work.
- Build human review into every public-facing workflow.
- Treat AI literacy as a core creative capability.
- Protect time for original ideation, editing, and cultural interpretation.

## For freelancers and independent creators

- Use AI to reduce routine workload, not to surrender control of authorship.
- Maintain evidence of your own creative contribution.
- Review licensing terms before using AI-generated assets commercially.
- Be cautious with voice, likeness, personal data, and culturally sensitive material.
- Negotiate AI use explicitly in contracts.

## For funders and public institutions

- Continue moving from broad principles to practical disclosure requirements.
- Support training for smaller organisations and independent practitioners.
- Fund research into entry-level pathways and employment effects.
- Develop culturally appropriate guidance for Māori and Pacific creators.
- Measure outcomes, not only adoption.

## For educators and training providers

- Teach AI alongside research, craft, ethics, copyright, and cultural competency.
- Preserve hands-on pathways into production roles.
- Train students to evaluate and edit AI outputs rather than merely generate them.
- Develop interdisciplinary programmes linking creative practice, technology, law, and business.

## For policymakers

- Resolve copyright and training-data settings with meaningful creator participation.
- Support local ownership of high-value intellectual property.
- Invest in creative-tech research and infrastructure.
- Monitor the distribution of AI productivity gains.
- Treat cultural and Indigenous data sovereignty as a central policy issue.

# Overall Assessment

As of **August 18, 2026**, AI adoption in Aotearoa New Zealand’s creative industries is **broad, practical, and increasingly institutionalised**.

The evidence does not support a simple narrative of either rapid creative-sector automation or widespread rejection. Instead, New Zealand is developing a distinctive model characterised by:

- widespread experimentation;
- workflow-first adoption;
- strong human oversight;
- cautious public-facing deployment;
- growing concern about entry-level employment;
- formal attention to creator rights;
- explicit protection of Māori cultural and intellectual property; and
- increasing investment in creative-technology capability.

The most important change since the July 13 update is the strengthening of the **research and capability agenda**. Massey’s pilot study has highlighted the risks to gateway roles. AUT research has demonstrated the practical limits of AI in documentary podcasting. Creative-sector summits and research programmes are examining authorship, trust, sustainability, data sovereignty, and the reorganisation of creative work. ([massey.ac.nz](https://www.massey.ac.nz/documents/2356/Generative_AI_in_the_creative_sector.pdf?utm_source=openai))

The next phase will be determined less by whether creative practitioners can access AI tools and more by whether New Zealand can build the institutions, contracts, skills, and cultural safeguards needed to ensure that value remains with creators and communities.

> **New Zealand’s creative industries are moving toward governed augmentation: using AI to expand creative capacity while retaining human authorship, cultural legitimacy, and accountability.**
