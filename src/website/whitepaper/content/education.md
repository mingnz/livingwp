# AI in Education in New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper  
*Updated for publication on 18 August 2026*

## Executive Snapshot

AI in New Zealand education is now best understood as **operationally mainstream, governance-heavy, and increasingly tied to qualification and curriculum redesign rather than isolated classroom experimentation**. The strongest national usage signal remains TALIS 2024: **69% of New Zealand Year 7–10 teachers** reported using AI in the prior year, compared with **36% across the OECD lower-secondary average**, with the most common uses still lesson planning and summarisation rather than marking. ([educationcounts.govt.nz](https://www.educationcounts.govt.nz/publications/series/teaching-and-learning-international-survey/talis-2024-publications/talis-2024-first-report?utm_source=openai))

Since the previous update on **13 July 2026**, the biggest shift has been in the **system architecture around assessment and curriculum**. The Ministry and NZQA have made AI more visible inside the NCEA support environment, consultation on replacing NCEA is now open, and the Government has published more detail on the subject-based qualifications that will progressively replace NCEA from **2028 to 2030**. ([ncea.education.govt.nz](https://ncea.education.govt.nz/generative-ai-ncea-assessment?utm_source=openai))

A second important development is that AI is becoming more visible not only as a tool used in education, but also as **curriculum content**. On **5 August 2026**, the Ministry announced nine new industry-led senior secondary subjects, including **Applied Intelligent Systems**. Related senior subject descriptors on Tāhūrangi also explicitly reference **artificial intelligence and intelligent systems** in computing and technology pathways. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/news/new-industry-led-subjects-announced-senior-secondary-students))

The overall pattern remains consistent: **AI is being framed as augmentation, not replacement**. In schools, official guidance keeps teacher judgment central and restricts GenAI in high-stakes assessment contexts. In tertiary education, leading universities are moving toward institution-wide assessment redesign, bounded AI environments, and formal governance models that explicitly include ethics, equity, privacy, Te Tiriti, and data sovereignty. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/education-professionals/schools-year-0-13/digital-technology/generative-ai))

## What Has Changed Since 13 July 2026

- **NCEA replacement consultation is now live.** The Ministry’s consultation on replacing NCEA opened on **4 August 2026** and runs until **15 September 2026**, with proposals centred on a more structured, subject-based qualification system and a stronger Year 11 literacy and numeracy foundation. ([ncea.education.govt.nz](https://ncea.education.govt.nz/whats-new/consultation-proposal-replace-ncea))
- **Further qualification details were published on 18 August 2026.** The Government confirmed a staged rollout: a **Foundational Award** from **2028**, **NZCE** for Year 12 from **2029**, and **NZACE** for Year 13 from **2030**. ([ncea.education.govt.nz](https://ncea.education.govt.nz/whats-new/further-details-new-senior-secondary-qualifications))
- **AI guidance is now more embedded inside NCEA operations.** The NCEA site’s dedicated **“Generative AI in NCEA assessment”** page was published on **30 July 2026**, and the NCEA support site now explicitly states that Ministry and NZQA are working with schools on authenticity and academic integrity in response to generative AI concerns. ([ncea.education.govt.nz](https://ncea.education.govt.nz/generative-ai-ncea-assessment?utm_source=openai))
- **AI expectations are appearing in subject-level assessment materials.** Recent NCEA subject pages for areas such as English, Mathematics and Statistics, Science, History, and Digital Technologies explicitly reference evidence produced with GenAI and the need for school-level guidance on acceptable use. ([ncea.education.govt.nz](https://ncea.education.govt.nz/english/english?utm_source=openai))
- **AI has become more visible in senior curriculum design.** On **5 August 2026**, the Ministry announced nine new industry-led subjects, including **Applied Intelligent Systems**, signalling that AI is now part of the future subject architecture, not just a classroom management issue. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/news/new-industry-led-subjects-announced-senior-secondary-students))
- **Tertiary capability-building has deepened.** UC Online published a **Postgraduate Certificate in Artificial Intelligence and Digital Education** with a **September 2026** study start and course content focused on AI literacy, assessment redesign, ethics, equity, and cultural responsibility. ([uconline.ac.nz](https://uconline.ac.nz/courses/postgraduate-certificate-in-ai-and-digital-education))

## Overall State of Adoption

### Schools and kura: use is mainstream, but capability is uneven

TALIS 2024 still gives the clearest system-wide picture. Among New Zealand teachers who had used AI, **78%** used it to generate lesson plans and activities and **73%** used it to learn about or summarise a topic, while only **12%** used it to assess or mark student work. Teachers also continue to report substantial professional learning demand around AI. ([educationcounts.govt.nz](https://www.educationcounts.govt.nz/publications/series/teaching-and-learning-international-survey/talis-2024-publications/talis-2024-first-report?utm_source=openai))

NZCER’s primary-school research reinforces that pattern. Its 2025 report found real uptake by both teachers and students, but also uneven access, uncertain policy environments, and strong demand for training. It reports that **85%** of surveyed teachers wanted more training, and that the study pointed to gaps in policy, resourcing, and professional learning. ([nzcer.org.nz](https://www.nzcer.org.nz/research/publications/generative-artificial-intelligence-aotearoa-new-zealand-primary-schools?language=mi&utm_source=openai))

The Ministry’s current guidance remains explicitly teacher-centred. It says AI in education should improve teaching and learning by **supporting knowledgeable and skilled teachers**, requires schools with consent to assess to have policies covering acceptable GenAI use, and states that **GenAI is not permitted in NCEA external assessment**. It also reiterates that AI marking tools should support, not replace, teacher professional judgment. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/education-professionals/schools-year-0-13/digital-technology/generative-ai))

### Assessment and qualifications: AI is now part of system design

Assessment remains the main governance battleground. Budget 2026 allocated **$2.1 million operating funding** to NZQA for pilots and proofs of concept in **auto text scoring, AI, and machine learning** to support **marking, moderation, and exam development**. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/our-work/publications/corporate-documents/budget-2026/investing-secondary-achievement))

NZQA remains the most mature production user in the sector. It says that from **May 2025** it has used a combination of **automated machine marking, automated text scoring, and human marking** for literacy and numeracy co-requisite assessments. NZQA also reports that more than **55,000** writing assessments were marked with automated text scoring in 2025 and results were returned **3.5 weeks earlier** than the previous year, with human check-marking still used for a substantial share of scripts near the achievement boundary. ([www2.nzqa.govt.nz](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/ncea/subjects/litnum/marking/?utm_source=openai))

What has changed since mid-July is that AI is no longer just a side issue within NCEA. It is now being woven into the public-facing support stack: the dedicated NCEA GenAI page is live, the support site explicitly references AI-related authenticity concerns, and subject pages increasingly bake acceptable-use language into assessment conditions. That indicates a shift from generic caution to **subject-level operationalisation**. ([ncea.education.govt.nz](https://ncea.education.govt.nz/generative-ai-ncea-assessment?utm_source=openai))

The qualification redesign now intensifies that trend. The move toward **subject-based qualifications**, whole-subject assessment, and a staged replacement of NCEA suggests that New Zealand’s answer to AI pressure is becoming less about policing tools and more about **rebuilding assessment structures around assurance, comparability, and clearer evidence of learning**. This is an inference from the qualification design and AI guidance, but it is strongly supported by current Ministry and NCEA materials. ([ncea.education.govt.nz](https://ncea.education.govt.nz/whats-new/further-details-new-senior-secondary-qualifications))

### Tertiary education: operating models are maturing

The University of Auckland remains one of the clearest examples of institutional codification. Its **Two-Lane Approach** requires all courses and programmes to implement the model by **2027**, distinguishes between **controlled** assessments where AI is restricted by default and **uncontrolled** assessments where AI may be used, and explicitly rejects reliance on third-party AI detectors because of reliability, equity, and data concerns. ([teachwell.auckland.ac.nz](https://teachwell.auckland.ac.nz/assessment/two-lane-approach-to-assessment/?utm_source=openai))

Auckland is also moving from policy to tooling. Its **Cogniti** platform is a Canvas-integrated, course-specific AI environment available for credit-bearing courses, with documented use cases including **24/7 Socratic tutoring** and a large first-year science deployment supporting **more than 1,600 students**. ([teachwell.auckland.ac.nz](https://teachwell.auckland.ac.nz/learning-technologies/cogniti/?utm_source=openai))

AUT has become more publicly explicit about a whole-of-institution response. Its two-channel assessment model distinguishes between invigilated non-AI summative settings and AI-permitted settings, while its **POOL** framework is designed to make learning visible across assessment development. AUT’s published AI principles emphasise equity, human-centred deployment, academic integrity, Māori and Indigenous data protection, and the continuing importance of human-to-human connection. ([aut.ac.nz](https://www.aut.ac.nz/about/teaching-learning-and-assessment/generative-ai-and-assessment-at-aut))

Otago’s maturity shows up most clearly in governance. Its **AI Governance Policy**, effective **10 March 2026**, applies across development, deployment, procurement, and use of AI systems, and requires governance aligned to Te Tiriti obligations, Māori data sovereignty, Pacific values, privacy, security, and human oversight. Its teaching guidance complements that with a deliberately balanced, division-sensitive approach to classroom integration. ([otago.ac.nz](https://www.otago.ac.nz/about/administration/policies/policy-collection/ai-governance-policy))

The University of Canterbury is helping push AI literacy into a more formal educational field. Its **SAIL** framework positions AI literacy as critical judgment, practical tool use, and AI citizenship, and UC Online is now offering a postgraduate pathway specifically in **Artificial Intelligence and Digital Education**. ([canterbury.ac.nz](https://www.canterbury.ac.nz/news-and-events/news/2026/new-research-helps-rethink-ai-literacy-in-education-))

## Policy, Governance, and Trust

The Ministry’s current school guidance is now fairly operational. Schools must have acceptable-use policies for GenAI in assessment, AI use for marking must remain under teacher control, and schools are warned not to send personal information about anyone under **13** into AI tools. The page also reiterates age restrictions attached to common tools such as ChatGPT. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/education-professionals/schools-year-0-13/digital-technology/generative-ai))

Privacy is becoming a harder constraint, not a softer one. The Office of the Privacy Commissioner’s **2026 annual survey**, published on **11 May 2026**, found **71%** of New Zealanders were concerned about **children’s privacy** and **67%** were concerned about organisations using AI to make decisions about people using personal data. ([privacy.org.nz](https://www.privacy.org.nz/tuhono-connect/surveys/2026-annual-survey-on-privacy/?utm_source=openai))

The Commissioner’s education-sector children’s privacy guidance makes the implications for schools concrete. It warns about vendors using learner data to train GenAI models, third-party sharing, profiling, biased inferences, inappropriate chatbot interactions, and over-collection of personal information. It also stresses that AI use in education must comply with the Privacy Act and be assessed against learners’ best interests. ([privacy.org.nz](https://www.privacy.org.nz/resources-and-learning/a-z-topics/protecting-children-and-young-peoples-privacy/childrens-privacy-guidance-for-the-education-sector/chapter-16-digital-technologies/?utm_source=openai))

In New Zealand’s context, that means **privacy, cultural legitimacy, and social licence are now core adoption conditions**. The AI Forum’s 2026 Blueprint reinforces this by positioning trust, AI literacy, transparency, authenticity, and data sovereignty as central national workstreams rather than peripheral concerns. ([aiforum.org.nz](https://aiforum.org.nz/reports/ai-blueprint-for-aotearoa-a-refreshed-vision-to-2030/?utm_source=openai))

## Research Overview

### The strongest evidence base still says “broad use, uneven readiness”

TALIS 2024 remains the best national benchmark for teacher AI uptake and suggests New Zealand educators are ahead of many peers in exposure and experimentation, but not yet in settled capability or assessment coherence. ([educationcounts.govt.nz](https://www.educationcounts.govt.nz/publications/series/teaching-and-learning-international-survey/talis-2024-publications/talis-2024-first-report?utm_source=openai))

NZCER’s primary-school report remains the most useful New Zealand classroom-level study. Its value is less about perfect representativeness and more about showing what early adoption actually looks like: frequent use among interested teachers, patchy funded access, unclear rules, and very strong demand for professional learning. ([nzcer.org.nz](https://www.nzcer.org.nz/research/publications/generative-artificial-intelligence-aotearoa-new-zealand-primary-schools?language=mi&utm_source=openai))

UC’s **SAIL** work is important because it broadens AI literacy beyond prompting and tool use. It explicitly includes critical judgment, social responsibility, and Māori and Pacific perspectives, making AI literacy a substantive educational capability rather than a tech add-on. ([canterbury.ac.nz](https://www.canterbury.ac.nz/news-and-events/news/2026/new-research-helps-rethink-ai-literacy-in-education-))

Ako Aotearoa’s **Empowering Aotearoa** report adds a tertiary inclusion lens, focusing on AI literacy for diverse ākonga and educators through the **InclusiveAI.nz** resource set. ([ako.ac.nz](https://ako.ac.nz/knowledge-centre/an-inclusive-approach-to-ai-literacy/report-empowering-aotearoa-an-inclusive-approach-to-ai-literacy-in-tertiary-education?utm_source=openai))

ERO is still studying the issue rather than closing it. As of August 2026, **“How are schools and students using AI?”** remains an active evidence question, which means national evaluation is still catching up with practice. ([evidence.ero.govt.nz](https://www.evidence.ero.govt.nz/current-work?utm_source=openai))

## Case Studies

### 1) NZQA: AI at national assessment scale

NZQA is still the clearest live case of AI at production scale in New Zealand education. Its co-requisite marking model combines automated scoring with human oversight, has already operated at national volume, and is now backed by new public funding for broader pilots in moderation and exam development. ([www2.nzqa.govt.nz](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/ncea/subjects/litnum/marking/?utm_source=openai))

### 2) Westlake Girls’ High School and Day of AI Aotearoa New Zealand

The Education Gazette’s April 2026 profile of **Day of AI** shows one of the strongest school-level AI literacy examples in the country. The pilot ran across **nine schools**, found that **92%** of students had already tried AI tools at baseline, and reported improved learner understanding, ethical awareness, and attention to Māori and Pacific perspectives after participation. It also functioned as professional learning for teachers. ([gazette.education.govt.nz](https://gazette.education.govt.nz/articles/the-day-of-ai-a-teachers-drive-for-responsible-ai-literacy-in-new-zealand-schools/))

### 3) University of Auckland: assessment redesign plus bounded AI tooling

Auckland’s combination of the **Two-Lane Approach** and **Cogniti** matters because it links policy to delivery architecture. The university is not just stating principles; it is redesigning assessment at programme level and giving staff a controlled AI environment embedded in Canvas. ([teachwell.auckland.ac.nz](https://teachwell.auckland.ac.nz/assessment/two-lane-approach-to-assessment/?utm_source=openai))

### 4) AUT: whole-of-institution assurance of learning

AUT’s case is notable for its systemic framing. The two-channel approach, POOL model, and public AI principles position AI not as an academic-misconduct anomaly but as a university-wide redesign challenge spanning pedagogy, assessment, ethics, equity, and graduate capability. ([aut.ac.nz](https://www.aut.ac.nz/about/teaching-learning-and-assessment/generative-ai-and-assessment-at-aut))

### 5) University of Canterbury: AI literacy as a formal capability pathway

UC’s SAIL framework and new postgraduate certificate show how AI literacy is moving from workshop culture into structured educational design and credentialed learning. That is significant because it builds educator capability, not just student tool familiarity. ([canterbury.ac.nz](https://www.canterbury.ac.nz/news-and-events/news/2026/new-research-helps-rethink-ai-literacy-in-education-))

## Emerging Trends

### 1) Qualification reform is becoming the main AI story
The centre of gravity has moved upward: AI in education is increasingly being shaped by qualification design, curriculum sequencing, and national assessment architecture, not just local tool experimentation. ([ncea.education.govt.nz](https://ncea.education.govt.nz/whats-new/consultation-proposal-replace-ncea))

### 2) Assessment redesign is hardening into doctrine
Across Ministry guidance, NZQA support materials, Auckland’s Two-Lane model, and AUT’s two-channel model, the dominant response is to redesign assessment around authenticity and observation of learning rather than rely on detectors or blanket bans. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/education-professionals/schools-year-0-13/digital-technology/generative-ai))

### 3) AI literacy is becoming both a curriculum and equity issue
The emergence of **Applied Intelligent Systems**, AI references in senior technology/computing descriptors, and school-level programmes like Day of AI suggest that AI literacy is becoming part of what students are expected to learn, not merely something adults manage in the background. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/news/new-industry-led-subjects-announced-senior-secondary-students))

### 4) Tertiary institutions are moving toward bounded, governed deployment
The most mature universities are converging on a common model: whole-of-programme assessment logic, controlled AI-enabled environments, explicit governance, and strong emphasis on staff capability. ([teachwell.auckland.ac.nz](https://teachwell.auckland.ac.nz/assessment/two-lane-approach-to-assessment/?utm_source=openai))

### 5) Privacy and legitimacy remain scale constraints
In New Zealand, successful AI adoption in education is increasingly contingent on children’s privacy, responsible data practices, Māori data sovereignty, and social trust. These are not compliance side notes; they are implementation gates. ([privacy.org.nz](https://www.privacy.org.nz/resources-and-learning/a-z-topics/protecting-children-and-young-peoples-privacy/childrens-privacy-guidance-for-the-education-sector/chapter-16-digital-technologies/?utm_source=openai))

## Conclusion

As of **18 August 2026**, AI in education in New Zealand is best characterised as **widely used, increasingly systematised, and still unevenly implemented**. The most important movement since **13 July 2026** is not a sudden jump in classroom automation; it is the stronger integration of AI into the machinery of **assessment, qualification reform, and curriculum design**. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/consultation-ncea?utm_source=openai))

The sector’s direction is now clearer. New Zealand is not moving toward an “AI replaces educators” model. It is moving toward a model in which AI supports teaching, literacy, workflow, and selected assessment operations, while **human judgment, authenticity, privacy, and cultural legitimacy** become more explicit conditions of use. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/education-professionals/schools-year-0-13/digital-technology/generative-ai))

The defining challenge from here is **implementation quality at scale**. The organisations furthest ahead are no longer simply the earliest adopters of tools; they are the ones building coherent operating models across pedagogy, assessment, governance, staff capability, equity, and trust. ([educationcounts.govt.nz](https://www.educationcounts.govt.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0006/250791/TALIS-2024-Findings-for-New-Zealand-1.pdf?utm_source=openai))
