# AI in Education in New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper  
*Updated for publication on 17 August 2026*  
*Previous update: 13 July 2026*

## Executive Snapshot

AI adoption in New Zealand education has moved from early experimentation into **widespread everyday use**, particularly in schools. The latest evidence from the Education Review Office (ERO) indicates that more than **90% of school leaders**, over **80% of teachers**, and approximately **75% of students** use generative AI. However, implementation remains uneven: many schools lack formal policies, most teachers have not seen the Ministry’s existing guidance, and concerns about critical thinking and inappropriate student use are increasing. ([odt.co.nz](https://www.odt.co.nz/news/national/its-addictive-ai-damaging-critical-thinking-in-schools-report-finds-ujwe515m?utm_source=openai))

The major development since the previous update is the publication of ERO’s **Ready or Not: How are Schools Responding to Artificial Intelligence?** report in late July 2026. It provides the clearest national snapshot yet of AI adoption in New Zealand schools and calls for a nationally consistent framework covering student and teacher use, assessment, privacy, safety, and professional learning. ([odt.co.nz](https://www.odt.co.nz/news/national/its-addictive-ai-damaging-critical-thinking-in-schools-report-finds-ujwe515m?utm_source=openai))

At system level, AI adoption is also becoming more operational:

- NZQA is moving from its existing automated text-scoring deployment toward funded pilots in marking, moderation, and examination development.
- The Ministry’s SMART assessment platform is providing automated marking and reporting for reading, writing, and mathematics.
- Universities are formalising AI-permitted and AI-restricted assessment models.
- New tertiary programmes and professional courses are emerging specifically to build AI and digital-education capability.
- Governance remains centred on human oversight, authenticity, privacy, equity, and cultural legitimacy.

**Overall assessment:** New Zealand education is now in a phase of **high usage but incomplete institutional maturity**. The central challenge has shifted from whether AI is being used to whether it is being used deliberately, equitably, and pedagogically well.

## What Has Changed Since 13 July 2026

### 1. ERO published the first substantial national review of school AI use

ERO’s new report found that generative AI is already part of ordinary school life, but strategic implementation is limited. Its research included surveys of students, teachers, school leaders, and parents and whānau, together with interviews, focus groups, and visits to eight schools. ([odt.co.nz](https://www.odt.co.nz/news/national/its-addictive-ai-damaging-critical-thinking-in-schools-report-finds-ujwe515m?utm_source=openai))

Key findings include:

- More than **90% of principals and school leaders** use AI.
- More than **80% of teachers** use AI.
- Approximately **75% of students** report using AI.
- Around **40% of secondary students** reported using AI inappropriately for schoolwork.
- **14% of students** reported inappropriate AI use in assessments or tests; the proportion was higher in secondary schools.
- **57% of teachers** believed AI was having a negative effect on students’ critical thinking, rising to **72% among secondary teachers**.
- **73% of teachers** had not seen the Ministry’s high-level generative-AI guidance.
- Around three-quarters of schools were not taking a strategic approach to AI implementation. ([odt.co.nz](https://www.odt.co.nz/news/national/its-addictive-ai-damaging-critical-thinking-in-schools-report-finds-ujwe515m?utm_source=openai))

ERO recommended a national AI-in-education framework and greater consistency in school policies, professional learning, student expectations, assessment practice, privacy, and safety. It also recommended integrating AI literacy into learning rather than creating a standalone senior-secondary AI subject. ([odt.co.nz](https://www.odt.co.nz/news/national/its-addictive-ai-damaging-critical-thinking-in-schools-report-finds-ujwe515m?utm_source=openai))

### 2. The evidence base has shifted from “are schools using AI?” to “how well are they using it?”

Before the ERO review, the most useful national indicators came from TALIS 2024 and NZCER’s primary-school study. Those sources showed relatively high teacher adoption but uneven access, confidence, and training.

ERO’s findings confirm and extend that picture:

- AI use is no longer concentrated only in technically confident or affluent schools.
- The more important difference is now **strategic capability**, not basic access.
- Schools vary widely in policy clarity, leadership ownership, staff development, and expectations for students.
- AI use is most mature when framed as workload support, resource development, formative feedback, or structured learning assistance.
- Unplanned student use remains more difficult to manage than teacher use.

This suggests that the next phase of adoption will be determined less by access to tools and more by **institutional routines, staff capability, assessment design, and leadership**.

### 3. SMART has entered a more visible operational phase

The Ministry’s SMART platform is now supporting twice-yearly assessment and aromatawai in Years 3–10. It is aligned with the refreshed New Zealand Curriculum and Te Marautanga o Aotearoa and includes AI assistance for marking open-ended questions. ([newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz](https://newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz/smart-overview-and-access/5637300577.p?activeTab=tab%3A3&utm_source=openai))

The first SMART assessment window was completed during Term 2, with detailed reports released on **3 July 2026**. The platform provides automated marking and reporting for reading, writing, mathematics, pānui, tuhituhi, and pāngarau. ([newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz](https://newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz/5637300577.p?activeTab=tab%3A1&utm_source=openai))

SMART is important because it represents a different model from classroom experimentation:

- It is centrally provided.
- It is aligned to national curriculum frameworks.
- It is intended to support consistent progress monitoring.
- It combines automated processes with teacher and kaiako interpretation.
- It introduces AI-assisted assessment into ordinary school operations rather than leaving use entirely to individual teachers.

The key issue will be whether automated reports improve instructional decisions without encouraging excessive standardisation or reducing professional understanding of learner context.

### 4. NZQA’s AI programme is entering a funded expansion phase

Budget 2026 allocated **$2.1 million in operating funding** for NZQA to develop pilots and proofs of concept involving automated text scoring, AI, and machine learning for marking, moderation, and examination development across NCEA and New Zealand Scholarship. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/our-work/publications/corporate-documents/budget-2026/investing-secondary-achievement?utm_source=openai))

This builds on NZQA’s existing use of automated text scoring in literacy and numeracy co-requisite assessments. NZQA’s current model combines automated scoring with human check-marking, particularly for responses close to achievement boundaries. ([www2.nzqa.govt.nz](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/tertiary/assessment-and-moderation-of-standards/academic-integrity-and-artificial-intelligence/?utm_source=openai))

The expansion is strategically significant because it could influence:

- Assessment turnaround times.
- Consistency across markers and cohorts.
- Moderation workload.
- Examination development.
- The design of future national qualifications.
- Public expectations about the role of human judgment in assessment.

The direction remains cautious. AI is being positioned as an aid to consistency and efficiency, not as a replacement for professional accountability.

### 5. Tertiary education capability-building is becoming a formal market

Since the previous update, New Zealand universities and university-linked providers have made AI capability more explicit through new programmes, short courses, and sector events.

The University of Canterbury has developed an online Master of Applied Artificial Intelligence, with certificate and diploma pathways for professionals in fields including education, health, government, law, business, and technology. The programme emphasises practical application, risk management, ethical responsibilities, and decision-making in the New Zealand context. ([canterbury.ac.nz](https://www.canterbury.ac.nz/news-and-events/news/2026/uc-online-develops-applied-ai-programme-for-professionals-?utm_source=openai))

UC Online is also offering:

- A postgraduate certificate in AI and digital education.
- A short course on designing authentic assessment in the age of AI.
- Courses focused on AI-informed pedagogy, assessment redesign, learner agency, and educational integrity. ([uconline.ac.nz](https://uconline.ac.nz/courses/designing-authentic-assessment?utm_source=openai))

The University of Otago has announced a 2026 New Zealand AI in Higher Education Symposium, including an immersion lab and awards for practical AI systems and workflows in higher education. ([otago.ac.nz](https://www.otago.ac.nz/news/events/2026-new-zealand-ai-in-higher-education-symposium?utm_source=openai))

These developments indicate that AI in education is becoming not only a governance issue but also a **professional field of study and institutional capability**.

## Current State of Adoption

## Schools and Kura

### Usage is widespread, but structured implementation is limited

The ERO findings show that AI is now used by most groups within schools. Teachers commonly use it to:

- Develop lesson resources.
- Adapt materials for different learners.
- Generate activities and ideas.
- Summarise information.
- Support administrative work.
- Assist with differentiation and planning.

School leaders commonly use AI to:

- Draft communications.
- Summarise information.
- Support administrative tasks.
- Prepare documents and planning material.

Students commonly use AI to:

- Find information.
- Generate ideas.
- Improve spelling, grammar, and writing.
- Explain concepts.
- Complete or accelerate parts of schoolwork.

The adoption pattern is therefore primarily **productivity-led and support-led**, rather than based on fully autonomous teaching or tutoring systems. ([odt.co.nz](https://www.odt.co.nz/news/national/its-addictive-ai-damaging-critical-thinking-in-schools-report-finds-ujwe515m?utm_source=openai))

### Student use is normalised but not consistently understood

ERO reported that students generally see AI as a normal part of school and home life. Many use it constructively, but a substantial minority also use it as a shortcut when work is repetitive, difficult, or competing with other demands. ([odt.co.nz](https://www.odt.co.nz/news/national/its-addictive-ai-damaging-critical-thinking-in-schools-report-finds-ujwe515m?utm_source=openai))

The main risks are:

- Students completing work without developing underlying knowledge.
- Reduced practice in writing, reasoning, and problem-solving.
- Confusion about when AI use is permitted.
- Unequal access to higher-quality tools.
- Overreliance on AI-generated explanations.
- Use of AI without verification or source checking.
- Difficulty distinguishing learning support from substitution of student work.

The evidence does not show that all student AI use is harmful. Rather, it shows that outcomes depend heavily on whether students are taught **when to use AI, how to question it, and when not to use it**.

### Teacher confidence and professional learning remain bottlenecks

The ERO report reinforces an established pattern: use is ahead of capability development. Teachers may be using AI for practical tasks without having received formal training in:

- Prompting and verification.
- Bias and hallucination.
- Privacy and data protection.
- Māori data sovereignty.
- Assessment integrity.
- Appropriate use with children.
- Designing AI-resilient learning activities.
- Supporting students to develop independent thinking.

The fact that 73% of teachers had not seen the Ministry’s guidance is especially important. It suggests that publishing guidance is insufficient unless accompanied by active dissemination, professional learning, leadership support, and time for implementation. ([odt.co.nz](https://www.odt.co.nz/news/national/its-addictive-ai-damaging-critical-thinking-in-schools-report-finds-ujwe515m?utm_source=openai))

## Assessment and Academic Integrity

### Assessment redesign remains the dominant response

New Zealand’s education system continues to move away from trying to detect all AI-generated work and toward redesigning assessment so that learning is more visible.

Common approaches include:

- Drafting and checkpoint requirements.
- Oral questioning or short verbal checks.
- In-class writing and supervised tasks.
- Reflection on the student’s process.
- Version history and working notes.
- Explicit declarations of AI use.
- Clear distinctions between AI-permitted and AI-restricted tasks.
- Greater use of authentic, contextual, collaborative, or applied assessment.

This approach is reflected in Ministry guidance, NZQA materials, the University of Auckland’s Two-Lane Approach, and AUT’s two-channel assessment model. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/education-professionals/schools-year-0-13/digital-technology/generative-ai?utm_source=openai))

### AI detection is not a sufficient integrity strategy

The New Zealand policy direction is increasingly consistent with the view that AI detectors should not be treated as definitive evidence of misconduct. Detection tools can produce false positives, are vulnerable to changing models, and do not establish how a student produced a piece of work.

The more defensible model is a combination of:

1. Clear rules.
2. Transparent disclosure expectations.
3. Process evidence.
4. Teacher or assessor judgment.
5. Direct observation where necessary.
6. Assessment tasks that require personal, local, practical, or oral evidence.

This does not eliminate misconduct, but it shifts the emphasis from uncertain technological detection to stronger assessment design and professional judgment.

### NZQA is testing the boundaries of machine-supported assessment

NZQA’s existing automated text-scoring model provides the most significant national case study. It demonstrates that machine support can be integrated into high-volume assessment when accompanied by human review and quality controls. ([www2.nzqa.govt.nz](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/tertiary/assessment-and-moderation-of-standards/academic-integrity-and-artificial-intelligence/?utm_source=openai))

The next phase—AI and machine learning for moderation and examination development—will be more consequential because these functions involve:

- Standard setting.
- Comparability across years.
- Cultural and linguistic context.
- Borderline judgments.
- Potential bias in training data.
- Public confidence in qualifications.

The success of these pilots will depend not only on technical accuracy but also on explainability, auditability, transparency, and the ability of students, teachers, and communities to understand how decisions are made.

## Tertiary Education

### University of Auckland: controlled AI environments and assessment lanes

The University of Auckland continues to implement a Two-Lane Approach:

- **Controlled assessments** restrict or prohibit AI where the purpose is to establish individual capability.
- **Uncontrolled assessments** allow AI where using it reflects the intended learning or professional context.

The university has also continued developing Cogniti, a bounded AI environment integrated with course delivery and intended for course-specific tutoring, role-play, and learning support. ([teachwell.auckland.ac.nz](https://teachwell.auckland.ac.nz/announcement-type/ai-in-education/?utm_source=openai))

The significance of this model is that it connects policy with infrastructure. Rather than simply telling students that AI is allowed or prohibited, it creates a programme-level structure for deciding:

- What students need to demonstrate independently.
- What AI capabilities they should learn to use.
- Where human observation is required.
- How assessment evidence will be collected over time.

### AUT: whole-of-institution assessment redesign

AUT’s institutional approach remains centred on a two-channel model:

- A channel for assessments requiring independent, non-AI demonstration.
- A channel for AI-permitted learning and assessment.

Its wider AI principles emphasise human-centred use, equity, Māori and Indigenous data considerations, integrity, and the importance of human connection in education. AUT is also developing AI teaching-assistant and learning-design work, including a New Zealand business-education case study presented through international higher-education networks. ([aut.ac.nz](https://www.aut.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0017/1036061/Part-A-Papers-Open-28-July-2025.pdf?utm_source=openai))

### University of Otago: governance-first adoption

Otago’s AI Governance Policy remains one of the strongest examples of institution-wide governance in the New Zealand tertiary sector. It covers AI development, procurement, deployment, and use, and incorporates risk management, Te Tiriti obligations, Māori data sovereignty, Pacific values, privacy, and environmental considerations. ([otago.ac.nz](https://www.otago.ac.nz/news/events/2026-new-zealand-ai-in-higher-education-symposium?utm_source=openai))

The Otago model illustrates an important trend: universities are treating AI as a cross-functional governance issue involving:

- Academic quality.
- Information security.
- Privacy.
- Procurement.
- Research integrity.
- Student support.
- Equity and inclusion.
- Staff capability.
- Cultural obligations.

### University of Canterbury: AI literacy and workforce preparation

UC’s Scaffolded AI Literacy framework continues to position AI literacy as a staged capability involving critical judgment, responsible use, citizenship, and awareness of different community perspectives. ([canterbury.ac.nz](https://www.canterbury.ac.nz/news-and-events/news/2026/new-research-helps-rethink-ai-literacy-in-education-?utm_source=openai))

The university’s new applied AI programmes extend that work into workforce development. This is particularly relevant to education because teachers, school leaders, policy staff, and education administrators are likely to need applied AI capability without becoming AI engineers. ([canterbury.ac.nz](https://www.canterbury.ac.nz/news-and-events/news/2026/uc-online-develops-applied-ai-programme-for-professionals-?utm_source=openai))

## Assessment Technology and Digital Infrastructure

### SMART: centrally supported automated reporting

SMART represents a major step toward national digital assessment infrastructure. Its use of automated marking and reporting is intended to reduce workload and give schools consistent information about learner progress. ([newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz](https://newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz/smart-overview-and-access/5637300577.p?activeTab=tab%3A3&utm_source=openai))

Potential benefits include:

- Faster feedback.
- More consistent assessment processes.
- Better visibility of progress across schools.
- Support for early identification of learning needs.
- Reduced administrative burden.
- Improved conversations with whānau.

Potential risks include:

- Overreliance on standardised outputs.
- Misinterpretation of automated reports.
- Reduced attention to learner context.
- Bias or cultural mismatch.
- Privacy and data-governance concerns.
- Pressure to teach to measurable indicators.

The critical implementation question is whether SMART remains a decision-support tool for teachers and kaiako, rather than becoming a substitute for professional interpretation.

### NZQA: national-scale machine-supported assessment

NZQA’s programme is more mature in operational terms than most classroom AI initiatives. The combination of automated text scoring and human check-marking provides a model for **human-in-the-loop assessment**. ([www2.nzqa.govt.nz](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/tertiary/assessment-and-moderation-of-standards/academic-integrity-and-artificial-intelligence/?utm_source=openai))

The planned expansion into moderation and exam development makes 2026–27 a potentially important period for determining how much of the national qualifications system can be supported by AI without weakening trust in results.

## Policy, Governance, and Trust

### The policy gap is now more visible

The Ministry has published principles-based guidance centred on:

- Teacher responsibility.
- Professional judgment.
- Human oversight.
- Appropriate use cases.
- Student authenticity.
- Privacy and age restrictions.
- Careful use of AI for marking.

The guidance states that AI should support, not replace, teachers and that AI-assisted marking should not displace teacher judgment. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/education-professionals/schools-year-0-13/digital-technology/generative-ai?utm_source=openai))

ERO’s report shows, however, that the existence of guidance has not translated into consistent awareness or implementation. This creates a policy gap between:

- National principles.
- School-level policies.
- Classroom practice.
- Student understanding.
- Assessment decisions.

### Privacy and cultural legitimacy remain central

AI tools used in education may process:

- Student writing.
- Voice recordings.
- Behavioural information.
- Learning-progress data.
- Special education or wellbeing information.
- Identifying information.
- Māori and culturally significant data.

New Zealand institutions therefore need to consider privacy, informed consent, vendor data practices, model training, data location, retention, security, and Māori data sovereignty before deploying AI tools at scale.

The University of Otago’s governance model and the Ministry’s existing guidance both demonstrate that AI adoption in Aotearoa cannot be separated from Te Tiriti obligations, cultural safety, equity, and community trust. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/education-professionals/schools-year-0-13/digital-technology/generative-ai?utm_source=openai))

### Equity is shifting from access to quality of access

ERO did not identify a straightforward socioeconomic digital divide in school AI use. AI appears to be used across a broad range of schools. ([odt.co.nz](https://www.odt.co.nz/news/national/its-addictive-ai-damaging-critical-thinking-in-schools-report-finds-ujwe515m?utm_source=openai))

However, equity concerns remain in other forms:

- Some schools have clearer policies and better professional learning.
- Some students have access to premium tools and stronger home support.
- Māori-medium and culturally specific contexts may be poorly represented in mainstream models.
- Students with disabilities may benefit from AI but also face new privacy and accessibility risks.
- Schools differ in their capacity to evaluate vendors and manage data.
- Students may receive unequal guidance about acceptable use.

The emerging equity question is therefore not simply whether students can access AI, but whether they have access to **safe, culturally relevant, well-supported, educationally valuable AI use**.

## Research Overview

### ERO: the most important new national evidence

ERO’s 2026 review is currently the most significant New Zealand evidence on school-level AI adoption. Its main contribution is to connect usage data with implementation quality, student behaviour, teacher concerns, and institutional readiness. ([odt.co.nz](https://www.odt.co.nz/news/national/its-addictive-ai-damaging-critical-thinking-in-schools-report-finds-ujwe515m?utm_source=openai))

Its central message is not that AI should be rejected. It is that adoption is happening faster than coherent governance and capability-building.

### TALIS 2024: teachers remain ahead of policy

TALIS 2024 remains the best comparative benchmark for teacher use. New Zealand lower-secondary teachers reported substantially higher AI use than the OECD average, with common applications including lesson planning, content development, and summarisation. Use for marking remained much less common than use for preparation and support. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/education-professionals/schools-year-0-13/digital-technology/generative-ai?utm_source=openai))

The TALIS evidence and ERO findings together indicate that New Zealand teachers are relatively active users but do not yet operate within a fully coherent national capability framework.

### NZCER: primary schools need greater support

NZCER’s research found widespread teacher experimentation in primary schools alongside gaps in funded access, confidence, policy clarity, and training. A large majority of surveyed teachers wanted more professional learning. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/education-professionals/schools-year-0-13/digital-technology/generative-ai?utm_source=openai))

The newer ERO findings suggest that these concerns are not isolated to primary schools. They are part of a wider system pattern in which practical use is developing faster than formal support.

### UC’s SAIL framework: AI literacy is broader than tool proficiency

The University of Canterbury’s SAIL work contributes a distinctly educational definition of AI literacy. It emphasises critical thinking, responsible decision-making, citizenship, and the ability to judge when AI should or should not be used. ([canterbury.ac.nz](https://www.canterbury.ac.nz/news-and-events/news/2026/new-research-helps-rethink-ai-literacy-in-education-?utm_source=openai))

This is consistent with the direction of New Zealand policy: AI literacy is increasingly understood as a cross-curricular capability rather than a narrow technical subject.

## Case Studies

### 1. ERO: national diagnosis of school readiness

**What happened:** ERO surveyed and visited schools to understand how teachers, leaders, students, and parents are using AI.

**What it demonstrates:**

- AI use is widespread.
- School-level strategy is inconsistent.
- Student use is more normalised than policy.
- Teachers want clearer national support.
- Critical-thinking concerns are significant.
- A national framework is likely to be more useful than isolated school-by-school interpretation.

**Strategic lesson:** New Zealand has moved beyond the pilot stage, but system coordination has not caught up.

### 2. NZQA: human-in-the-loop automated assessment

**What happened:** NZQA used automated text scoring alongside human marking and check-marking in literacy and numeracy co-requisite assessment. Budget 2026 now funds further pilots. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/our-work/publications/corporate-documents/budget-2026/investing-secondary-achievement?utm_source=openai))

**What it demonstrates:**

- AI can support high-volume national assessment.
- Human review remains essential, particularly around boundaries.
- Efficiency gains can be achieved without removing accountability.
- Expansion into moderation and exam development will require stronger assurance processes.

**Strategic lesson:** The most credible public-sector model is augmentation with auditability, not full automation.

### 3. SMART: national digital assessment support

**What happened:** SMART introduced automated marking and reporting for curriculum-aligned assessment in Years 3–10, with first detailed reports released in July 2026. ([newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz](https://newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz/5637300577.p?activeTab=tab%3A1&utm_source=openai))

**What it demonstrates:**

- AI-assisted assessment is becoming embedded in routine school administration.
- National alignment can improve consistency.
- Teacher interpretation and whānau communication remain important safeguards.

**Strategic lesson:** Digital assessment systems should provide evidence for professional decisions, not replace them.

### 4. University of Auckland: Two-Lane assessment and Cogniti

**What happened:** The university combined an institution-wide assessment model with a controlled AI environment for course-specific applications. ([teachwell.auckland.ac.nz](https://teachwell.auckland.ac.nz/announcement-type/ai-in-education/?utm_source=openai))

**What it demonstrates:**

- AI policy is more effective when tied to course design.
- Students need opportunities both to work without AI and to learn responsible AI use.
- Bounded tools may provide stronger governance than unrestricted external chatbots.

**Strategic lesson:** Tertiary institutions are moving toward programme-level assurance rather than individual assignment rules alone.

### 5. UC Online: educator capability as a formal qualification pathway

**What happened:** UC Online introduced postgraduate and short-course options focused on AI, digital education, authentic assessment, and applied AI capability. ([uconline.ac.nz](https://uconline.ac.nz/courses/designing-authentic-assessment?utm_source=openai))

**What it demonstrates:**

- AI literacy is becoming a recognised professional-development field.
- Education professionals need applied, ethical, and context-specific capability.
- Universities are responding to demand from sectors beyond technology.

**Strategic lesson:** Sustainable adoption will require a larger pipeline of educators and leaders able to evaluate and govern AI.

## Emerging Trends

### 1. Adoption is widespread, but maturity is uneven

The dividing line is no longer “AI users versus non-users.” It is increasingly between organisations with:

- Clear policy.
- Named leadership responsibility.
- Professional learning.
- Approved tools.
- Assessment redesign.
- Privacy processes.
- Cultural and community engagement.

### 2. Assessment is becoming the main design problem

AI is forcing education providers to clarify what they want students to demonstrate independently and what capabilities they expect students to develop with AI support.

The most resilient assessments are likely to combine:

- Authentic tasks.
- Process evidence.
- Multiple checkpoints.
- Oral or practical demonstration.
- Contextualised outputs.
- Explicit AI-use declarations.

### 3. AI literacy is becoming cross-curricular

ERO’s recommendation to integrate AI into learning rather than create a standalone subject aligns with the direction of UC’s SAIL framework and existing Ministry curriculum work. ([canterbury.ac.nz](https://www.canterbury.ac.nz/news-and-events/news/2026/new-research-helps-rethink-ai-literacy-in-education-?utm_source=openai))

AI literacy is likely to be embedded across:

- English and literacy.
- Mathematics.
- Digital Technologies.
- Science.
- Social sciences.
- The arts.
- Vocational education.
- Information and media literacy.
- Citizenship and ethics.

### 4. National assessment agencies are moving faster than many schools

NZQA and SMART now represent the most visible operational deployments. Many schools, by contrast, are still developing basic policies and professional-learning arrangements.

This creates a possible mismatch: central agencies may be able to use AI in controlled environments more consistently than individual schools can manage unrestricted classroom and student use.

### 5. Governance is becoming a competitive capability

Universities and providers with clear governance structures are better positioned to adopt AI safely. Governance is not merely a compliance layer; it affects:

- Procurement speed.
- Staff confidence.
- Student trust.
- Data protection.
- Assessment quality.
- Public legitimacy.
- Ability to scale successful pilots.

### 6. Human judgment is becoming more explicit, not less important

Across Ministry guidance, ERO findings, NZQA practice, and university assessment models, the common direction is clear:

> AI may support educational work, but responsibility for educational judgment remains with people.

This applies to teachers, kaiako, assessors, school leaders, academic staff, and institutional decision-makers.

## Strategic Implications for New Zealand Education Stakeholders

### For government and system agencies

Priority actions include:

- Develop a national AI-in-education framework, as recommended by ERO.
- Align Ministry, NZQA, ERO, and tertiary-sector guidance.
- Fund sustained professional learning rather than one-off resources.
- Establish clear privacy, procurement, and data-governance expectations.
- Include Māori and Pacific perspectives in AI policy and curriculum design.
- Publish evaluation findings from SMART and NZQA pilots.
- Ensure national guidance reaches schools in practical, usable formats.

### For schools and kura

Effective implementation is likely to require:

- A named AI lead or leadership group.
- A clear staff and student policy.
- Examples of permitted, restricted, and prohibited use.
- Teacher training in verification, privacy, bias, and assessment design.
- Explicit teaching of AI literacy.
- Processes for documenting student work and AI use.
- Careful review of vendor terms and data practices.
- Ongoing engagement with whānau and communities.

### For tertiary institutions

Universities and tertiary providers should:

- Adopt programme-level assessment frameworks.
- Distinguish between AI-restricted and AI-permitted learning outcomes.
- Build AI literacy into disciplinary curricula.
- Provide approved tools where appropriate.
- Avoid relying solely on detection systems.
- Strengthen staff development and student guidance.
- Establish governance that covers teaching, research, procurement, privacy, and equity.

### For technology providers

Education-specific AI vendors will need to demonstrate:

- Curriculum alignment.
- Strong privacy controls.
- Clear data-retention and training policies.
- Explainable outputs.
- Human override mechanisms.
- Accessibility.
- Cultural and linguistic relevance.
- Evidence of educational benefit rather than simple productivity claims.

## Conclusion

As of **17 August 2026**, AI in New Zealand education is best characterised as **widely adopted, unevenly governed, and increasingly embedded in assessment infrastructure**.

The most important development since the 13 July 2026 update is ERO’s national review. It confirms that AI use is already mainstream among school leaders, teachers, and students, while also showing that policy awareness, strategic planning, professional learning, and student guidance have not kept pace. ([odt.co.nz](https://www.odt.co.nz/news/national/its-addictive-ai-damaging-critical-thinking-in-schools-report-finds-ujwe515m?utm_source=openai))

At the same time, adoption is becoming more systematic:

- NZQA is expanding funded AI and machine-learning pilots.
- SMART is delivering automated marking and reporting in curriculum-aligned assessment.
- Universities are implementing structured AI-permitted and AI-restricted assessment models.
- New qualifications and short courses are building AI capability among educators and professionals.
- National and institutional governance is increasingly incorporating privacy, Te Tiriti, Māori data sovereignty, equity, and human oversight.

New Zealand is therefore not moving toward a simple “AI replaces teachers” model. The emerging model is more constrained and more demanding: AI supports planning, administration, learning, feedback, assessment operations, and professional work, while educators remain responsible for interpretation, relationships, authenticity, cultural safety, and final judgment.

The defining risk is not lack of adoption. It is **ad hoc adoption without sufficient capability or governance**. The defining opportunity is to use AI to improve education while preserving the human, cultural, and developmental purposes of schooling and tertiary learning.

The next stage of the sector’s development will be determined by whether New Zealand can convert high levels of informal use into **coherent, equitable, evidence-based, and culturally legitimate practice**.
