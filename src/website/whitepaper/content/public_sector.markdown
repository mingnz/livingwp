# AI in Public Sector in Aotearoa New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper
**Updated: May 29, 2026**

## Introduction

Since the previous **April 2, 2026** edition, New Zealand’s public-sector AI story has become more concrete in three ways: central coordination has tightened, frontline and customer-facing deployments have become more visible, and the machinery for safe adoption has become more operational. The centre of gravity is no longer just frameworks and pilots. It is now shared delivery infrastructure, agency-ready toolkits, targeted workforce training, regulator-specific guidance, and a growing set of bounded production uses across central and local government. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/digital-government/leadership/government-digital-delivery-agency?source=rss&utm_source=openai))

The overall pattern remains conservative by design. Most public-sector AI in Aotearoa is still being used to **assist** people rather than replace them: search, summarisation, reporting, drafting, pattern detection, triage support, documentation, and service navigation. Human accountability remains the operating norm, and public trust still looks like the main constraint on how far and how fast agencies can move into higher-stakes uses. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/standards-and-guidance/technology-and-architecture/artificial-intelligence/public-service-ai-work-programme))

## Executive Snapshot

- **System-wide adoption remains substantial and growing.** The most recent cross-agency evidence still shows **70 agencies reporting 272 AI use cases**, including **55 deployed or operational** uses, up sharply from **108 use cases across 37 agencies in 2024**. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/dmsdocument/263~report-2025-cross-agency-survey-for-artificial-intelligence-ai-use-cases/html))
- **Central digital leadership has been restructured for delivery.** On **April 1, 2026**, the **Government Digital Delivery Agency (GDDA)** was established inside the Public Service Commission, taking on the functions of the Government Chief Digital Office and combining system leadership, delivery support, and capability functions. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/digital-government/leadership/government-digital-delivery-agency?source=rss&utm_source=openai))
- **The public-service AI operating stack is getting more usable.** By late May 2026, Digital.govt.nz was surfacing a consolidated **Public Service AI Toolkit** with an agency AI policy template and records-management guidance for AI, complementing the existing framework and GenAI guidance. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/standards-and-guidance/technology-and-architecture/artificial-intelligence/public-service-ai-toolkit))
- **Capability building has moved from intention to delivery.** The public-service **AI Development Programme** began its first cohort on **May 18, 2026**, with the first facilitated workshop on **May 28**; a broader self-paced **AI Development Series** is expected in **July 2026**. ([ldc.govt.nz](https://www.ldc.govt.nz/public-service-core-learning-hub/system-capability/ai-capability/ai-development-programme?utm_source=openai))
- **A new regulator-specific guidance layer arrived this week.** The Ministry for Regulation released **AI guidance for regulators** on **May 27, 2026**, backed by bite-sized guides published in May 2026, explicitly positioning AI as a practical tool for analysis, pattern detection, and decision support while retaining human responsibility. ([regulation.govt.nz](https://www.regulation.govt.nz/news/helping-regulators-use-ai-with-confidence/))
- **Customer-facing local-government AI has advanced materially.** Auckland Council launched **Ask Auckland Council** in April 2026 as a web-based AI assistant for reporting common issues, following testing with **1,000 Aucklanders** and published usability results. ([ourauckland.aucklandcouncil.govt.nz](https://ourauckland.aucklandcouncil.govt.nz/news/2026/04/new-ai-assistant-for-fast-easy-problem-reporting/))
- **Health remains the clearest national frontline deployment.** As of **February 28, 2026**, every emergency department in New Zealand had access to an **AI scribe**, with rollout completed to **1,250 ED doctors and frontline staff**; Health New Zealand is also progressing **more than 1,000 additional licences**, mainly for mental health teams. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department))
- **Trust is still lagging capability.** Only **4%** of New Zealanders told Kantar they felt well informed about how government uses AI, while **55%** said they were comfortable with government agencies using it. In the wider service context, **84%** of New Zealanders trust the government service they most recently used, and trust in the Public Service brand sits at **64%**. ([kantarnewzealand.com](https://www.kantarnewzealand.com/ai-in-the-public-sector/))

## What Changed Since the April 2, 2026 Edition

### 1) Central AI enablement is now more operational

The biggest shift since early April is that central enablement is becoming easier for agencies to use. The **GDDA** is now formally in place inside the Public Service Commission, and Digital.govt.nz is presenting a more practical AI support stack: the **Public Service AI Toolkit**, an agency **AI policy template**, and explicit **records-management guidance** for AI outputs and workflows. This is a meaningful move from “principles only” toward reusable implementation support. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/digital-government/leadership/government-digital-delivery-agency?source=rss&utm_source=openai))

### 2) Workforce capability has moved into live delivery

Since the last edition, the public service has shifted from discussing AI capability uplift to actually running it. The new **AI Development Programme** started its first cohort in May, the programme is built around practical public-sector scenarios and human oversight, and the wider **AI Development Series** is expected in July. This suggests capability building is being treated as a scaling prerequisite rather than an optional extra. ([ldc.govt.nz](https://www.ldc.govt.nz/public-service-core-learning-hub/system-capability/ai-capability/ai-development-programme?utm_source=openai))

### 3) Regulators now have their own AI playbook

A distinct May 2026 development is the Ministry for Regulation’s new guidance package for regulators. It explicitly frames AI as useful for processing large information volumes, spotting patterns, and supporting consistency, while insisting that people remain responsible for decisions. That matters because regulation is one of the first public-sector domains where AI use can quickly become consequential. ([regulation.govt.nz](https://www.regulation.govt.nz/news/helping-regulators-use-ai-with-confidence/))

### 4) Local-government public-facing AI took a visible step forward

The clearest new frontline service launch since April is **Ask Auckland Council**. Unlike earlier examples focused on internal productivity, this is a live public interface for common reporting tasks, with conversational intake, photo analysis, te reo Māori place-name understanding, and published trial feedback. It is one of the strongest current examples of bounded, citizen-facing AI in New Zealand government. ([ourauckland.aucklandcouncil.govt.nz](https://ourauckland.aucklandcouncil.govt.nz/news/2026/04/new-ai-assistant-for-fast-easy-problem-reporting/))

### 5) Budget 2026 added fresh AI-adjacent delivery pressure

Budget week brought two notable signals. First, the **National Emergency Management Agency’s EMS-OS programme** was funded to deliver a common operating picture and to look for opportunities to use **automation and AI** during emergencies. Second, Health New Zealand received cyber-security funding that includes **AI-enabled assessments** to lift cyber maturity across primary care. These are not headline “AI transformation” announcements, but they show AI being embedded into core operational systems. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-technology-means-safer-communities))

## Current State of AI Adoption

## Governance and Operating Model

New Zealand’s governance base still rests on the **Public Service AI Framework** and the **Responsible AI Guidance for the Public Service: GenAI**, with the GDDA/GCDO leading safe and trustworthy public-service AI adoption. Cabinet’s 2024 direction remains to encourage agencies to adopt AI for its benefits while managing risk, and the **Public Service AI Work Programme** continues to translate that into deliverables such as an assurance model, safety mechanisms, supplier partnerships, workforce training, and a Govt.nz AI assistant. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/standards-and-guidance/technology-and-architecture/artificial-intelligence/gcdo-role))

The operating model is also becoming more centralised. The **Digitising Government Programme** explicitly aims to reduce digital costs, centralise procurement, and provide more joined-up services, while the **Digital Target State** and **Service Modernisation Roadmap v2** position AI inside a broader reusable public infrastructure layer and a unified digital front door. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/digital-government/digitising-government-programme-dgp/overview-of-the-digitising-government-programme?source=rss))

## Scale and Maturity

The best system-wide evidence remains the **2025 cross-agency AI survey**, which shows AI use has moved beyond scattered experimentation. The survey recorded **272 use cases** from **70 agencies**, including **207** from **44 public service organisations** and **65** from **26 wider public-sector organisations**. The number of operational uses reached **55**, compared with **15** a year earlier. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/dmsdocument/263~report-2025-cross-agency-survey-for-artificial-intelligence-ai-use-cases/html))

The pattern inside that portfolio still points to augmentation rather than autonomy. Current use is concentrated in internal productivity, analysis, administration, communications, search, and workflow support, with a smaller share in direct public-facing service delivery. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/standards-and-guidance/technology-and-architecture/artificial-intelligence/research-ai-public-service/2025-cross-agency-ai-survey-highlights?source=rss&utm_source=openai))

## Workforce Readiness

Workforce readiness remains real but shallow. In the **2025 Public Service Census**, **33%** of public servants said they had used AI for work, but only **14%** used it regularly. Although **88%** said they were confident learning new digital skills, only **42%** agreed their organisation takes advantage of technology to deliver better public services or information. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/data/public-service-census/delivery/innovation))

That gap helps explain the new emphasis on structured capability building. The new AI capability programme is explicitly designed around safe, responsible use in public-sector settings, with live case-based workshops, foundational learning, and leader sessions. ([ldc.govt.nz](https://www.ldc.govt.nz/public-service-core-learning-hub/system-capability/ai-capability))

## Public Trust and Legitimacy

Trust remains a strength at the service-experience level but a weakness at the AI-specific level. **Kiwis Count** shows **84%** of New Zealanders trust the government service they most recently used, and **88%** satisfaction among those using exclusively digital channels. But Kantar’s 2025 AI study found only **4%** felt well informed about government AI use and just **55%** were comfortable with it. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/data/kiwis-count))

The practical implication is that service trust does not automatically convert into AI trust. Public agencies can still draw on a relatively high trust base, but they need better disclosure, explanation, and accountability if they want to expand AI into more visible or higher-stakes settings. This is a synthesis based on the divergence between general service-trust data and AI-awareness data. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/data/kiwis-count))

## Recent News and Policy Developments

### GDDA establishment sharpened the centre

The **Government Digital Delivery Agency** was established on **April 1, 2026** inside the Public Service Commission. Its stated purpose is to accelerate digital capability across the public sector, create a unified digital front door, centralise digital investment and procurement, and improve use of technologies including AI. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/digital-government/leadership/government-digital-delivery-agency?source=rss&utm_source=openai))

### The AI Toolkit is now a visible agency resource

By late May 2026, the **Public Service AI Toolkit** was being surfaced as a consolidated entry point for agencies. It includes a best-practice **AI policy template** and **records-management guidance** that states AI use does not change what counts as a public record and that records must still be managed under the **Public Records Act 2005**. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/standards-and-guidance/technology-and-architecture/artificial-intelligence/public-service-ai-toolkit))

### Regulators got dedicated AI guidance

On **May 27, 2026**, the Ministry for Regulation published guidance to help regulators use AI safely and practically. It is supported by a May 2026 resource pack covering leadership, procurement, organisational integration, opportunities and risks, and implementation steps. ([regulation.govt.nz](https://www.regulation.govt.nz/news/helping-regulators-use-ai-with-confidence/))

### Budget 2026 embedded AI into operational systems

Budget 2026 backed the **EMS-OS** emergency-management programme, including a common operating picture and future use of automation and AI where appropriate. Budget 2026 also funded Health New Zealand cyber upgrades that include **AI-enabled assessments** to improve cyber-security maturity across primary care. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-technology-means-safer-communities))

## Research Overview

### Cross-agency research

The GCDO’s research page continues to anchor the public evidence base for government AI use, with 2023, 2024, and 2025 survey series now available. The 2025 survey remains the strongest quantitative picture of adoption and confirms that AI is spreading across a much wider agency base than it was even a year earlier. ([dns.govt.nz](https://dns.govt.nz/standards-and-guidance/technology-and-architecture/artificial-intelligence/research-ai-public-service))

### System-level assessment

The **State of the Public Service 2025** argues that New Zealand must reduce fragmentation, accelerate and harmonise technology and AI adoption, and build a unified digital front door. That assessment aligns closely with the current Digitising Government Programme and the AI work programme. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/publications/state-of-the-public-service-te-kahu-tuatini))

### Workforce evidence

The Public Service Census data suggests the main challenge is no longer awareness of AI, but routinised, confident, organisation-supported use. The new public-service AI development programme is best understood as a response to that maturity gap. This is an inference from the census findings and the new training rollout. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/data/public-service-census/delivery/innovation))

## Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Health New Zealand’s AI scribe rollout

Health remains the strongest national example of frontline operational AI. As of **February 28, 2026**, AI scribes were available in **every emergency department**, with **1,250** clinicians and frontline staff covered. Pilot data cited by government said doctors using the tool saw **one additional patient per shift on average**, and further licences are being progressed, mainly for mental health teams. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department))

**Why it matters:** this is scaled, production AI in a live clinical workflow, but still in a tightly bounded assistive role focused on documentation rather than diagnosis or autonomous decision-making. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department))

### Case Study 2: Auckland Council’s Ask Auckland Council

Auckland Council launched **Ask Auckland Council** in April 2026 as a public AI assistant for reporting graffiti, illegal dumping, roaming dogs, missed bin collections, and party noise. The council says the assistant can understand everyday language, place names in te reo Māori, ask follow-up questions, and analyse uploaded photos. In February 2026 it was tested with **1,000 Aucklanders**, with **80%** finding it easy to use versus **42%** for existing channels. ([ourauckland.aucklandcouncil.govt.nz](https://ourauckland.aucklandcouncil.govt.nz/news/2026/04/new-ai-assistant-for-fast-easy-problem-reporting/))

**Why it matters:** it is one of the clearest New Zealand examples of customer-facing, transaction-adjacent AI in local government, and it shows that the next wave of public AI is likely to be narrow, task-specific, and channel-integrated. ([ourauckland.aucklandcouncil.govt.nz](https://ourauckland.aucklandcouncil.govt.nz/news/2026/04/new-ai-assistant-for-fast-easy-problem-reporting/))

### Case Study 3: Hutt City Council’s AI programme

Hutt City Council remains one of the country’s most mature visible local-government adopters. Its public AI page says it has **300 secure AI licences**, an **AI Risk Management Framework**, an internal governance group, and a public **AI Register**. It is also developing **CERA**, a generative-AI emergency information assistant, after being named one of **50 global finalists** in the Bloomberg Philanthropies Mayors Challenge and receiving **US$50,000** to develop and test the prototype. ([huttcity.govt.nz](https://www.huttcity.govt.nz/council/our-projects/ai-at-council))

**Why it matters:** Hutt shows what “organised adoption” looks like when governance, transparency, training, and custom tooling are developed together rather than separately. ([huttcity.govt.nz](https://www.huttcity.govt.nz/council/our-projects/ai-at-council))

### Case Study 4: NZQA’s AI-enabled assessment operations

NZQA continues to be one of the most mature operational users in the public sector. It says automated text scoring was used on more than **55,000 writing assessments** in May 2025, with human quality assurance, and it currently describes co-requisite assessment marking as a mix of automated machine marking, automated text scoring, and human marking. NZQA also published OIA releases on **AI marking and assessment** and **May 2025 automated text scoring results** on **March 6, 2026**. ([www2.nzqa.govt.nz](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/about-us/news/nzqas-responsible-use-of-artificial-intelligence/?utm_source=openai))

**Why it matters:** NZQA shows that New Zealand agencies will use AI in consequential processes when the workflow is narrow, the evaluation regime is strong, and public explanation is provided. ([www2.nzqa.govt.nz](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/about-us/news/nzqas-responsible-use-of-artificial-intelligence/?utm_source=openai))

### Case Study 5: DPMC’s Honours Unit

The Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet states that its **Honours Unit** may use a generative AI tool to summarise nomination material and support letters, with human checking, no retention of personal information, and no data sharing outside DPMC. ([dpmc.govt.nz](https://www.dpmc.govt.nz/our-programmes/new-zealand-royal-honours/privacy-statement))

**Why it matters:** this is a small but strategically important example because it reflects the dominant public-service pattern in 2026: using AI to compress administrative effort while preserving accountable human judgement. ([dpmc.govt.nz](https://www.dpmc.govt.nz/our-programmes/new-zealand-royal-honours/privacy-statement))

## Adoption Patterns and Emerging Trends

### 1) New Zealand is moving from policy to operating discipline

The combination of GDDA centralisation, the public AI toolkit, structured workforce training, and regulator guidance suggests the sector is entering a more disciplined implementation phase. The question is less “should agencies try AI?” and more “what common controls, skills, and reusable assets are needed to scale it safely?” This is a synthesis grounded in the new GDDA structure, toolkit, training, and regulator guidance. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/digital-government/leadership/government-digital-delivery-agency?source=rss&utm_source=openai))

### 2) The dominant model is still bounded augmentation

The most visible deployments remain tightly scoped: ED documentation, reporting problems to council, assessment marking support, nomination summarisation, and property/consent workflow support. That pattern strongly suggests New Zealand’s public sector is still prioritising low-to-medium-risk augmentation over autonomous state action. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department))

### 3) Shared infrastructure is the main strategic lever

The Digitising Government Programme, Service Modernisation Roadmap, Digital Target State, and AI Work Programme all point in the same direction: centralised procurement, reusable channels, AI platform services, and a unified digital front door. The strategic bet is clearly that public-sector AI will scale faster through shared architecture than through agency-by-agency reinvention. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/digital-government/digitising-government-programme-dgp/overview-of-the-digitising-government-programme?source=rss))

### 4) Local government is becoming the most visible innovation layer

Hutt City Council and Auckland Council now provide some of the clearest public-facing examples of live AI use in New Zealand government. That does not mean local government is ahead across the board, but it does suggest councils are becoming an important proving ground for service-interface AI. This is an inference from the visibility and maturity of the currently published council examples. ([huttcity.govt.nz](https://www.huttcity.govt.nz/council/our-projects/ai-at-council))

## Pressure Points and Risks

- **Capability remains a scaling constraint.** Public servants are interested and increasingly exposed to AI, but regular use is still limited and many workers do not think their organisations make strong use of technology. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/data/public-service-census/delivery/innovation))
- **Trust and understanding remain weak.** General trust in public services is solid, but AI-specific awareness is poor, which raises the risk of backlash if agencies expand faster than they explain. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/data/kiwis-count))
- **Governance is becoming operational, not optional.** Records-management obligations still apply to AI-generated material, and the sector is increasingly building practical controls through policy templates, assurance work, and guidance. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/standards-and-guidance/technology-and-architecture/artificial-intelligence/public-service-ai-toolkit/records-managements-for-ai?source=rss))
- **Biometrics are a near-term compliance test.** The **Biometric Processing Privacy Code 2025** was amended in **March 2026** to reflect **IPP3A**, and the transition period for existing biometric processing ends on **August 3, 2026**. ([privacy.org.nz](https://www.privacy.org.nz/privacy-principles/codes-of-practice/biometric-processing-privacy-code/))
- **Cybersecurity and data protection are now central AI-adoption issues.** Budget 2026’s health cyber package, including AI-enabled assessments, underlines that AI expansion and cyber maturity are becoming linked operational priorities. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/investing-cyber-security-protect-patient-data))

## Overall Assessment

As of **May 29, 2026**, AI adoption in New Zealand’s public sector is best described as **organised scaling under constraint**. The sector now has clearer central leadership, a more practical enablement stack, live capability programmes, dedicated regulator guidance, and a broader set of visible production deployments across central and local government. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/digital-government/leadership/government-digital-delivery-agency?source=rss&utm_source=openai))

But the expansion model remains deliberately narrow. New Zealand is mostly deploying AI where it can improve search, reporting, documentation, analysis, and workflow speed without displacing accountable human judgement. That bounded approach is consistent across health, councils, central administration, and assessment operations. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department))

The strategic reality is now clear: the public sector is no longer debating whether AI belongs in government. It is building the conditions for routine use while trying to hold onto legitimacy. The near-term winners will be agencies that can combine **shared infrastructure, workforce fluency, transparent governance, and clearly measurable service gains**. The hard limit is still trust. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/digital-government/digitising-government-programme-dgp/overview-of-the-digitising-government-programme?source=rss))
