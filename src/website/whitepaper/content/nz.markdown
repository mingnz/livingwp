# State of AI in New Zealand

**Updated: 29 May 2026**

## Executive Summary

May 2026 pushed New Zealand’s AI story further into practical implementation, but not into a dramatic new phase. The clearest genuinely new national signal was the Government expanding its AI Advisory Pilot for small businesses from 50 firms to 150, extending it to **31 January 2027**, and pairing that with a nationwide AI roadshow. At the same time, the Ministry for Regulation issued regulator-specific AI guidance, Health New Zealand moved breast-screening AI from exploration into procurement and validation planning, and the Privacy Commissioner published fresh evidence that public concern about AI decision-making remains high. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/more-practical-ai-support-for-small-businesses))

The month’s overall texture was **practical rather than spectacular**. Compared with earlier 2026 snapshots that were dominated by emergency-department scribes, data-centre infrastructure, and the research-platform contest, May was more about helping ordinary firms adopt AI, giving agencies operational rules, and tightening the social licence around AI use. The biggest missing item was research: documents had pointed to late-April and May decisions on the national AI Research Platform, but MBIE’s public page now says only that an updated announcement timeline will be provided “in due course.” ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

## What Happened in the Past Month

### Small-business adoption became the strongest new national signal

The most material economy-wide development in May was the Government’s decision to widen hands-on AI support for SMEs. Through the Regional Business Partner Network, the **AI Advisory Pilot** was expanded from **50 to 150 businesses**, eligibility was broadened, and participating firms can access **up to 50% co-funding, capped at $15,000**, to identify and implement practical AI use cases. That shift matters because it moves New Zealand’s AI policy from high-level strategy into subsidised, on-the-ground business adoption. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/more-practical-ai-support-for-small-businesses))

The same announcement also launched two new **Business Mentors New Zealand** AI tools and tied the package to the **Great New Zealand AI Roadshow**, which began in Auckland on **15 May** and is travelling to other centres through June and early July. In other words, May’s strongest signal was not a frontier-model breakthrough; it was an attempt to diffuse AI capability into the long tail of New Zealand business. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/more-practical-ai-support-for-small-businesses))

### Public-service AI moved from generic encouragement to role-specific operating guidance

Another notable change was the public sector getting more explicit about where and how AI should be used. On **27 May**, the **Ministry for Regulation** released a dedicated guidance package for regulators, focused on lower-risk uses such as working through large volumes of information, spotting patterns, and supporting more consistent decisions. The ministry says agencies should start small, keep people accountable for decisions, and treat AI as support rather than a substitute for human judgement. ([regulation.govt.nz](https://www.regulation.govt.nz/news/helping-regulators-use-ai-with-confidence/?utm_source=openai))

That arrived alongside a broader Government message that AI and digitisation are now part of its productivity agenda for the state sector. In its **19 May** public-service overhaul announcement, the Government explicitly said the programme would increase use of AI and other digital tools as part of a wider effort to streamline agencies and reduce administrative cost. That does not amount to a new whole-of-government AI law or mandate, but it does show AI moving closer to core operating assumptions inside Wellington. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/public-service-be-overhauled))

### Health remained the most concrete public-service domain

Health continued to be the clearest area where AI is moving beyond discussion. On **27 May**, the Government said procurement is now underway to select a preferred **AI mammogram-reading tool** for **testing and validation**, with a **planned rollout from early 2027**. That is a meaningful step forward from February’s exploratory market engagement: breast-screening AI is now moving into product selection and implementation planning. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-breast-screening-tool-one-step-closer))

Budget 2026 also reinforced that health AI cannot be separated from cyber resilience and trust. The Government announced **$153.6 million** for health-sector cyber security, including 24/7 monitoring, third-party risk work, annual audits of critical systems, and **AI-enabled assessments** to improve cyber-security maturity across primary care. After the **Manage My Health** breach, this is a reminder that New Zealand’s health-AI story is now as much about secure digital plumbing as it is about new algorithms. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/investing-cyber-security-protect-patient-data))

### Trust and social licence stayed under pressure

If May’s adoption story strengthened, its trust story did too—but in a cautionary direction. The **Privacy Commissioner’s** latest survey found **67%** of New Zealanders are concerned about government agencies and businesses using AI to make decisions about people using personal data, up **5 percentage points** on 2025. Only **18%** said they were very or extremely confident that New Zealand law adequately protects personal information. ([privacy.org.nz](https://www.privacy.org.nz/tuhono-connect/statements-media-releases/new-survey-privacy-concerns-are-no-longer-niche-worries/))

That helps explain why May’s most serious AI developments were so governance-heavy. New Zealand is still a country where usage is climbing, but confidence has not caught up. The AI Forum’s refreshed **AI Blueprint for Aotearoa**, released on **6 May**, captures that tension directly by describing the country as “high-use, low-trust” and adding **social licence** and **sustainable AI** as new focus areas to 2030. As an industry-body document, it is not government policy, but it is a useful signal of what the organised ecosystem thinks the bottlenecks now are. ([aiforum.org.nz](https://aiforum.org.nz/2026/05/06/ai-blueprint-for-aotearoa-a-refreshed-vision-to-2030/))

### Skills and education kept moving, mostly through guidance and capability-building

The skills picture also shifted upward in May, though again more through capability-building than system redesign. During Satya Nadella’s **21 April** visit to New Zealand, Microsoft announced it would extend access to AI and digital skilling for a **further 200,000 people by the end of 2028**, with programmes aimed at educators, community organisations, and the broader workforce. This is vendor-led rather than public funding, but it is still one of the larger visible skilling commitments in the market. ([news.microsoft.com](https://news.microsoft.com/source/asia/features/microsoft-doubles-its-commitment-to-ai-and-digital-upskilling-in-new-zealand/))

In schools, guidance also became a bit clearer. The Ministry of Education says schools must have policies on acceptable GenAI use, and that **GenAI is not permitted in NCEA external assessment**. Its updated guidance on AI tools for marking stresses teacher responsibility, human oversight, and appropriate use cases, and an NCEA page pointing schools to that guidance appeared on **28 May**. This is still early-stage governance rather than deep classroom transformation, but it shows education steadily catching up to AI’s assessment implications. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/education-professionals/schools-year-0-13/digital-technology/generative-ai/?utm_source=openai))

### Research was the conspicuous near-miss

The biggest item that **didn’t** clearly land in May was the national research-platform decision. Earlier MBIE material set out a timetable pointing to a **late-April platform funding decision** and **May investment announcements** for the AI Research Platform, backed by **up to $70 million over seven years**. MBIE’s live platform page now still lists the phase-two process, but says only that **“an update on a timeline of announcements will be made in due course.”** ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/assets/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform.pdf?utm_source=openai))

That matters because the research platform is supposed to become a national “centre of gravity” for AI science, capability, and commercialisation. As of **29 May 2026**, the strongest public signals this month were about diffusion and governance, not about the research system finally locking in its flagship national AI institution. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/refocusing-the-science-innovation-and-technology-system/public-research-organisations/new-zealand-institute-for-advanced-technology/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform))

## Trend Line Across Recent Snapshots

The centre of gravity has shifted again. **Late February** was about visible frontline deployment in health, especially the nationwide emergency-department scribe rollout. **March** was about infrastructure, privacy guidance, and the mechanics of the research-funding pipeline. **May** looks different again: it is more distributed, more operational, and less headline-driven, with momentum showing up in SME support, regulator playbooks, school guidance, and workforce capability. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

That suggests New Zealand’s AI momentum is **broadening**, but not necessarily **accelerating** in depth. More institutions are doing something; fewer are making giant leaps. This is what a diffusion phase looks like: less theatre, more process. The upside is that AI is reaching beyond a handful of flagship pilots. The downside is that the country is still short of a fresh research decision, a major new commercial breakout, or a new infrastructure milestone to match the intensity of earlier months. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/more-practical-ai-support-for-small-businesses))

## What Looks Quiet, Unchanged, or Early

New Zealand still does **not** have a new AI-specific law or specialist AI regulator. Most of May’s movement was through pilots, guidance, procurement, and capability-building rather than formal new regulation. That keeps the system flexible, but it also means much still depends on agency judgement, vendor practice, and public trust holding up. ([regulation.govt.nz](https://www.regulation.govt.nz/news/helping-regulators-use-ai-with-confidence/?utm_source=openai))

Infrastructure was also quieter as a news story than it was in March. AI data centres remained part of the national conversation—especially around energy use and who captures the benefits—but May did not produce an obvious new public milestone on the scale of Datagrid’s earlier consent and power-deal progress. ([auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/05/13/nz-s-ai-data-centre-boom--who-benefits-.html?utm_source=openai))

And while skills activity is clearly rising, much of it is still **early**: roadshows, school guidance, executive programmes, and industry blueprints are useful scaffolding, but they are not the same thing as nationwide productivity gains or a surge of homegrown AI firms. May showed a country getting more organised around AI, not yet one visibly compounding its advantages at scale. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/more-kiwi-businesses-get-ai-support))

## Overall Assessment

The simplest reading of May 2026 is that AI in Aotearoa New Zealand is becoming **more distributed, more operational, and more governed**. This was a month when the most meaningful changes were not flashy product launches or giant capital rounds, but expanded SME advisory support, regulator guidance, school rules, breast-screening procurement, and renewed attention to privacy and cyber resilience. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/more-practical-ai-support-for-small-businesses))

That is real progress. But it is also a reminder that New Zealand’s AI story is still uneven. **Adoption support is broadening; governance is tightening; health remains the strongest applied domain; skills activity is growing; but research leadership and large-scale commercial breakout remain less settled than the strategy language suggests.** If March was about building the machinery, May was about trying to get more people to use it—carefully. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/more-practical-ai-support-for-small-businesses))
