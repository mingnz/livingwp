# AI in Finance in Aotearoa New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper

**Snapshot date: August 18, 2026**  
**Updated from the July 13, 2026 edition**  
**Article slug: `finance`**

## Introduction

AI adoption across New Zealand’s finance sector—banking, insurance, financial advice, payments, fintech and wealth management—continues to develop through **supervised, operationally focused applications** rather than widespread autonomous decision-making.

The strongest public evidence remains concentrated in:

- Fraud, scam and cyber-risk detection
- Contact-centre and frontline staff assistance
- Adviser productivity and document preparation
- Claims and service workflow automation
- Open-banking-enabled payments and data services
- Early experimentation with agentic commerce

Since the July 13 edition, the most significant development has been the broadening of AI from an internal productivity issue into a matter of **payments infrastructure, consumer consent, privacy disclosure, financial-market regulation and operational resilience**.

---

## Executive Summary

- **AI adoption is becoming more embedded but remains human-supervised.** Public deployments in banking, insurance and advice continue to focus on staff augmentation, summarisation, fraud detection and workflow assistance rather than fully automated consequential decisions. This is consistent with the FMA’s finding that AI in financial advice is predominantly being used to enable advisers rather than replace them. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/assets/Reports/Access-to-advice-Financial-advice-sector-challenges-and-opportunities.pdf?utm_source=openai))
- **Agentic payments have moved from concept toward controlled ecosystem testing.** Mastercard completed authenticated agentic transactions involving a Westpac-issued card, while ANZ NZ, ASB, BNZ and Kiwibank joined Visa’s Agentic Ready programme. ([westpac.co.nz](https://www.westpac.co.nz/about-us/media/mastercard-completes-nzs-first-authenticated-agentic-transactions-with-westpac/))
- **Governance is becoming visible to customers.** Westpac’s updated privacy policy now explicitly describes the use of AI and machine learning for fraud prevention, risk management, customer service and service improvement, subject to safeguards and human oversight. ([westpac.co.nz](https://www.westpac.co.nz/about-us/legal-information-privacy/changes-to-westpac-terms-and-conditions/?utm_source=openai))
- **Open banking is now a functioning foundation layer.** API Centre reporting shows the four major banks were ready for payment and account-information APIs, while Kiwibank became ready for both on May 28, 2026. ([apicentre.paymentsnz.co.nz](https://www.apicentre.paymentsnz.co.nz/standards/implementation/implementation-reporting/))
- **Measured productivity gains remain strongest in service and advice workflows.** Tower reports saving customers more than 796,000 minutes through its AI-enabled contact centre, while NZHL reports an 80% reduction in annual client-review preparation time. ([tower.co.nz](https://www.tower.co.nz/news/contact-centre-cuts-call-time/?utm_source=openai))
- **Regulators remain cautious.** The FMA is planning a thematic review of AI in financial advice, while RBNZ continues to highlight third-party dependency, cyber risk, biased or fraudulent outputs and possible credit-market effects. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/assets/Reports/Access-to-advice-Financial-advice-sector-challenges-and-opportunities.pdf?utm_source=openai))
- **The evidence base is still incomplete.** Stats NZ has launched a new national business survey that will measure AI adoption across approximately 20,000 businesses, but results were not yet available as of August 18, 2026. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/business-ai-use-covered-new-national-survey))

## What Has Changed Since July 13, 2026

### 1. AI is becoming part of formal financial-market reform

MBIE’s July 2026 consultation on capital-markets reform explicitly asks how New Zealand should respond to **digital and AI-related innovation in financial products and markets**. This indicates that AI is now being considered alongside broader questions of market structure, investor access, crowdfunding, wholesale markets and digital finance—not merely as an internal technology issue. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/have-your-say/consultation-on-capital-markets-reform?utm_source=openai))

### 2. Industry discussion has shifted toward open finance and responsible AI

The Financial Services Council’s August 12 conference programme placed “AI, Open Finance and the Next Frontier of Financial Services” alongside mainstream sector priorities. The session description focused on customer experience, risk management, product design, operational efficiency, explainability, human–AI collaboration and governance. This is a useful indicator of where industry leadership attention is moving, although it is not evidence of widespread production deployment by itself. ([fsc.org.nz](https://www.fsc.org.nz/wednesday-12-august-fsc-conference-2026-programme))

### 3. Customer-facing privacy disclosures are catching up with internal AI use

Westpac’s privacy policy, current from April 30, 2026, now states that automated tools—including AI and machine learning—may assist with:

- Fraud, scam and financial-crime detection
- Risk management and system security
- Customer service and digital assistance
- Product, service and customer-experience analysis

The policy also commits to fairness, accuracy, notice, safeguards, internal governance and human oversight. This is an important maturity signal: AI governance is increasingly being expressed through customer disclosures and privacy frameworks, not only internal policy documents. ([westpac.co.nz](https://www.westpac.co.nz/about-us/legal-information-privacy/privacy-policy/?utm_source=openai))

### 4. Agentic commerce has gained a credible New Zealand pathway

Mastercard completed New Zealand’s first authenticated agentic transactions using a Westpac-issued debit card, including cinema-ticket and accommodation purchases. Visa separately announced that ANZ NZ, ASB, BNZ and Kiwibank had joined its Agentic Ready programme to test agent-initiated payments using tokens, identity, risk controls and consumer consent. ([westpac.co.nz](https://www.westpac.co.nz/about-us/media/mastercard-completes-nzs-first-authenticated-agentic-transactions-with-westpac/))

These developments do not mean autonomous purchasing is already mainstream. They show that New Zealand’s major banks and payment networks are preparing the authentication, liability and control mechanisms that would be required if AI agents begin acting on behalf of consumers at scale.

### 5. There has been more ecosystem progress than public evidence of autonomous finance

The period since July 13 has not produced clear public evidence of widespread autonomous AI lending, underwriting, investment advice or claims settlement. Instead, the visible pattern remains:

- AI copilots for employees
- AI-assisted fraud and scam defence
- Automated transcription and summarisation
- Data and payment infrastructure
- Controlled agentic-payment experiments
- Governance and regulatory preparation

This assessment is based on the public deployments and regulatory material currently available. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/assets/Reports/Access-to-advice-Financial-advice-sector-challenges-and-opportunities.pdf?utm_source=openai))

## Current State of AI Adoption

### Banking

Banking remains the most visible area of AI deployment, but public examples are concentrated in operational assistance rather than autonomous financial decisions.

Westpac began deploying Microsoft Dynamics 365 Contact Centre as a Service in April 2026. The AI-enabled platform provides frontline staff with relevant customer and product information during conversations. Westpac’s accompanying survey found that 65% of respondents were comfortable or neutral about AI supporting contact-centre staff, while 70% were comfortable or neutral about AI being used to detect fraud and scams. ([westpac.co.nz](https://www.westpac.co.nz/about-us/media/westpac-nz-becomes-first-major-nz-company-to-launch-microsoft-ai-tool-to-support-human-to-human-customer-conversations/?utm_source=openai))

Westpac has also created a dedicated **Chief Data, Digital and AI Officer** role. Russell Jones was appointed to the position in June 2026, with responsibility for the bank’s data, digital and AI strategy. This represents a shift toward named executive accountability for AI. ([westpac.co.nz](https://www.westpac.co.nz/about-us/executive-team/))

### Fraud, scams and cyber defence

Defensive AI remains one of the strongest areas of practical adoption.

ANZ NZ reported that a cross-industry initiative involving banks, telcos, digital platforms and a New Zealand technology company had disrupted more than 5,000 phishing domains over two months. ANZ also reported a 39% reduction in card-phishing cases involving its customers during the period. The bank linked the initiative to the increasing sophistication and scale of AI-generated scams. ([anz.com.au](https://www.anz.com.au/newsroom/new-zealand/2026/01/Banks-and-telcos-use-kiwi-tech-to-block-phishing-websites/?utm_source=openai))

The use of AI by scammers is also shaping customer behaviour. BNZ research found that 62% of New Zealanders were checking communications and links more carefully, 55% were limiting what they shared online and 50% were fact-checking news to reduce exposure to fake-news scams. ([bnz.co.nz](https://www.bnz.co.nz/about-us/news/ai-scam-fears-drive-shift-in-new-zealanders-online-behaviour?utm_source=openai))

The sector is therefore experiencing a continuing arms race:

- Financial institutions are using AI to identify abnormal behaviour and malicious infrastructure.
- Criminals are using AI to produce more convincing phishing, impersonation and deepfake content.
- Customers are being asked to make more independent verification checks.
- Banks and regulators are placing greater emphasis on cross-industry collaboration.

### Insurance

Insurance is showing some of the clearest public evidence of measurable AI-enabled service improvements.

Tower reports that its AI-enabled contact centre:

- Saved customers more than **796,000 minutes** over seven months
- Reduced customer interaction time by approximately **15%**
- Reduced average handling time by **2 minutes and 38 seconds**
- Applied real-time transcription across sales, service and claims calls

The system supports staff by capturing customer context and reducing the need for manual note-taking. ([tower.co.nz](https://www.tower.co.nz/news/contact-centre-cuts-call-time/?utm_source=openai))

Insurance brokers and advisers are also beginning to treat AI as a source of new liability and governance exposure. Rothbury’s July 2026 market update identified operational, privacy, legal, reputational and governance risks arising from AI adoption, with implications for cyber, professional indemnity, directors’ and officers’ liability and other insurance lines. ([rothbury.co.nz](https://www.rothbury.co.nz/resources-support/client-newsletters/market-update-commercial-2026/))

### Financial advice

The FMA’s March 2026 access-to-advice review remains the strongest current public evidence on AI in advice.

The FMA observed that AI is mainly being used to:

- Support adviser analysis
- Improve scalability and consistency
- Summarise client information
- Assist with compliance checks
- Prepare documentation
- Develop digital and hybrid advice models

The regulator also identified examples of AI agents, client-analysis tools and proactive financial-wellbeing services. However, the predominant model remains adviser-led, with human accountability retained. The FMA is planning a thematic review of AI use in financial advice. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/assets/Reports/Access-to-advice-Financial-advice-sector-challenges-and-opportunities.pdf?utm_source=openai))

Consumer trust remains conditional:

- Net trust in AI-provided advice ranged from **28% to 41%**, depending on the product.
- **82%** preferred face-to-face interaction with an adviser.
- Only **3%** preferred AI as the primary interaction method.

This suggests that AI is more likely to gain acceptance first as an adviser-support tool than as a standalone replacement for professional advice. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/assets/Reports/Access-to-advice-Consumer-research-findings.pdf?utm_source=openai))

### Payments, open banking and fintech

Open banking is becoming the infrastructure on which more advanced financial applications can be built.

API Centre reporting shows:

- ANZ, ASB, BNZ and Westpac were ready for payment-initiation and account-information APIs.
- Kiwibank became ready for payment and account-information services on **May 28, 2026**.
- Kiwibank implemented version 3.0 of the API standard and included eligible retail and business accounts.
- All four major banks had entered partnerships with at least one third party on the common payment standard. ([apicentre.paymentsnz.co.nz](https://www.apicentre.paymentsnz.co.nz/standards/implementation/implementation-reporting/))

Open banking itself is not AI, but it creates the consented data and payment-access layer needed for:

- Personalised cash-flow services
- Automated budgeting and financial coaching
- Embedded lending
- Intelligent treasury and reconciliation tools
- Agentic purchasing and payment initiation
- Cross-provider financial comparison

The strategic implication is that **AI value in finance will increasingly depend on the quality, consent and interoperability of underlying data and payment infrastructure**.

## Research and Policy Overview

### Financial Markets Authority

The FMA’s current direction combines innovation support with closer scrutiny.

Key points include:

- AI may improve access to advice through scalable and hybrid models.
- Human oversight and conduct obligations remain essential.
- Consumer trust varies by product complexity.
- The FMA plans a thematic review of AI in financial advice.
- Digital advice providers must still meet existing suitability, disclosure and fair-treatment expectations. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/assets/Reports/Access-to-advice-Financial-advice-sector-challenges-and-opportunities.pdf?utm_source=openai))

### Reserve Bank of New Zealand

RBNZ’s May 2026 Financial Stability Report continues to treat AI as both a potential productivity tool and a source of systemic risk.

The Bank identified risks involving:

- Concentration among a small number of AI and cloud providers
- Biased, misleading or fraudulent model outputs
- Increased cyber risk
- Operational-resilience failures
- Possible job losses affecting borrowers’ ability to service debt
- AI-amplified deposit runs and confidence shocks

RBNZ also noted that AI-related investment and asset-price expectations could affect global financial conditions. Its next Financial Stability Report is scheduled for **November 11, 2026**. ([rbnz.govt.nz](https://www.rbnz.govt.nz/financial-stability/financial-stability-report/financial-stability-reports/2026/may/financial-stability-report-may-2026/web-version?utm_source=openai))

A February 2026 RBNZ analytical note found that approximately 30% of New Zealand workers face high combined exposure to AI and robotics, with AI exposure particularly associated with professional, managerial and administrative occupations. This is relevant to financial institutions because many banking, insurance, compliance, operations and analyst roles fall within these categories. ([rbnz.govt.nz](https://www.rbnz.govt.nz/hub/publications/analytical-note/2026/assessing-ai-and-robotics-exposure-in-the-nz-labour-market-using-llms))

### Government data collection

Stats NZ’s new Survey of Business Operations will collect information on AI adoption from approximately 20,000 businesses with six or more employees. The survey is intended to provide the first large, representative national picture of where AI adoption is occurring and where barriers remain. Sector-specific results were not yet available as of August 18, 2026. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/business-ai-use-covered-new-national-survey))

## Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Westpac’s AI-supported contact centre

**Deployment:** Microsoft Dynamics 365 Contact Centre as a Service.

**Application:** Real-time support for customer-service representatives, including relevant customer and product information.

**Reported outcomes and signals:**

- Faster access to information for frontline staff
- Reduced administrative burden
- 65% of surveyed customers comfortable or neutral about AI supporting contact-centre employees
- Dedicated executive ownership through the Chief Data, Digital and AI Officer role

**Assessment:** Westpac illustrates the dominant New Zealand model: AI is introduced into a regulated customer interaction, but the employee remains responsible for the conversation and outcome. ([westpac.co.nz](https://www.westpac.co.nz/about-us/media/westpac-nz-becomes-first-major-nz-company-to-launch-microsoft-ai-tool-to-support-human-to-human-customer-conversations/?utm_source=openai))

### Case Study 2: Tower’s AI-enabled contact centre

**Deployment:** Amazon Connect-based cloud contact centre.

**Application:** Real-time transcription and AI-assisted support across sales, service and claims.

**Reported outcomes:**

- 796,000-plus minutes saved for customers
- Approximately 15% reduction in interaction time
- Average handling time reduced by 2 minutes and 38 seconds

**Assessment:** Tower provides one of the clearest public examples of AI producing measurable operational and customer-service gains inside a New Zealand insurance workflow. ([tower.co.nz](https://www.tower.co.nz/news/contact-centre-cuts-call-time/?utm_source=openai))

### Case Study 3: NZHL’s generative-AI adviser workflow

**Deployment:** Deloitte and Snowflake-supported generative AI solution.

**Application:** Analysis of client data and preparation of annual client-review reports.

**Reported outcome:** Preparation time was reduced by 80%.

**Assessment:** The case demonstrates the value of combining governed data foundations with generative AI. However, because the evidence is published by the implementation partners, the result should be treated as a directional case study rather than independently verified sector-wide evidence. ([deloitte.com](https://www.deloitte.com/nz/en/services/consulting/case-studies/empowering-advisers-through-data-and-generative-ai.html?utm_source=openai))

### Case Study 4: Authenticated agentic payments

**Deployment:** Mastercard Agent Pay transactions using a Westpac-issued debit card.

**Application:** AI agents searched for and completed purchases, with the agent visible and authenticated in the payment flow.

**Assessment:** This is an early infrastructure and trust experiment rather than a mass-market product. Its significance lies in demonstrating how future agentic payments may require explicit consent, agent identity, transaction visibility and network-level fraud controls. ([westpac.co.nz](https://www.westpac.co.nz/about-us/media/mastercard-completes-nzs-first-authenticated-agentic-transactions-with-westpac/))

### Case Study 5: ASB’s AI capability programme for businesses

**Deployment:** ASB’s Pathway to Productivity programme, including an AI bootcamp developed with Xero and delivered by academyEX.

**Target:** More than 4,100 New Zealand businesses, including approximately 4,000 small and medium-sized businesses in the AI bootcamp.

**Application:** Practical implementation of AI agents, workflow automation, market analysis, standard operating procedures and business insight tools.

**Assessment:** This is not simply an internal bank deployment. It positions a major bank as an enabler of AI adoption across its business customers, potentially increasing demand for AI-enabled accounting, payments, lending and cash-flow services. ([asb.co.nz](https://www.asb.co.nz/documents/media-centre/media-releases/asb-backs-nz-businesses-to-lift-productivity-with-new-programme.html))

## Key Trends

### 1. Copilots are scaling before autonomous decision engines

The most mature deployments assist employees with information retrieval, transcription, summarisation, fraud detection and workflow management. There is little public evidence that New Zealand institutions are broadly delegating final lending, claims, investment or advice decisions to autonomous systems. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/assets/Reports/Access-to-advice-Financial-advice-sector-challenges-and-opportunities.pdf?utm_source=openai))

### 2. Defensive AI remains the leading use case

Fraud, phishing, scams, financial crime and cyber defence have the clearest risk-adjusted justification. They also generate relatively direct performance metrics, such as disrupted domains, reduced phishing cases and faster detection. ([anz.com.au](https://www.anz.com.au/newsroom/new-zealand/2026/01/Banks-and-telcos-use-kiwi-tech-to-block-phishing-websites/?utm_source=openai))

### 3. Open banking is becoming the sector’s AI substrate

The next wave of financial AI will depend less on isolated language-model demonstrations and more on access to accurate, consented and timely financial data. New Zealand’s regulated open-banking infrastructure is increasingly capable of supporting this transition. ([apicentre.paymentsnz.co.nz](https://www.apicentre.paymentsnz.co.nz/standards/implementation/implementation-reporting/))

### 4. Trust and transparency are becoming product features

Consumers are not rejecting AI outright, but they want clear boundaries. Visible human oversight, authenticated agents, privacy disclosures and explainable controls are becoming part of the product experience rather than merely compliance documentation. ([fma.govt.nz](https://www.fma.govt.nz/assets/Reports/Access-to-advice-Consumer-research-findings.pdf?utm_source=openai))

### 5. AI governance is moving into executive and regulatory structures

Westpac’s dedicated AI leadership role, the FMA’s planned thematic review and RBNZ’s prudential focus all suggest that AI governance is becoming part of core operating architecture. ([westpac.co.nz](https://www.westpac.co.nz/about-us/executive-team/?utm_source=openai))

### 6. The skills and workforce question is becoming more material

AI exposure is concentrated in many professional and administrative occupations, including roles common across financial institutions. The likely near-term effect is not simply job elimination, but redesign of analyst, operations, compliance, customer-service and advice workflows. ([rbnz.govt.nz](https://www.rbnz.govt.nz/hub/publications/analytical-note/2026/assessing-ai-and-robotics-exposure-in-the-nz-labour-market-using-llms))

## Risks and Constraints

The principal constraints on adoption remain:

- **Privacy and data governance:** Financial data is highly sensitive, and AI use must be compatible with the Privacy Act, customer consent and fair-use expectations. ([westpac.co.nz](https://www.westpac.co.nz/about-us/legal-information-privacy/privacy-policy/?utm_source=openai))
- **Model reliability:** Incorrect, biased or fabricated outputs may create conduct, credit, claims or advice risks. ([rbnz.govt.nz](https://www.rbnz.govt.nz/financial-stability/financial-stability-report/financial-stability-reports/2026/may/financial-stability-report-may-2026/web-version?utm_source=openai))
- **Third-party concentration:** Dependence on a small group of cloud, model and platform providers may create common points of failure. ([rbnz.govt.nz](https://www.rbnz.govt.nz/financial-stability/financial-stability-report/financial-stability-reports/2026/may/financial-stability-report-may-2026/web-version?utm_source=openai))
- **Cyber and scam escalation:** AI gives both institutions and criminals greater speed, scale and sophistication. ([anz.com.au](https://www.anz.com.au/newsroom/new-zealand/2026/01/Banks-and-telcos-use-kiwi-tech-to-block-phishing-websites/?utm_source=openai))
- **Consumer exclusion:** Digital and AI-led models may improve access for some consumers while disadvantaging people with low digital confidence, limited connectivity or complex needs.
- **Māori data governance and inclusion:** MBIE’s open-banking work has specifically considered impacts on Māori customers and the alignment of data-sharing systems with te ao Māori approaches to data. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/have-your-say/consultation-on-exposure-draft-amendment-open-banking-regulations-relating-to-business-banking-digital-channels?utm_source=openai))
- **Evidence quality:** Many public AI performance claims are vendor- or institution-published, making independent evaluation and comparable sector metrics difficult.

## Overall Assessment

As of **August 18, 2026**, AI in New Zealand finance is **operationally established in targeted domains but not yet broadly autonomous**.

The sector’s current pattern is best described as:

> **AI as copilot, detector, summariser, workflow engine and governed payment participant—not yet as an unsupervised financial decision-maker.**

The strongest evidence of value comes from fraud prevention, customer-service augmentation, insurance contact centres and adviser workflow automation. The most important infrastructure development is the maturation of open banking, which provides a foundation for more intelligent, consented and embedded financial services.

The next phase will likely be defined by the interaction of four systems:

1. **AI models and agents**
2. **Open-banking and real-time payment infrastructure**
3. **Fraud, identity and cyber controls**
4. **Human-centred governance and accountability**

New Zealand’s finance sector is therefore progressing steadily, but cautiously. The competitive advantage will not come simply from deploying a model. It will come from combining AI with trusted data, clear customer consent, resilient infrastructure, measurable outcomes and credible human oversight.
