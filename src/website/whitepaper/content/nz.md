# State of AI in New Zealand

August brought the clearest evidence yet of AI moving from pilots into New Zealand’s public-service machinery, while NZIAT launched and compute plans widened. Adoption is broadening, but the flagship AI research-platform decision and large-scale commercial uptake remain unresolved.

## Executive Summary

The national picture changed materially in August. The strongest new evidence is no longer a collection of individual pilots or guidance documents, but a Government survey showing **545 AI use cases across 59 public-sector organisations**. That is double the number reported in 2025, while 167 use cases had reached operational phases — three times the previous year’s figure. The data is self-reported and does not prove equal levels of impact, but it confirms that AI activity across government is broader and more mature than the previous public record suggested. ([2026 cross-agency survey](https://www.digital.govt.nz/dmsdocument/264~report-2026-cross-agency-survey-for-artificial-intelligence-ai-use-cases)) ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/dmsdocument/264~report-2026-cross-agency-survey-for-artificial-intelligence-ai-use-cases/html))

The second major development was the **21 August launch of the New Zealand Institute for Advanced Technology (NZIAT)**, the country’s fourth Public Research Organisation. NZIAT is intended to accelerate development and commercialisation in AI, quantum technologies, and advanced materials, giving the Government’s advanced-technology programme a more visible institutional home. Its launch follows the appointment of a permanent chair on **18 August**. ([NZIAT launch](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-zealand-institute-advanced-technology-launches-auckland)) ([permanent chair appointment](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/permanent-chair-appointed-new-zealand-institute-advanced-technology)) ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-zealand-institute-advanced-technology-launches-auckland))

Infrastructure momentum also broadened. Alongside Datagrid’s Southland project and Mercury’s investment, Contact Energy and CDC Data Centres announced plans to explore a **250MW data centre at Stratford, Taranaki**. The project remains subject to consenting, commercial work, anchor tenants, and financing, but it shows that large-scale AI and sovereign-compute infrastructure is becoming a strategic issue for New Zealand’s energy companies as well as technology developers. ([CDC and Contact announcement](https://cdc.com/resources/news/cdc-and-contact-energy-to-explore-data-centre-stratford-taranaki/)) ([Mercury’s Datagrid announcement](https://www.nzx.com/announcements/476540)) ([cdc.com](https://cdc.com/resources/news/cdc-and-contact-energy-to-explore-data-centre-stratford-taranaki/))

The broader conclusion is **broadening momentum, especially in government, infrastructure, education, and specialised startups — but still selective rather than economy-wide**. Private-sector adoption is producing credible examples, including finance automation, tourism discovery, agricultural robotics, and autonomous aviation certification. However, there is still little public evidence of widespread AI deployment across large New Zealand enterprises, and the national AI Research Platform has still not been publicly awarded.

## What Happened in the Past Month

### Public-sector AI moved from guidance to measurable adoption

The most important development was the Government Digital Delivery Agency’s **19 August 2026** survey of AI use across government.

The survey covered **59 organisations**, including 42 Public Service agencies and 17 wider public-sector organisations. They reported **545 use cases**, compared with 272 from 70 organisations in 2025. More than half of participating organisations reported six or more use cases, with the highest volumes in the social, justice, central-agency, health, and finance sectors.

The more meaningful indicator is maturity. The survey recorded **167 use cases in operational phases**, three times the 2025 figure. Generative AI was the most common technology, followed by natural-language processing, agentic AI, and machine learning. Common applications included administration, digital and technology functions, communications, policy, project management, and corporate and human-resources work. More than half of reported use cases directly or indirectly supported public-facing services. ([2026 cross-agency survey](https://www.digital.govt.nz/dmsdocument/264~report-2026-cross-agency-survey-for-artificial-intelligence-ai-use-cases)) ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/dmsdocument/264~report-2026-cross-agency-survey-for-artificial-intelligence-ai-use-cases/html))

This changes the interpretation of the public-sector story. The previous snapshots identified guidance, toolkits, and individual service pilots as the main signals. The new survey suggests those measures are now translating into a distributed portfolio of operational activity. The immediate opportunity is reuse: the Government says it will identify promising use cases that can be shared or scaled across agencies.

The constraints have not disappeared. Staff capability, cost, security, privacy, public acceptance, reliability, and data sovereignty remain recurring barriers. The survey therefore points to growing institutional capacity, not a solved deployment problem.

### NZIAT gave the advanced-technology programme a physical home

NZIAT’s launch in Auckland on **21 August** was the month’s most important research and innovation development. The institute has been established to connect researchers, businesses, investors, and commercialisation pathways around advanced technologies, including AI. It is based at GridAKL, placing it alongside Auckland’s startup and investment ecosystem. ([NZIAT launch](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-zealand-institute-advanced-technology-launches-auckland)) ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-zealand-institute-advanced-technology-launches-auckland))

The launch follows the appointment of Steve O’Connor as permanent chair on **18 August**, with Marko Bogoievski also joining the board. The Government says NZIAT has already committed **$71 million over seven years** to a future magnetic and materials technologies platform and is progressing work on AI and quantum technologies. ([permanent chair appointment](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/permanent-chair-appointed-new-zealand-institute-advanced-technology)) ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/permanent-chair-appointed-new-zealand-institute-advanced-technology))

This is a significant institutional step, but it is not the same as resolving the AI Research Platform contest. MBIE’s public material still describes the AI platform as a closed contest for up to **$70 million over seven years**, with five concepts previously selected for detailed proposals. The page does not yet identify a winning platform or confirm that contracts have been signed. ([AI Research Platform](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/refocusing-the-science-innovation-and-technology-system/public-research-organisations/new-zealand-institute-for-advanced-technology/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform)) ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/refocusing-the-science-innovation-and-technology-system/public-research-organisations/new-zealand-institute-for-advanced-technology/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform?utm_source=openai))

The distinction matters. New Zealand now has the institutional architecture for advanced-technology investment, but the central AI research-and-commercialisation commitment remains publicly incomplete.

### The data-centre debate became a national infrastructure question

On **10 August**, CDC Data Centres and Contact Energy announced that they were exploring a potential **250MW data centre near Contact’s former Taranaki Combined Cycle power station in Stratford**. The site offers an existing grid connection, fibre connectivity to Auckland and international cable landings, and access to Contact’s renewable-generation pipeline. CDC said the facility could support sovereign workloads across government, health, emergency services, research, finance, and communications. Neither company has committed to construction. ([CDC and Contact announcement](https://cdc.com/resources/news/cdc-and-contact-energy-to-explore-data-centre-stratford-taranaki/)) ([cdc.com](https://cdc.com/resources/news/cdc-and-contact-energy-to-explore-data-centre-stratford-taranaki/))

The proposal broadens the infrastructure picture established by Datagrid’s Southland project. Datagrid has already secured consent for its Makarewa project and attracted a **NZ$53 million investment from Mercury**, while the Stratford proposal would reuse a site with existing energy and network infrastructure. The two projects represent different models: a new large-scale campus requiring substantial grid development, and a possible data centre colocated with an existing energy site. ([Mercury’s Datagrid announcement](https://www.nzx.com/announcements/476540)) ([nzx.com](https://www.nzx.com/announcements/476540?utm_source=openai))

The activity is also generating organised opposition. Protesters gathered during the August data-centre industry summit, with concerns focused on electricity demand, water, planning, transparency, and whether local communities will receive sufficient benefits. The issue is moving beyond whether New Zealand can attract compute investment to whether large AI facilities have an acceptable social and environmental licence. ([data-centre protest coverage](https://95bfm.com/bcast/the-stop-data-centres-nz-protest-in-auckland-18-august-2026)) ([95bfm.com](https://95bfm.com/bcast/the-stop-data-centres-nz-protest-in-auckland-18-august-2026?utm_source=openai))

### Specialist AI companies produced more credible commercial signals

The strongest startup development was **Sterling’s NZ$3.8 million seed round**, announced on **14 August**. Led by Blackbird and supported by New Zealand technology founders and angel investors, the Wellington- and Auckland-based company is building AI workflows for finance teams. Its software is designed to process invoices, reconcile bank feeds, manage month-end routines, log actions, and escalate ambiguous cases to humans. ([Sterling funding announcement](https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/BU2608/S00132/nzs-sterling-lands-38m-to-run-finance-on-autopilot.htm)) ([scoop.co.nz](https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/BU2608/S00132/nzs-sterling-lands-38m-to-run-finance-on-autopilot.htm?utm_source=openai))

Sterling is notable because it is targeting operational work rather than selling a general-purpose chatbot. The funding will support hiring and expansion into larger enterprise customers in New Zealand, Australia, and the United States. It remains an early-stage company, but the deal is a stronger commercial signal than the small pre-seed rounds that dominated earlier snapshots.

There were also sector-specific deployment signals:

- The first **Kilter AX-1 autonomous weeding robot** arrived in New Zealand on **21 August**, with a roadshow planned for growers across both islands. The Norwegian-developed platform uses computer vision and targeted spraying to identify weeds among vegetable crops. It is an imported system rather than a New Zealand-developed AI product, but its arrival provides a concrete example of AI-enabled physical automation entering primary production. ([Rural News coverage](https://ruralnewsgroup.co.nz/rural-news/rural-machinery-products/kilter-ax-1-autonomous-weeding-robot-nz)) ([ruralnewsgroup.co.nz](https://ruralnewsgroup.co.nz/rural-news/rural-machinery-products/kilter-ax-1-autonomous-weeding-robot-nz))
- US-based Merlin Labs reported progress with the Civil Aviation Authority of New Zealand on **6 August**, reaching Stage of Involvement 3 for components of its autonomous-flight system and completing an issue paper covering AI and machine learning used in natural-language processing for aviation communications. The milestone is regulatory rather than commercial, but it shows New Zealand participating in the difficult work of certifying AI-enabled autonomy. ([Merlin milestone](https://merlinlabs.com/news/merlin-passes-milestone-toward-certification-of-ai-enabled-autonomous-flight/)) ([merlinlabs.com](https://merlinlabs.com/news/a-closer-look-at-soi-3/?utm_source=openai))
- Tourism New Zealand received **$800,000** to improve how New Zealand businesses and destinations appear in AI search tools and to expand its AI travel assistant with regional content. This is an early but important response to the possibility that AI systems will increasingly mediate travel discovery. ([MBIE tourism announcement](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/using-ai-to-improve-new-zealand-tourism-visibility)) ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/using-ai-to-improve-new-zealand-tourism-visibility?utm_source=openai))

### Education began building AI capability into the senior-secondary pipeline

On **6 August**, the Government announced nine new industry-led subjects for senior secondary students, including **Applied Intelligent Systems**. The proposed subject is intended to cover low- and no-code technologies, AI-enabled workflows, and autonomous agents, with links to emerging technology and software-sector roles. ([new subjects announcement](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-subjects-bring-industry-and-classroom-closer-together)) ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-subjects-bring-industry-and-classroom-closer-together?utm_source=openai))

Budget 2026 also allocated **$2.1 million** for NZQA to develop pilots and proofs of concept using AI and machine learning in marking, moderation, and examination development. These initiatives are still developmental, but they show AI moving into both the content of education and the machinery of assessment. ([Budget 2026 education investment](https://www.education.govt.nz/our-work/publications/corporate-documents/budget-2026/investing-secondary-achievement)) ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/our-work/publications/corporate-documents/budget-2026/investing-secondary-achievement?utm_source=openai))

The nationwide Aotearoa AI Hackathon Festival, held from **3 to 10 August**, added an ecosystem layer, with events involving students, industry, community groups, and Māori participants. More than 220 people attended two sold-out AUT hackathons alone. ([AI Hackathon Festival](https://aihackathon.nz/)) ([AUT coverage](https://www.publicnow.com/view/8381B71F6D7F20A291F233FB0176437AE1639235)) ([aihackathon.nz](https://aihackathon.nz/?utm_source=openai))

## Trend Line Across Recent Snapshots

The direction across recent editions is now clearer:

- **March:** infrastructure, privacy, data centres, and the mechanics of the AI Research Platform dominated.
- **May and June:** the emphasis shifted to practical adoption support, health procurement, public-service tooling, cyber readiness, and small-business capability.
- **July:** AI became more visible in operational government work, biosecurity, energy infrastructure, startup finance, and ecosystem activity.
- **August:** the centre of gravity moved to **measured public-sector adoption, national research institutions, compute infrastructure, and workforce preparation**.

The momentum is **broadening and becoming more measurable**. The public-sector survey is the strongest evidence yet that activity is not limited to a few flagship pilots. Education is beginning to build a longer-term talent pipeline, while tourism, agriculture, finance, and aviation provide examples of sector-specific adoption.

Infrastructure momentum is also accelerating in visibility, but not necessarily in completed capacity. Datagrid is still under development, the Stratford project is exploratory, and both are exposed to consent, financing, power, community, and commercial risks.

## What Looks Quiet, Unchanged, or Early

The AI Research Platform remains the largest unresolved structural issue. NZIAT’s launch gives the programme a clearer institutional setting, but the Government has still not publicly announced which proposal, if any, will receive the flagship AI investment. The original timetable anticipated a decision in late April or May and contract commencement on **1 July 2026**; those dates have passed without a public award announcement. ([MBIE AI Research Platform](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/refocusing-the-science-innovation-and-technology-system/public-research-organisations/new-zealand-institute-for-advanced-technology/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform)) ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/refocusing-the-science-innovation-and-technology-system/public-research-organisations/new-zealand-institute-for-advanced-technology/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform?utm_source=openai))

Large-enterprise adoption also remains difficult to assess. Sterling is a promising local funding story, and there are credible examples in tourism, energy, finance, agriculture, and aviation, but the public record does not yet show a broad wave of major AI deployments by New Zealand banks, manufacturers, retailers, exporters, or professional-services firms.

Health, which led the March and June snapshots, was comparatively quiet in this period. Existing deployments such as emergency-department scribes and telehealth triage remain important, but no new health announcement matched those earlier milestones.

Regulation also remains incremental. New Zealand has built a substantial framework of public-service guidance and voluntary business guidance, but there is still no comprehensive AI-specific statute or national regulatory regime comparable to the most prescriptive overseas models. The immediate policy focus remains responsible adoption, privacy, security, procurement, and assurance.

## Overall Assessment

August marks a step up in the quality of evidence about AI in New Zealand.

The country is no longer defined only by strategy documents, exploratory pilots, and isolated announcements. Government agencies now report hundreds of use cases, with a growing share operating in practice. NZIAT has launched, specialist startups are attracting capital, schools are preparing AI-related subjects, and energy companies are considering how to support large-scale compute demand.

At the same time, the pattern remains cautious and uneven. Much of the strongest activity involves AI assisting professionals, processing complex information, improving workflow, or enabling narrowly defined physical tasks. The two largest infrastructure projects remain partly speculative, and the social licence debate is intensifying before either project is operational.

The best description is therefore **broadening adoption with emerging institutional momentum, but without national-scale commercial execution**. New Zealand’s AI system is becoming more capable and more connected across government, research, infrastructure, education, and industry. Whether that momentum turns into durable productivity growth will depend heavily on the delayed AI Research Platform, the ability to scale proven public-sector use cases, and whether large infrastructure projects can secure both commercial viability and public legitimacy.
