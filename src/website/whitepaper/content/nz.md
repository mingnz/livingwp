# State of AI in New Zealand

**Updated: 18 August 2026**

## Executive Summary

The past month has brought a more tangible kind of momentum to Aotearoa New Zealand’s AI story. The clearest new signals were the start of physical works on Datagrid’s planned Southland AI data-centre campus, a new industry strategy positioning New Zealand as a trusted and sustainable data-centre and AI hub, and government pilots applying generative AI to biosecurity standards and tourism discovery. At the same time, new business research suggests adoption is spreading faster than organisational readiness: **83 percent of surveyed Kiwi businesses are already using AI, but two-thirds remain in experimentation or pilot mode**. The overall picture is therefore stronger than a quiet policy month, but still better described as **infrastructure and adoption catching up with ambition** than as a national AI breakout. ([Datagrid works](https://www.reseller.co.nz/article/4210204/works-start-on-datagrids-southland-ai-data-centre-campus.html); [DataCentres New Zealand strategy](https://www.reseller.co.nz/article/4209424/industry-led-strategy-calls-for-nz-to-become-trusted-sustainable-data-centre-and-ai-hub.html); [EMA research](https://ema.co.nz/a-people-powered-future-for-kiwi-workplaces/)) ([reseller.co.nz](https://www.reseller.co.nz/article/4210204/works-start-on-datagrids-southland-ai-data-centre-campus.html))

## What Happened in the Past Month

### Datagrid moved from consent to construction

The most significant infrastructure development was the announcement on **18 August** that horizontal works had begun on Datagrid’s planned AI data-centre campus at Makarewa, north of Invercargill. The initial work includes a new access road, upgrades to existing roads, and the construction of a six-metre-high bund to reduce the project’s visual and noise impact.

Datagrid says the 49-hectare site will host a **78,000-square-metre AI factory** with high-density GPU clusters for global AI and cloud providers. The foundation platform for the future data centre, a subsea cable landing station, and a power substation is expected to be completed by the end of 2026. The company has not, however, provided an updated completion date for the previously reported **$3.5 billion** project. ([Reseller News](https://www.reseller.co.nz/article/4210204/works-start-on-datagrids-southland-ai-data-centre-campus.html)) ([reseller.co.nz](https://www.reseller.co.nz/article/4210204/works-start-on-datagrids-southland-ai-data-centre-campus.html))

This is an important change from the March snapshot, when Datagrid’s resource consent and power arrangements were still the main story. The project is now visibly entering implementation. That does not remove questions around cost, energy demand, delivery timing, or whether sufficient customers will materialise, but it does make New Zealand’s AI-infrastructure ambitions more concrete.

### Industry made the case for a national data-centre strategy

On **14 August**, DataCentres New Zealand, a Tech New Zealand industry group, released a strategy calling for the country to become a **trusted and sustainable hub for data-centre and AI infrastructure investment**.

The strategy argues that too much processing for New Zealand businesses and public services currently occurs offshore, and that greater domestic capacity could support productivity, resilience, security, skilled employment, renewable-energy investment, and export growth. It calls for stronger coordination between government and industry, international promotion of New Zealand’s data-centre capabilities, and investment in domestic capacity.

The strategy is an industry position rather than government policy, so it should not be treated as a commitment to new public funding or a settled national plan. Its significance is that data centres and AI compute are increasingly being framed as **essential national infrastructure**, not merely as private-sector technology projects. ([DataCentres New Zealand strategy](https://www.reseller.co.nz/article/4209424/industry-led-strategy-calls-for-nz-to-become-trusted-sustainable-data-centre-and-ai-hub.html)) ([reseller.co.nz](https://www.reseller.co.nz/article/4209424/industry-led-strategy-calls-for-nz-to-become-trusted-sustainable-data-centre-and-ai-hub.html))

### Biosecurity became a new government AI use case

On **25 July**, the Government announced a four-month pilot of a generative-AI tool to help develop **import health standards**. These standards govern the biosecurity conditions attached to plant, animal, and biological imports.

The proposed tool is intended to assist with document-heavy, evidence-based work while leaving key decisions with Biosecurity New Zealand experts. The Government says the aim is to make the standards process faster and more consistent without weakening biosecurity protections. ([Beehive](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-ai-tool-help-develop-biosecurity-standards)) ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-ai-tool-help-develop-biosecurity-standards))

This is a useful example of the current New Zealand deployment pattern: AI is being placed inside a defined administrative bottleneck, with human experts retaining responsibility for consequential decisions. It also extends the adoption story beyond health, business support, and central-government productivity into **primary industries and border protection**.

### Tourism New Zealand began preparing for AI-mediated travel discovery

On **29 July**, the Government announced **$800,000 from the International Visitor Conservation and Tourism Levy** for an upgrade of Tourism New Zealand’s systems so local tourism products are easier for AI search tools to find and use.

The work includes making Tourism New Zealand’s database more “AI-ready” by improving how information about thousands of tourism and hospitality businesses is structured. It will also expand Tourism New Zealand’s AI travel assistant with more detailed regional information, personalised recommendations, and booking links. ([Beehive](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-search-harnessed-back-tourism-growth)) ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-search-harnessed-back-tourism-growth))

This is a different kind of AI investment. Rather than building a model, New Zealand is adapting its public information infrastructure to a world in which travellers increasingly discover destinations through AI-generated answers. The immediate issue is not automation but **visibility, data quality, and commercial discoverability**.

### Business adoption is spreading, but readiness is lagging

Research released by the Employers and Manufacturers Association in July provides one of the clearest recent indicators of private-sector adoption. In a survey of more than 200 New Zealand businesses, **83 percent said they were already using AI**, while a further 10 percent were planning to adopt it.

The more important finding is the gap between use and integration. EMA reported that **two-thirds of AI-using businesses remained in experimentation or pilot stages**, with only one-third embedding AI into regular business processes. One in five businesses had no clear internal owner for AI, while governance and workforce capability remained uneven. ([EMA](https://ema.co.nz/a-people-powered-future-for-kiwi-workplaces/)) ([ema.co.nz](https://ema.co.nz/a-people-powered-future-for-kiwi-workplaces/))

That evidence complicates the optimistic reading of high adoption. New Zealand businesses may be using AI widely, but much of that use still appears informal, localised, or experimental. The next national challenge is therefore less about persuading firms to try AI and more about helping them redesign work, manage risk, train staff, and measure whether the technology is producing durable value.

### AI security and implementation capability became more visible

The private-sector response is also shifting from general consulting to specialised AI governance and security. On **24 July**, TrendAI appointed former Foodstuffs South Island CISO Richard Harrison as its global vice-president of AI security, based in Auckland. The company said the role would focus on helping organisations manage AI deployments while reducing governance gaps, operational complexity, and security exposure. ([Reseller News](https://www.reseller.co.nz/article/4200943/trendai-picks-foodstuffs-ciso-richard-harrison-as-global-ai-security-vp.html)) ([reseller.co.nz](https://www.reseller.co.nz/article/4200943/trendai-picks-foodstuffs-ciso-richard-harrison-as-global-ai-security-vp.html))

New Zealand IT services firm Equinox IT also changed its leadership structure in August, creating a chief client officer role partly to help clients turn AI adoption into measurable business outcomes supported by architecture, delivery, and governance. ([Reseller News](https://www.reseller.co.nz/article/4208851/equinox-it-changes-leadership-structure-to-help-client-ai-adoption.html)) ([reseller.co.nz](https://www.reseller.co.nz/article/4208851/equinox-it-changes-leadership-structure-to-help-client-ai-adoption.html))

These are not national-scale AI launches, but they are evidence of a maturing services market. More organisations are moving from asking whether they should use AI to asking how to secure, govern, integrate, and operationalise it.

### The ecosystem continued to build capability

The **Aotearoa AI Hackathon Festival** ran nationwide from **3 to 10 August**, bringing together developers, students, designers, and domain experts to work on practical challenges including food insecurity, digital accessibility, workforce upskilling, cross-border collaboration, and indigenous environmental custodianship. Finalists are due to pitch at the Aotearoa AI Summit in September. ([AI Forum events](https://aiforum.org.nz/events/month/2026-08/); [Aotearoa AI Hackathon](https://aihackathon.nz/)) ([aiforum.org.nz](https://aiforum.org.nz/events/month/2026-08/))

The festival is best read as ecosystem infrastructure rather than evidence of commercial scale. It shows that AI activity is broadening geographically and socially, but it does not yet demonstrate that prototypes are routinely becoming funded products, research programmes, or export companies.

## Trend Line Across Recent Snapshots

The month-on-month pattern is becoming clearer:

- **March:** attention shifted toward compute infrastructure, privacy, cyber risk, and the mechanics of the national research platform.
- **May to early June:** activity broadened into practical adoption, including small-business support, public-service tooling, and health-related AI.
- **June to July:** AI became more institutionalised through science investment, telehealth, cyber preparedness, and government-backed implementation support.
- **July to August:** the infrastructure story became more tangible, while AI adoption spread into biosecurity, tourism, workplace transformation, and commercial AI security.

The current month therefore looks like a combination of **physical build-out and operational normalisation**. Datagrid’s construction start is the strongest new infrastructure signal since the March snapshot, while the biosecurity and tourism initiatives show government agencies treating AI as a tool for specific workflow and information problems.

Momentum is broadening across sectors, but it is not accelerating uniformly. The strongest areas are now:

1. **AI and data-centre infrastructure**
2. **Public-sector workflow pilots**
3. **SME adoption and capability-building**
4. **AI security and governance services**
5. **Health, tourism, biosecurity, and primary-industry applications**

What remains weaker is the conversion of that activity into globally scaled New Zealand AI companies, large local research breakthroughs, or a visibly stronger venture-capital pipeline. The month did produce notable company activity, including Wellumio’s **$9.75 million oversubscribed pre-Series A** for AI-enhanced portable stroke-imaging technology, but the broader pattern remains selective rather than investment-led. ([Wellumio announcement](https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/BU2607/S00343/wellumio-closes-oversubscribed-975-million-pre-series-a-round-to-accelerate-portable-stroke-imaging-technology.htm)) ([scoop.co.nz](https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/BU2607/S00343/wellumio-closes-oversubscribed-975-million-pre-series-a-round-to-accelerate-portable-stroke-imaging-technology.htm?utm_source=openai))

## What Looks Quiet, Unchanged, or Early

### The national AI Research Platform remains unresolved publicly

The biggest outstanding structural question is still the **AI Research Platform backed by up to $70 million over seven years**. MBIE’s public material continues to describe the five shortlisted concepts, the March phase-two deadline, and the intended July 2026 contract start, but it does not publicly identify a final platform or funding decision.

That is now more than a timetable issue. The platform was intended to provide a centre of gravity for New Zealand AI research, connect research to commercialisation, and anchor a long-term national capability. The longer the public decision remains unresolved, the longer New Zealand’s largest dedicated AI research investment remains difficult for universities, firms, investors, and international partners to plan around. ([MBIE AI Research Platform](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/refocusing-the-science-innovation-and-technology-system/public-research-organisations/new-zealand-institute-for-advanced-technology/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform); [MBIE concept announcement](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/concepts-selected-for-ai-research-platform)) ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/refocusing-the-science-innovation-and-technology-system/public-research-organisations/new-zealand-institute-for-advanced-technology/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform?utm_source=openai))

### Large-scale commercial adoption remains hard to verify

The EMA survey indicates substantial AI use, but much of it remains experimental. Public evidence of major enterprise-wide deployments, measurable productivity gains, or broad workforce redesign is still limited.

That means New Zealand’s adoption rate should be interpreted carefully. There is a meaningful difference between employees using general-purpose AI tools, businesses running pilots, and organisations embedding AI into core systems with strong data, security, accountability, and evaluation practices.

### Governance is developing through guidance and practice rather than comprehensive legislation

The past month added more examples of bounded, human-supervised deployment, especially in biosecurity and tourism. However, there was no major new cross-economy AI regulatory framework or AI-specific health legislation announced during the period reviewed.

The country’s governance model therefore remains incremental: privacy guidance, sector-specific rules, cyber preparedness, targeted harm legislation, and agency-level assurance. That may be proportionate to New Zealand’s size and institutional capacity, but it also leaves important questions about accountability, Māori and Pacific data interests, procurement, transparency, and high-impact automated decision-making to be worked through progressively.

### Infrastructure is advancing, but delivery risk remains

Datagrid’s construction start is a genuine milestone, but it is still a project milestone rather than proof of operational AI capacity. The company has not published an updated completion date for the $3.5 billion campus, and the wider industry strategy remains an advocacy document.

The next meaningful test will be whether New Zealand can turn consented and announced infrastructure into functioning capacity with viable power, connectivity, customers, and community legitimacy.

## Overall Assessment

Aotearoa New Zealand’s AI position strengthened over the past month, but in a distinctly practical and uneven way. The country is building the physical foundations for larger-scale AI activity, adapting public information and regulatory workflows to AI-mediated services, and seeing widespread business experimentation. The most important shift is that AI is no longer confined to technology policy: it is now appearing in **biosecurity administration, tourism marketing, workplace planning, security operations, and regional infrastructure development**.

The evidence still does not support calling this a breakout month. New Zealand has not yet publicly resolved its flagship AI Research Platform, and high reported adoption is running ahead of organisational readiness. The more accurate assessment is that momentum is **broadening and becoming more concrete**, while the country remains in the difficult middle phase between experimentation and scaled execution.

The next signal to watch is whether the emerging pieces connect: whether Datagrid and other infrastructure projects translate into usable national compute; whether the AI Research Platform finally moves from contest to operation; and whether the large pool of SME experimentation becomes governed, integrated, productivity-enhancing adoption. For now, New Zealand is making progress on the machinery around AI faster than it is producing headline AI champions.
