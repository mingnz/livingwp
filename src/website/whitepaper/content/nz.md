# State of AI in New Zealand

**Updated: 18 August 2026**

## Executive Summary

Since the previous snapshot on **13 July 2026**, Aotearoa New Zealand’s AI story has split into two clearer tracks. **Operational adoption is broadening**, with new or expanded uses in healthcare rostering, biosecurity standards, tourism discovery, and small-business support. At the same time, **AI infrastructure has become a national political issue**: Mercury took a minority stake in Datagrid’s proposed Southland AI campus, while the Green Party called for a one-year pause on new AI data-centre consents and the Government moved toward sector-wide principles. The result is a month of genuine momentum, but also a sharper question about who benefits from AI investment, who pays for its energy and infrastructure demands, and whether governance is keeping pace. ([mercury.co.nz](https://www.mercury.co.nz/investors/news?utm_source=openai))

The most important unresolved structural issue remains the Government’s **up to $70 million national AI Research Platform**. MBIE’s public page still describes the completed phase-two process but says an announcement timeline will be provided “in due course”; it does not identify a final platform. That leaves New Zealand with visible movement on deployment and compute infrastructure, but without public closure on the country’s largest dedicated AI research and commercialisation bet. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/refocusing-the-science-innovation-and-technology-system/public-research-organisations/new-zealand-institute-for-advanced-technology/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform?utm_source=openai))

## What Happened in the Past Month

### AI infrastructure moved from project news into political contest

The largest commercial development was **Mercury’s investment of US$30 million, or about NZ$53 million, for a 12.7 percent stake in Datagrid Holding Group NZ**. Datagrid is developing a large-scale data-centre campus at North Makarewa in Southland, with a proposed capacity of up to **360MW** in the latest Mercury announcement. Mercury says a final investment decision is expected later this year, while Datagrid says the new partnership allows it to begin horizontal construction works. ([mercury.co.nz](https://www.mercury.co.nz/investors/news?utm_source=openai))

This is more significant than another infrastructure memorandum. It links one of New Zealand’s major renewable electricity generators directly to the proposed AI campus and gives the project a stronger domestic corporate connection. It does not, however, mean that the campus is built, fully financed, or operational. The project remains a major future infrastructure proposition rather than current national compute capacity. ([mercury.co.nz](https://www.mercury.co.nz/investors/news?utm_source=openai))

The project also triggered a new political fault line. The Green Party announced a call for a **one-year pause on consenting new AI data centres**, arguing that New Zealand needs rules covering electricity, water, infrastructure costs, environmental effects, and community benefit before further projects proceed. Prime Minister Christopher Luxon rejected the proposal as alarmist, while the issue was debated in Parliament amid discussion of the electricity use of current and proposed facilities. ([action.greens.org.nz](https://action.greens.org.nz/pause_ai_data_centres?utm_source=openai))

That changes the national conversation. Earlier snapshots treated compute mainly as a capability and investment question. This month it became a question of **public-interest regulation and distributional impact**. Whether Datagrid proceeds on schedule will matter, but so will the Government’s eventual answer to a broader issue: what conditions should apply when large, foreign-linked AI infrastructure competes for scarce electricity, land, water, network capacity, and political attention?

### Government tested generative AI in biosecurity administration

On **25 July**, the Government announced a four-month pilot of a generative-AI tool to help develop **import health standards**. The intended use is document-heavy work: helping staff process evidence and prepare standards more efficiently while leaving key decisions with Biosecurity New Zealand experts. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-ai-tool-help-develop-biosecurity-standards))

This is a useful example of the kind of AI deployment now appearing across the public sector. It is narrow, supervised, and tied to a recognisable administrative bottleneck. It is also materially different from a public-facing chatbot or a speculative AI strategy: the tool is being tested inside an expert workflow where accuracy, traceability, and human accountability matter.

The biosecurity pilot broadens the public-sector picture beyond health and central-government productivity. It suggests that AI adoption is spreading into **regulatory and scientific administration**, particularly where agencies manage large volumes of technical documents and evidence. The pilot remains early, however, and there is not yet public evidence of productivity gains or a decision to scale it nationally. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-ai-tool-help-develop-biosecurity-standards))

### Health adoption broadened from clinical notes to workforce operations

Health remained active, but the most notable new signal was not another headline clinical deployment. In July, Health New Zealand’s **HealthX** innovation programme began evaluating RosterLab’s AI-powered rostering technology across medical radiation services at **Counties Manukau, Waikato, Starship, and Waitematā**. The stated objectives are to improve roster quality, support clinician wellbeing, and reduce administrative workload. ([rosterlab.com](https://rosterlab.com/newsroom/healthnz-and-rosterlab-partner-on-initial-rollout-of-ai-powered-healthcare-rostering-across-nz?utm_source=openai))

This adds an operational layer to the health story. Earlier 2026 developments centred on AI scribes, breast-screening assessment, and mental-health triage. The RosterLab work targets the machinery around care delivery: staffing, scheduling, and service coordination. That is consistent with the broader New Zealand pattern of adopting AI first in bounded workflows where capacity constraints are obvious and human oversight can remain close to the system.

The evidence is still limited to an evaluation or initial rollout. It should therefore be read as a sign of **continued experimentation and procurement interest**, not yet as proof of a large-scale productivity transformation across Health New Zealand. ([rosterlab.com](https://rosterlab.com/newsroom/healthnz-and-rosterlab-partner-on-initial-rollout-of-ai-powered-healthcare-rostering-across-nz?utm_source=openai))

### Tourism began adapting to AI-mediated discovery

On **30 July**, the Government announced **$800,000 from the International Visitor Conservation and Tourism Levy** to make New Zealand tourism businesses and destinations easier to find through AI tools. The investment will support upgrades to Tourism New Zealand’s content and systems, as well as an expanded AI travel assistant incorporating material from Regional Tourism Organisations. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/using-ai-to-improve-new-zealand-tourism-visibility?utm_source=openai))

This is one of the clearest examples yet of a traditional sector responding to the way generative AI is changing customer discovery. The focus is not on building a foundation model. It is on making local businesses, attractions, and regional experiences more legible to AI search and travel-planning systems.

The move also shows how AI is entering economic-development policy through **visibility and intermediation**. Tourism operators are increasingly competing not only for search-engine rankings, but for inclusion in AI-generated recommendations. The Government’s intervention is intended to improve regional dispersal, encourage longer stays, and help smaller operators connect with international visitors. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/using-ai-to-improve-new-zealand-tourism-visibility?utm_source=openai))

### The responsible-AI conversation remained active

On **14 July**, the Tech Users Association of New Zealand released a position paper calling for trust, transparency, human oversight, and investment in domestic AI capability. TUANZ argued that New Zealand’s ability to capture AI’s economic benefits will depend on public confidence and responsible implementation rather than adoption alone. ([scoop.co.nz](https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/SC2607/S00027/unlocking-aotearoas-ai-opportunity-tuanz-calls-for-trust-transparency-and-human-oversight.htm?utm_source=openai))

The paper is not a regulatory change, but it reinforces a theme running through the past several snapshots: New Zealand’s AI debate is increasingly about **conditions for adoption**. The same month saw arguments over data-centre impacts, expert-in-the-loop biosecurity tools, and public-sector health pilots. In each case, the central issue was not whether AI exists, but how it should be introduced and who remains accountable.

Ecosystem activity also continued through the nationwide **Aotearoa AI Hackathon Festival**, held from **3 to 10 August**, and through ongoing preparation for the September Aotearoa AI Summit. Those events point to a live and increasingly distributed community, although they are capability-building and convening signals rather than evidence of a step-change in capital or commercial scale. ([aiforum.org.nz](https://aiforum.org.nz/events/month/2026-08/?utm_source=openai))

## Trend Line Across Recent Snapshots

The recent sequence now looks like this:

- **March and April:** attention shifted toward infrastructure, data centres, privacy, cyber risk, and the mechanics of the national AI Research Platform.
- **May and June:** the centre of gravity moved toward practical adoption, including small-business assistance, public-service tooling, health procurement, and AI readiness.
- **July:** AI became more institutionalised through science investment, telehealth, cyber preparedness, and targeted government support.
- **Mid-July to August:** adoption broadened further, while infrastructure became politically contested.

The current month therefore looks like **acceleration in breadth, but not yet a breakthrough in depth**. More sectors are now testing or funding concrete AI uses: biosecurity, tourism, healthcare operations, small-business support, education and workforce capability. The infrastructure story is also stronger than it was in June, with Mercury’s investment giving Datagrid a more substantial domestic anchor. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-ai-tool-help-develop-biosecurity-standards))

But the country still lacks a public result for the national AI Research Platform. The original programme documents anticipated a funding decision in late April, an announcement in May, and contract commencement on **1 July 2026**. MBIE’s current public page still does not name a final recipient. That gap is increasingly conspicuous because the Government’s wider Science Investment Plan positions advanced technologies, including AI, as a major national investment priority. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/assets/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform.pdf?utm_source=openai))

The emerging pattern is consequently dual-track:

1. **Distributed, bounded deployment** is moving ahead across agencies and sectors.
2. **National-scale capability bets** remain slower, more politically exposed, or unresolved.

That is progress, but it is not yet a fully coherent national AI system.

## What Looks Quiet, Unchanged, or Early

The national AI Research Platform remains the clearest unresolved item. The public record confirms that phase two proposals were submitted and assessed, but still does not show a final investment decision. Until that changes, New Zealand’s flagship AI research-commercialisation programme remains more visible as a selection process than as an operating institution. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/refocusing-the-science-innovation-and-technology-system/public-research-organisations/new-zealand-institute-for-advanced-technology/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform?utm_source=openai))

There is also still limited public evidence of a major new wave of locally headquartered AI venture funding, enterprise-scale deployments, or globally significant product launches during this period. The month’s most material capital signal was infrastructure-related rather than a conventional AI software or research-company funding round. That suggests the ecosystem remains stronger at **public support, applied pilots, research formation, and infrastructure proposals** than at sustained private-sector scale-up.

The data-centre story is also earlier than some of the rhetoric implies. Datagrid has consent, an electricity relationship, and a strategic investor, but its final investment decision and construction programme remain ahead. The proposed campus should not be confused with currently available sovereign AI compute. ([datagrid.nz](https://www.datagrid.nz/pr1-rc?utm_source=openai))

Finally, many public-sector projects remain pilots, evaluations, or work-programme commitments. The Government’s public-service AI programme includes ambitions such as a central AI hub, assurance tools, an AI marketplace, and an AI search assistant, but the public evidence still points to a fragmented system with uneven deployment rather than a fully scaled all-of-government capability. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/digital-government/key-areas-of-work/public-service-artificial-intelligence-ai?utm_source=openai))

## Overall Assessment

The clearest change this month is that AI in Aotearoa New Zealand became **both more practical and more contested**. Practical adoption is spreading into the less glamorous but economically important parts of the system: biosecurity standards, health rostering, tourism information, and business workflows. At the same time, the proposed Southland AI campus has forced a national debate about energy, water, infrastructure, sovereignty, transparency, and who captures the benefits of compute investment. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-ai-tool-help-develop-biosecurity-standards))

Compared with the previous snapshot, momentum is **broadening and becoming more politically consequential**. New Zealand is no longer discussing AI only as a future productivity opportunity or research capability gap. It is now dealing with the practical trade-offs of deployment and infrastructure in real institutions and communities.

The caution is that the country still appears to be moving faster on **pilots, support programmes, and infrastructure proposals** than on **national research-platform execution, commercial scale, and coherent governance**. The next meaningful test will be whether the Government can convert its AI investment architecture into a named, funded research institution while also establishing credible rules for the energy- and resource-intensive infrastructure needed to support it. Until then, New Zealand’s AI trajectory is best described as **active, increasingly broad, and institutionally serious—but still early and uneven**.
