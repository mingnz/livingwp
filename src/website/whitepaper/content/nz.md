# State of AI in New Zealand

**Updated: 13 July 2026**

## Executive Summary

Since the last snapshot on **10 June 2026**, Aotearoa New Zealand’s AI story has looked less like a breakthrough month and more like an **institutionalisation month**. The clearest new signals were government widening AI-enabled small-business support on **11 June**, the release of a **10-year Science Investment Plan** on **10 June** that locks advanced technology and commercialisation more firmly into the science system, a sharper **Five Eyes cyber warning on AI preparedness** on **23 June**, and a new **AI-enabled telehealth triage and referral tool** funded on **13 July**. Together, those moves suggest AI is becoming more embedded in ordinary policy, service-delivery, and risk-management machinery, even if New Zealand still lacks a single headline-grabbing research, infrastructure, or startup-finance announcement this month. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/ai-powered-tools-to-support-small-business-growth))

The other important reading is what **did not** happen. The long-awaited public outcome of the national **AI Research Platform** still does not appear to have been announced on MBIE’s main platform page, which continues to say a timeline update will be made “in due course,” despite earlier programme material pointing to a **May 2026** announcement window and a **1 July 2026** contract start. That absence matters because it leaves New Zealand’s biggest AI research-commercialisation bet still publicly unresolved just as the wider science system is being reset. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/refocusing-the-science-innovation-and-technology-system/public-research-organisations/new-zealand-institute-for-advanced-technology/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform?utm_source=openai))

## What Happened in the Past Month

### Government deepened practical AI adoption support for small business

The most concrete new business-adoption move since the last snapshot was the Government’s **11 June** funding package for two AI-enabled support programmes. MBIE said **Business Mentors New Zealand** would receive **$82,000** to build its **AI Digital Mentor** tool, while **Business South** would receive an additional **$400,000** to expand its **AcceleratorNZ** programme to **500 more businesses** over the next 12 months. The package is modest in fiscal terms, but it is meaningful because it pushes AI support beyond strategy documents and into hands-on diagnostics, mentoring, and workflow improvement for smaller firms. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/ai-powered-tools-to-support-small-business-growth))

This builds directly on the previous snapshot’s story of practical adoption support. The difference now is that the state’s role is becoming more operational: not just encouraging firms to “consider AI,” but funding specific tools and intermediaries to help businesses decide where AI is useful and where it is not. That is a sign of follow-through rather than a one-off pilot announcement. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/ai-powered-tools-to-support-small-business-growth))

### Science policy became more explicit about AI’s place in the national growth agenda

A bigger structural development was the **10 June** release of the Government’s **Science Investment Plan 2026–2036**. The plan says the science system will be organised around four enduring pillars, including **“Technology for Prosperity,”** and frames the reforms as a shift from a fragmented, instrument-led model to a more strategy-led system tied to economic growth and commercial outcomes. MBIE’s accompanying material says advanced-technology research investment is set to increase by **$122 million by 2029/30**, and that **$142 million** is being invested through the **New Zealand Institute for Advanced Technology** in areas such as **AI** and **quantum technologies**. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/dmsdocument/32023-science-investment-plan-2026-2036))

The same day, MBIE also published the **Prime Minister’s Science, Innovation and Technology Advisory Council’s** refined priorities report, which the ministry says fed directly into the Science Investment Plan. In other words, AI is no longer sitting only in a standalone strategy or isolated pilot programmes; it is being written into the country’s longer-horizon science-funding architecture. That is a substantive policy shift, even if it is still more about system design than immediate new AI labs or products. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/refocusing-the-science-innovation-and-technology-system/prime-ministers-science-innovation-and-technology-advisory-council))

### Health stayed the most visible deployment sector

Health remained New Zealand’s clearest area of public-facing AI deployment. On **13 July**, the Government announced a **more than $10 million** mental-health and addiction telehealth package that includes **$3.35 million over four years** to develop and implement an **AI-enabled triage and referral tool** across publicly funded helplines. Ministers said the tool is intended to identify needs more consistently and connect people to the most appropriate service, while the wider package aims to expand sector capacity by about **15 percent**. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/telehealth-investment-deliver-faster-access-support))

That matters because it extends the recent NZ health pattern beyond scribes and screening into **front-door service coordination**. It is still a bounded use case, not autonomous clinical decision-making, but it is another example of AI being inserted into specific pressure points where waiting times, routing, and administrative friction are real operational problems. In New Zealand’s current AI landscape, health continues to be where deployment is most visible and politically legible. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/telehealth-investment-deliver-faster-access-support))

At the same time, the cautionary side of the health story remained visible. At a **24 June** Pacific health research symposium, University of Auckland researcher **Professor Robyn Whittaker** warned that many AI tools used globally are not adequately tested on **Māori and Pacific populations**, and argued New Zealand still lacks AI-specific healthcare legislation. That does not change deployment on its own, but it is an important reminder that equity, validation, and governance are still unresolved even as implementation moves ahead. ([auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/06/29/ai-in-health-scholar-brings-clarity-and-caution-to-pacific-symposium.html?utm_source=openai))

### Cyber readiness and AI harms moved further toward the centre

On **23 June**, New Zealand’s **National Cyber Security Centre** joined its Five Eyes counterparts in a public **Call to Action on AI Preparedness**, warning that frontier AI is already changing the cyber-risk landscape by helping malicious actors identify and exploit vulnerabilities faster and at larger scale. The language was notably immediate: AI was framed not as a future governance problem but as a current operating condition for defenders and business leaders. ([ncsc.govt.nz](https://www.ncsc.govt.nz/news/leaders-of-five-eyes-cyber-security-agencies-call-to-action-on-ai-preparedness/))

That built on the NCSC’s earlier **4 June** New Zealand guidance on **“cyber readiness in the Frontier AI era,”** which had already told government agencies to treat frontier AI as a “dual-use challenge” and a potential “vulnerability storm.” The June Five Eyes intervention shows that this is no longer just a domestic caution note. It is now part of a coordinated allied cyber-security posture, and New Zealand is clearly aligning with that framing. ([ncsc.govt.nz](https://www.ncsc.govt.nz/protect-your-organisation/cyber-readiness-in-the-frontier-ai-era/?utm_source=openai))

AI harms also stayed active on the legislative side. Parliament’s **Deepfake Digital Harm and Exploitation Bill** remained open for submissions until **19 June 2026** and would expand the definition of intimate visual recordings to cover images that are **created, synthesised, or altered** to appear intimate. InternetNZ backed the bill as a useful start but argued the regulatory response still needs to go further. That keeps deepfakes firmly in the country’s live AI policy agenda, even if the bill is targeted at one harm class rather than AI regulation as a whole. ([www3.parliament.nz](https://www3.parliament.nz/en/pb/sc/committees-press-releases/have-your-say-on-the-deepfake-digital-harm-and-exploitation-bill/?utm_source=openai))

### The ecosystem stayed active, but mostly through convening rather than capital

Outside government, the AI ecosystem did not look dormant, but it did look **convening-heavy**. The **AI Forum** opened entries for the **2026 Aotearoa AI Awards** on **11 June**, ran a **Sustainable AI** event in Wellington on **30 June**, and continues to promote the **Aotearoa AI Summit 2026** for September. That is useful evidence of a live national community, but it is still ecosystem choreography more than a step-change in research funding or private investment. ([aiforum.org.nz](https://aiforum.org.nz/2026/06/11/entries-are-open-2026-aotearoa-ai-awards/?utm_source=openai))

Higher education showed a similar pattern. The **University of Otago** is hosting the **2026 New Zealand AI in Higher Education Symposium**, positioning AI in education as a national practice-sharing and capability-building topic. Again, that is a real signal of institutional engagement, but it points to sector learning and coordination rather than a transformative new national programme. ([otago.ac.nz](https://www.otago.ac.nz/news/events/2026-new-zealand-ai-in-higher-education-symposium?utm_source=openai))

## Trend Line Across Recent Snapshots

Compared with the **2 April 2026** snapshot, which was dominated by **infrastructure, data-centre momentum, privacy guidance, and research-platform mechanics**, and the **10 June 2026** snapshot, which looked like a **practical adoption month**, the current period feels more like a month of **system hardening**. AI is being embedded in science-investment logic, telehealth operations, cyber preparedness, and targeted business-support programmes. The centre of gravity has shifted again: from building conditions, to trying bounded use cases, to making those use cases part of ongoing institutions and budgets. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-science-investment-plan-drive-economic-growth-and-prosperity?utm_source=openai))

That is progress, but it is also narrower than a true acceleration story. Momentum is strongest in **public-sector scaffolding, health workflows, cyber risk management, and ecosystem capability-building**. It is weaker in the areas that would most clearly change the national picture overnight: a resolved national AI research-platform winner, a major new sovereign-compute milestone, or a run of standout NZ AI funding rounds and company breakouts. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/refocusing-the-science-innovation-and-technology-system/public-research-organisations/new-zealand-institute-for-advanced-technology/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform?utm_source=openai))

## What Looks Quiet, Unchanged, or Early

The biggest unresolved item remains the **AI Research Platform**. MBIE’s public page still says phase-two proposals were due on **31 March 2026**, that the assessment panel met in **mid-April 2026**, and that an announcement timeline will be shared “in due course.” Earlier programme documentation had pointed to a **late April funding decision**, **May 2026 announcement**, and **1 July 2026** contract commencement. As of **13 July 2026**, that means the public-facing process still appears to be lagging its original timetable. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/refocusing-the-science-innovation-and-technology-system/public-research-organisations/new-zealand-institute-for-advanced-technology/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform?utm_source=openai))

Infrastructure also looked quieter than in March. In the reviewed sources for this update, there was no fresh public milestone comparable to Datagrid’s **March 2026** consent and power-deal progress. That does not mean infrastructure work has stopped, but it does suggest the month’s visible AI story was led by **policy, services, and governance**, not by new compute announcements. ([datagrid.nz](https://www.datagrid.nz/pr1-rc/resourceconsent?utm_source=openai))

Commercially, the evidence remained thin. The public record this month skewed toward support programmes, state-system reform, health delivery, and sector events. There was little in the reviewed material to suggest a new wave of major NZ AI venture rounds, large enterprise rollouts, or nationally significant commercial launches. If anything, the ecosystem still looks stronger at **adoption support and convening** than at **capital formation or frontier-scale commercial execution**. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/ai-powered-tools-to-support-small-business-growth))

## Overall Assessment

The simplest reading of the past month is that AI in Aotearoa New Zealand became **more official, more operational, and more routinised**. The notable developments were not frontier-model breakthroughs. They were a science plan that gives AI a firmer place in national investment logic, more state-backed help for smaller firms, a new telehealth AI workflow, and an allied cyber-security posture that treats frontier AI as a present-tense risk. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-science-investment-plan-drive-economic-growth-and-prosperity?utm_source=openai))

That is a credible form of momentum, but it is not the same as a breakout month. New Zealand still appears to be moving faster on **guardrails, adoption support, and targeted service use cases** than on **research-platform closure, sovereign compute execution, or breakout private-sector scaling**. So the July 2026 picture is neither stagnant nor explosive: it is a country steadily wiring AI into its institutions while the biggest structural bets remain unresolved or early. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/refocusing-the-science-innovation-and-technology-system/public-research-organisations/new-zealand-institute-for-advanced-technology/call-for-proposals-for-the-artificial-intelligence-research-platform?utm_source=openai))
