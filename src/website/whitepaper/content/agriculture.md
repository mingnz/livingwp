# AI in Agriculture in Aotearoa New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper
*Updated 18 August 2026*

## Introduction

AI adoption in New Zealand agriculture is still best understood as a **portfolio of practical systems tied to specific farm, orchard, and biosecurity decisions**, not as a single uniform technology wave. The commercial centre of gravity remains dairy and livestock, but the market is broadening: AI is now showing up not only in pasture and animal-management tools, but also in sector support channels, horticultural quality systems, weed control, and government biosecurity workflows. That matters in a food-and-fibre sector that MPI expects to generate **NZ$64.3 billion** in export revenue in the year to **30 June 2026**. ([mpi.govt.nz](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/resources-and-forms/economic-intelligence/situation-and-outlook-for-primary-industries?utm_source=openai))

## Executive Snapshot

- **Dairy and livestock remain the most mature AI segment.** Halter, Aimer Farming, and DairyNZ continue to provide the clearest examples of AI being used in day-to-day farm operations, especially for virtual fencing, pasture intelligence, and farmer-facing decision support. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/en-nz/news/halters-largest-investment-in-beef-product-sees-farmers-reaping-rewards-of-ag-tech))
- **The biggest change since 13 July 2026 is that AI is spreading beyond dairy into beef operations and government biosecurity processes.** Halter’s July beef update and the Government’s July generative-AI pilot for import health standards are the clearest new signals. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/en-nz/news/halters-largest-investment-in-beef-product-sees-farmers-reaping-rewards-of-ag-tech))
- **Horticulture remains narrower than dairy, but often strategically higher value per use case.** Fruit sizing, grading, disease-risk modelling, and export-quality decisions continue to be the strongest horticulture applications, with Hectre, T&G’s AI-enabled postharvest systems, and the STELLA apple-disease programme standing out. ([auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/02/16/hectre-raises-millions-to-tackle-global-fruit-waste.html?utm_source=openai))
- **Institutional embedding is strengthening.** DairyNZ’s Responsible Dairy programme, DairyNZ’s DAiSY assistant, Fonterra-linked funding pathways, and MBIE’s adoption-led AI strategy all point to AI becoming more normalized inside the sector’s mainstream institutions. ([dairynz.co.nz](https://www.dairynz.co.nz/research/science-projects/responsible-dairy-programme/?utm_source=openai))
- **The key constraints remain trust, integration, connectivity, and proof of value.** DairyNZ still frames GenAI as support rather than replacement; FAR highlights rural connectivity and NZ-specific fit; MBIE still identifies a large SME adoption gap. ([dairynz.co.nz](https://www.dairynz.co.nz/people/productive-workplaces/using-artificial-intelligence-on-farm/))

## What Changed Since the Previous Update on 13 July 2026

- **Halter published materially stronger beef-side adoption signals on 16 July 2026.** The company said **500 New Zealand beef farms** had joined its satellite solution and **185 farms** were already on its new **Beef Pro** product. These are company-reported figures, but they are the strongest recent sign that AI-enabled livestock control is no longer only a dairy story. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/en-nz/news/halters-largest-investment-in-beef-product-sees-farmers-reaping-rewards-of-ag-tech))
- **Biosecurity AI moved further into official workflow.** On **25 July 2026**, the Government announced a **four-month pilot** of a generative-AI tool to help develop import health standards, with decisions remaining with Biosecurity New Zealand experts. That expands agricultural AI from on-farm operations into regulatory infrastructure. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-ai-tool-help-develop-biosecurity-standards?utm_source=openai))
- **Aimer’s June platform launch is now being operationalised into seasonal workflow.** Its **7 August 2026** calving-season guidance emphasised partial farm walks, AI estimation, and 14-day feed forecasting during one of the busiest periods of the dairy year. That is not a new breakthrough product, but it reinforces the market’s direction: AI that fits farm routines wins. ([aimer-farming.com](https://www.aimer-farming.com/blog-articles/make-staying-ahead-of-pasture-easier-this-calving))
- **The public outcome of the national AI research-platform process still does not appear clearly posted on the MBIE pages reviewed for this update.** The public material visible still points to the **December 2025 shortlist**, a **discovery phase running to mid-2026**, and an expectation that the final platform would be confirmed in the first half of 2026. This is an inference from currently visible public pages, not a formal delay notice. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/concepts-selected-for-ai-research-platform?utm_source=openai))

## Current News and Market Developments

### 1) Dairy and livestock AI remain the operating core

Halter is still the clearest scaled example of AI in New Zealand agriculture, but the latest story is now as much about **beef** as dairy. After its **28 April 2026** direct-to-satellite launch, the company’s **16 July 2026** update said hundreds of New Zealand beef farms had already joined the satellite service and that beef users were moving beyond fencing into pasture planning, behaviour monitoring, and automated grazing records. Halter also said it now serves **more than 3,500 farmers and ranchers** across New Zealand, Australia, and the United States and has sold **one million collars** globally; those are company figures, but they indicate meaningful category scale. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/en-nz/news/halter-launches-world-first-virtual-fencing-via-satellite))

Mainstream funding channels are also widening. Fonterra’s **2026/27 On-farm Solutions** programme offers eligible farmers **up to NZ$2,000 + GST** toward Halter, while Fonterra also highlighted a North Waikato farm using **Aimer**, described as an AI-powered farm-planning tool, with customer funding reducing part of the risk of adoption. Together, those examples suggest AI is increasingly being treated as ordinary improvement spend rather than experimental tech. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/en-nz/articles/fonterra-on-farm-solutions))

Aimer remains the strongest pasture-intelligence signal. Its **30 June 2026** AIMER Satellite launch combined proprietary satellite data, AI-powered smartphone measurement, and live paddock modelling; the company says the system is already used on **more than 650 farms** with **more than 10,000 pasture measurements a week**, and that direct smartphone measurements exceed **90% accuracy** while the blended system is designed to maintain **around 80% daily accuracy**. These are company-reported figures, but they show the product maturing from point measurement into a broader operating layer. ([aimer-farming.com](https://www.aimer-farming.com/blog-articles/aimer-farming-launches-pasture-system-combining-satellite-smartphone-measurement-and-live-farm-modelling))

Aimer’s **May 2026** government co-investment is strategically important for a second reason: it points toward **conversational farm decision support**. The company said MPI is investing **NZ$600,000** in a **NZ$1.675 million** project, with a next development phase focused on **Ask AIMER**, a chat-based assistant intended to return farm-specific recommendations rather than just dashboards. That is one of the clearest current examples of generative AI being pushed into a production workflow for pasture-based farming. ([aimer-farming.com](https://www.aimer-farming.com/blog-articles/aimer-farming-secures-government-co-investment-in-new-ai-tools))

### 2) Sector institutions are becoming AI deployment channels

DairyNZ’s **Responsible Dairy** programme is now one of the strongest institutional signals in the market. DairyNZ says the seven-year programme is funded at **NZ$45.85 million**, including **NZ$18.34 million** from MPI’s Primary Sector Growth Fund, and is designed to strengthen dairy resilience through modern infrastructure, environmental management, genetics, and emerging technologies. The significance is not one AI tool; it is the creation of a structured pathway for stacked technologies to be trialled and normalized. ([dairynz.co.nz](https://www.dairynz.co.nz/research/science-projects/responsible-dairy-programme/?utm_source=openai))

DairyNZ is also directly shaping how farmers encounter AI. Its public guidance describes GenAI as a practical tool for decision support, administration, translation, and access to farm-specific information, but explicitly says it should not replace independent advice and that DairyNZ does not endorse any particular tool. That cautious framing remains important because trust is still a gating issue in agriculture. ([dairynz.co.nz](https://www.dairynz.co.nz/people/productive-workplaces/using-artificial-intelligence-on-farm/))

At policy level, MBIE’s AI strategy is explicitly **adoption-led**, not frontier-model-led. The strategy says New Zealand should focus on using AI to solve local challenges, including in agriculture, and the summary material says AI use is rising among large firms while **68% of SMEs** were still not planning for AI. For agriculture, that makes the real challenge less about invention than about diffusion across a fragmented operator base. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/business-and-employment/economic-growth/digital-policy/new-zealands-ai-strategy-investing-with-confidence?utm_source=openai))

### 3) Horticulture and arable AI remain narrower, but the economics are sharp

Hectre remains one of the strongest horticulture cases. The company’s **NZ$12 million Series A** round in **February 2026** gave it fresh capital to expand AI and computer-vision systems for fruit quality. It then picked up major recognition at the **2026 NZ Hi-Tech Awards**, where it won agritech-related categories. AGMARDT says Hectre’s AI-powered grading now serves **around a third of the New Zealand apple market** and customers in **22 countries**. ([auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/02/16/hectre-raises-millions-to-tackle-global-fruit-waste.html?utm_source=openai))

Large-scale postharvest AI is also visible in incumbent operators. T&G Global’s 2024 annual report says its Hawke’s Bay postharvest systems use **deep learning technology** to improve yield and optimise packout, processing **3,000 to 3,500 apples per minute** at peak. Plant & Food Research separately highlighted T&G’s AI-enabled grading in Nelson and Hawke’s Bay, including assessment of colour, weight, defects, and internal fruit quality in real time. ([tandg.global](https://tandg.global/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/TG-Global-Annual-Report-2024.pdf?utm_source=openai))

For disease risk, the **STELLA** programme remains highly relevant. MBIE’s project summary says Hawke’s Bay orchards are one of the key international sites using digital technologies including AI to combat pests and diseases, with the New Zealand focus on apples. This is a high-value pattern in New Zealand horticulture: AI is justified where it protects export quality, storage life, and market access. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/news-success-stories/success-stories/growing-global-food-security-with-digital-foods?utm_source=openai))

In arable and seed systems, Plant & Food Research’s **Hyperseeds** project remains one of the more concrete examples of AI being applied to contamination screening and quality assurance. FAR’s technology strategy adds an important adoption reality check: digital-tool uptake in arable is mixed, and independent proof of value in New Zealand conditions is still essential. ([plantandfood.com](https://www.plantandfood.com/en-nz/data-science-and-new-technologies?utm_source=openai))

### 4) Public-good AI is becoming part of agricultural infrastructure

The clearest new public-sector signal is the **25 July 2026** decision to design and test a generative-AI tool for import health standards. The Government said the tool is intended to take on document-heavy work while leaving substantive decisions with Biosecurity New Zealand experts. In practical terms, this is AI being used to speed agricultural market and genetics access without formally relaxing expert oversight. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-ai-tool-help-develop-biosecurity-standards?utm_source=openai))

MPI’s yellow-legged hornet response remains another important example. MPI says the response has used **AI-enabled cameras**, radio tracking, bait stations, and other tools; as of the public update reviewed, **77 queen hornets** and **132 nests** had been found and destroyed, and no hornets or nests had been found since early April 2026. This is agricultural AI in the biosecurity layer rather than the farm software layer, but it is still directly tied to protecting production systems. ([mpi.govt.nz](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/news/media-releases/new-technology-introduced-for-autumn-push-in-hornet-eradication?utm_source=openai))

AgResearch’s **Map and Zap®** shows the same shift toward public-interest and field-deployable AI. The system uses AI to identify weeds and laser-zap them, targeting a problem AgResearch estimates costs New Zealand agriculture and forestry **at least NZ$1.7 billion a year**. It is significant because it combines analytics with physical intervention. ([agresearch.co.nz](https://www.agresearch.co.nz/products-and-services/map-and-zap/?utm_source=openai))

## Research Overview

The most useful current research framing still comes from DairyNZ, Perrin Ag, Rural Leaders, FAR, AgResearch, and MBIE rather than from academic theory alone. DairyNZ’s commissioned report on AI for dairy farmers explored current and near-future GenAI use cases, while DairyNZ’s public guidance continues to stress decision support, prompting quality, and human judgment. ([dairynz.co.nz](https://www.dairynz.co.nz/media/m11h0z1l/opportunities-of-ai-for-nz-dairy-farmers-dec2025-perrin-ag-final-report.pdf))

The **June 2026** Rural Leaders report adds a valuable adoption insight: AI is changing the role of rural professionals from knowledge transfer toward **data interpretation and decision facilitation**. It identified four critical future capabilities — data interpretation, critical thinking, adaptability, and relationship building — and found that **more than 50%** of respondents did not feel well supported through the shift. ([ruralleaders.co.nz](https://ruralleaders.co.nz/report/the-ai-shift-redefining-skills-and-leadership-for-rural-professionals-in-new-zealands-primary-sector/))

AgResearch’s research bench remains one of the deepest in the country. Its annual reporting says there are **more than 50 AI-focused research projects** under way, including CT-scan analysis for livestock traits linked to methane, feed efficiency, productivity, and welfare. That suggests New Zealand agricultural AI is increasingly as much a science-platform capability as a startup category. ([agresearch.co.nz](https://www.agresearch.co.nz/assets/Documents/AgR-AR-2025-FULL-v3.6.pdf?utm_source=openai))

At system level, the Government is trying to make the research base more commercially useful. MBIE’s **Science Investment Plan 2026–2036** says **NZ$142 million** has been invested through the New Zealand Institute for Advanced Technology in high-potential areas such as AI and quantum. Agriculture matters here because one of the shortlisted AI research-platform concepts is the **BioAI Platform**, aimed at practical innovation in agriculture, aquaculture, and forestry. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/dmsdocument/32023-science-investment-plan-2026-2036?utm_source=openai))

## Case Studies

### Halter: from standout startup to sector operating layer
Halter now spans virtual fencing, remote shifting, behaviour monitoring, reproduction tools, pasture planning, and satellite connectivity. Its latest beef-side numbers and Fonterra funding pathway make it the clearest sign that AI-enabled livestock control is moving toward mainstream farm operations. Company-reported metrics should still be treated as such, but the adoption trajectory is hard to ignore. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/en-nz/news/halters-largest-investment-in-beef-product-sees-farmers-reaping-rewards-of-ag-tech))

### Aimer Farming: pasture AI built around New Zealand’s grazing model
Aimer’s stack — smartphone measurement, partial-farm estimation, feed forecasting, live paddock modelling, and satellite augmentation — is tightly aligned with pasture-based dairy. The government co-investment and the planned Ask AIMER assistant show how the platform is evolving from measurement to recommendation. ([aimer-farming.com](https://www.aimer-farming.com/blog-articles/aimer-farming-launches-pasture-system-combining-satellite-smartphone-measurement-and-live-farm-modelling))

### Hectre: export-oriented horticulture AI
Hectre remains one of the strongest New Zealand examples of AI with global reach: fresh capital, industry awards, meaningful presence in the domestic apple market, and international customer spread. It is a strong example of New Zealand agriculture exporting digital capability as well as produce. ([auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/02/16/hectre-raises-millions-to-tackle-global-fruit-waste.html?utm_source=openai))

### STELLA and T&G: AI where export quality is won or lost
STELLA shows AI being used for earlier disease-risk detection in apples, while T&G shows AI embedded in high-throughput grading and packout. In both cases, the business logic is less about novelty and more about reducing loss before fruit reaches the wrong market or storage outcome. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/science-and-technology/science-and-innovation/news-success-stories/success-stories/growing-global-food-security-with-digital-foods?utm_source=openai))

### MPI and AgResearch: AI as protection infrastructure
The biosecurity standards pilot, hornet-response cameras, and Map and Zap® system show AI becoming part of agriculture’s defensive infrastructure: standards, surveillance, and weed control. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-ai-tool-help-develop-biosecurity-standards?utm_source=openai))

## Core Trends

### 1) Workflow-embedded AI continues to outperform general-purpose AI
The strongest tools are attached to a job farmers or growers already do: move cattle, allocate feed, estimate pasture, grade fruit, detect disease risk, or identify weeds. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/en-nz/news/halters-largest-investment-in-beef-product-sees-farmers-reaping-rewards-of-ag-tech))

### 2) Dairy remains the benchmark, but beef is catching up fast
Dairy still has the deepest stack of production-grade tools, but Halter’s latest beef rollout suggests beef is now the fastest-moving livestock AI subsegment. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/en-nz/news/halters-largest-investment-in-beef-product-sees-farmers-reaping-rewards-of-ag-tech))

### 3) Institutional channels are reducing adoption friction
DairyNZ, Fonterra, MPI, and MBIE are no longer just commentators on AI adoption; they are becoming delivery channels, validators, and funders. ([dairynz.co.nz](https://www.dairynz.co.nz/research/science-projects/responsible-dairy-programme/?utm_source=openai))

### 4) Horticulture AI is concentrated where quality failures are expensive
Fruit grading, storage decisions, and disease-risk prediction remain easier to justify than generic “AI transformation” promises because the commercial payoff is more immediate and measurable. ([agmardt.org.nz](https://agmardt.org.nz/our-impact-stories/hectre-grows-into-global-fruit-technology-success-story/?utm_source=openai))

### 5) New Zealand’s AI posture in agriculture is increasingly adoption- and commercialisation-led
Policy settings, science investment, and sector programmes all point toward translating AI into usable tools rather than prioritising frontier-model development for its own sake. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/business-and-employment/economic-growth/digital-policy/new-zealands-ai-strategy-investing-with-confidence?utm_source=openai))

## Constraints and Risks

- **Trust and validation remain decisive.** DairyNZ continues to present AI as support, not substitute, and advises farmers to verify outputs. ([dairynz.co.nz](https://www.dairynz.co.nz/people/productive-workplaces/using-artificial-intelligence-on-farm/))
- **Interoperability and data fragmentation still matter.** The value of AI rises when it can combine multiple datasets, but much of the sector still works across separate tools and inconsistent data flows. ([ruralleaders.co.nz](https://ruralleaders.co.nz/report/the-ai-shift-redefining-skills-and-leadership-for-rural-professionals-in-new-zealands-primary-sector/))
- **Rural connectivity remains a practical bottleneck.** FAR explicitly highlights the need for tools that work with low bandwidth, intermittent coverage, or no internet. ([far.org.nz](https://www.far.org.nz/resources/checking-the-tech-for-arable))
- **Many performance claims are still company-reported.** That is especially true for commercial livestock and pasture platforms, so independent longitudinal validation is still needed. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/en-nz/news/halters-largest-investment-in-beef-product-sees-farmers-reaping-rewards-of-ag-tech))
- **The SME adoption gap remains a structural issue.** MBIE’s summary still points to a large share of SMEs not planning to invest in AI. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/dmsdocument/30889-new-zealands-artificial-intelligence-strategy-summary-1-pager?utm_source=openai))
- **National AI research coordination is still not fully legible in public.** Agriculture-facing concepts are prominent in the AI research-platform process, but a clearly visible final public outcome was not readily apparent in the materials reviewed for this update. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/concepts-selected-for-ai-research-platform?utm_source=openai))

## Conclusion

As of **18 August 2026**, AI adoption in New Zealand agriculture is best described as **practical, selective, and increasingly institutionalised**. Dairy and livestock still lead the market, but the most important shift since **13 July 2026** is that AI is moving more decisively into **beef operations**, **biosecurity workflow**, and **mainstream sector delivery channels**. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/en-nz/news/halters-largest-investment-in-beef-product-sees-farmers-reaping-rewards-of-ag-tech))

The sector’s strongest use cases remain the same: tools that are easy to fit into existing work, produce a clear action, and tie to visible economics. That is why virtual fencing, pasture intelligence, fruit grading, disease-risk modelling, and weed identification continue to outperform broader, less grounded AI propositions. ([halterhq.com](https://www.halterhq.com/en-nz/news/halter-launches-world-first-virtual-fencing-via-satellite))

The near-term outlook is therefore not one of sudden autonomous farming. It is one of **more AI inside ordinary agricultural decisions**: more recommendations inside pasture systems, more machine vision inside postharvest lines, more surveillance inside biosecurity, and more institutional support helping farmers decide which technologies are genuinely worth adopting. ([aimer-farming.com](https://www.aimer-farming.com/blog-articles/aimer-farming-secures-government-co-investment-in-new-ai-tools))
