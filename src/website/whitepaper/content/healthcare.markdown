# AI Adoption in Healthcare in New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper  
**Updated: 29 May 2026**

## Executive Summary

New Zealand healthcare AI is still best described as **targeted deployment under tightening controls**, not broad autonomous clinical adoption. The strongest live implementation remains **ambient documentation**, with AI scribes already deployed across all public emergency departments. Since the previous edition on **2 April 2026**, the most important shift has been a move from general governance principles to **more operational controls and clearer implementation pathways**: a nationally endorsed AI evaluation framework is now being used across Health NZ, new internal guidance sharply restricts unapproved generative AI use, an AI-supported symptom checker has moved into formal exploration, and breast-screening AI has advanced from market sounding to procurement and planned validation. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

The overall pattern has not changed: New Zealand is most comfortable adopting AI where it can **augment clinicians, reduce administrative load, or assist high-volume interpretation tasks** under human oversight. That makes documentation, screening, imaging, triage support, and selected workflow tools the main adoption lanes. More ambitious clinical AI remains mostly in research, pilot, validation, or controlled rollout stages rather than routine nationwide care. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

A second notable change since early April is that the **digital and security foundations** for AI are being treated more explicitly as part of the adoption story. Budget 2026 includes new cyber funding for Health NZ, plus additional digital investment for core platforms and radiology modernisation, while Health NZ’s Shared Digital Health Record and National Primary Care Dataset continue to build the data infrastructure needed for safe scaling. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/investing-cyber-security-protect-patient-data))

## What Has Changed Since 2 April 2026

- **Breast-screening AI has moved one stage closer to deployment.** On **27 May 2026**, the Government said procurement was underway to select a preferred AI mammogram-reading tool for **testing and validation**, with a **planned rollout from early 2027**. The proposed model is for AI to perform **one of the two independent reads** in BreastScreen Aotearoa, while clinicians remain central to diagnosis and follow-up. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-breast-screening-tool-one-step-closer))
- **Health NZ’s AI assessment process is now more concrete.** A University of Auckland TRANSFORM update published on **30 April 2026** said a nationally endorsed framework for evaluating AI tools in the Aotearoa New Zealand health sector was endorsed by Health NZ’s Executive Leadership Team in **March 2026** and is now being used to assess **all proposed AI tools across Health NZ services**. ([transform.auckland.ac.nz](https://www.transform.auckland.ac.nz/2026/04/30/collaboratively-developing-an-evaluation-framework-for-ai-tools-in-the-aotearoa-new-zealand-health-sector/))
- **Health NZ has published explicit internal rules for generative AI.** Guidance last updated on **5 May 2026** says employees and contractors must **not** enter personal, confidential, or sensitive patient information into unapproved LLMs, and must **not** use LLMs for clinical decisions or personalised patient advice. Health NZ also says it does **not currently have any private LLMs available**. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-professionals/guidance-standards/topic/digital-technologies/using-generative-ai-and-large-language-models))
- **AI-enabled navigation is becoming a more visible pathway.** A Health NZ page updated **5 May 2026** says it is exploring an **AI-supported online symptom checker** to help people navigate to the right service, with a clinically led governance group being set up. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/what-we-do/programmes-and-initiatives/symptom-checker))
- **Budget 2026 has strengthened the enabling layer.** On **28 May 2026**, the Government announced **$153.6 million** for cyber security improvements across the health system and said Health NZ was also investing an additional **$300 million** to help deliver the first three years of the Health Digital Investment Plan, including radiology and core IT upgrades. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/investing-cyber-security-protect-patient-data))

## Current State of AI Adoption

### 1. Operational deployment: documentation remains the most mature use case

The clearest example of AI at scale in New Zealand healthcare remains the **nationwide public emergency department scribe rollout**. On **28 February 2026**, the Government said AI scribes were live in **all emergency departments**, reaching **1,250 ED doctors and frontline staff**. It also said pilot clinicians were able to see **one additional patient per shift on average**, and Health NZ was progressing **more than 1,000 additional licences**, mainly for mental health teams. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

This matters because it shows the New Zealand model most clearly: AI is being accepted first where it acts as a **workflow-embedded clinician support tool** rather than an autonomous decision-maker. That model is also consistent with the newer breast-screening proposal, the symptom-checker language, and Health NZ’s own restrictions on unapproved generative AI. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

### 2. Primary care: market uptake continues, with more integrated products

Primary care remains the fastest-moving adoption environment outside hospitals. A New Zealand primary care survey published in **March 2026** found strong early uptake of AI scribes: **80%** of users found them helpful or very helpful, while the study also highlighted unresolved issues around consent, legal oversight, data security, and the broader impact on consultation dynamics. ([ourarchive.otago.ac.nz](https://ourarchive.otago.ac.nz/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Using-AI-scribes-in-New-Zealand/9926780884301891?utm_source=openai))

On the vendor side, Medtech Global launched **Medtech AI** in New Zealand on **23 February 2026** as a system integrated directly into **Medtech Evolution**, rather than a stand-alone transcription tool. Medtech says the platform generates structured consultation notes, referral letters, and correspondence, and that **every output is reviewed and approved by the clinician**. ([medtechglobal.com](https://medtechglobal.com/nz/medtech-ai-launch/))

The practical implication is that primary care AI in New Zealand is moving from ad hoc experimentation toward **EHR-integrated workflow support**, but still under a trust-and-governance shadow. That reading is reinforced by the survey findings, by the Medical Council’s March guidance, and by Health NZ’s May restrictions on unapproved LLM use. ([ourarchive.otago.ac.nz](https://ourarchive.otago.ac.nz/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Using-AI-scribes-in-New-Zealand/9926780884301891?utm_source=openai))

### 3. Screening and imaging: still the strongest next-wave clinical adoption lane

Breast screening is now the most visible near-term clinical scale-up opportunity. In February, Health NZ began exploring how AI image-reading tools could be safely used within BreastScreen Aotearoa; by **27 May 2026**, that had advanced to active procurement for a preferred tool, with testing and validation ahead of a planned early-2027 rollout. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/exploring-ai-support-breast-screening-services?utm_source=openai))

Diabetes eye screening remains another important implementation pathway. Health NZ’s **National Diabetes Roadmap 2026**, published last month, includes a specific action to **evaluate the role of AI in supporting retinal photoscreening**. The roadmap also says the **Diabetes Retinal Screening Model of Care pilot has been completed** and is being evaluated as part of an ongoing quality-improvement approach. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/assets/Publications/national-diabetes-roadmap-2026.pdf))

That policy direction sits on top of an older but still important New Zealand evidence base. THEIA, a New Zealand-developed diabetic retinopathy AI system, was built using local screening datasets, and its prospective multi-centre evaluation showed strong performance in the New Zealand screening context. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32794618/?utm_source=openai))

### 4. Navigation, access, and triage support are becoming a second visible lane

Health NZ’s newly published **Symptom Checker** page shows that AI-supported navigation is being taken seriously as a health-system access tool, not just as a consumer app experiment. The proposed tool is intended to help people identify the right level of care and could also summarise symptoms for clinicians, while Health NZ stresses that clinical decision-making will remain with healthcare providers. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/what-we-do/programmes-and-initiatives/symptom-checker))

This direction aligns with the Government’s earlier **Whakarongorau mental health AI navigation platform** announcement from **26 November 2025**, which framed AI as a digital front door for identifying services and improving access rather than as a diagnostic substitute. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-navigation-deliver-faster-access-support?utm_source=openai))

Taken together, these signals suggest that **navigation and triage support** may become the next important adoption category after documentation and imaging—especially where AI can reduce fragmentation and route people more quickly into the right service. This is an inference from the currently visible programme mix rather than a formal Health NZ statement. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/what-we-do/programmes-and-initiatives/symptom-checker))

## Research and Innovation Pipeline

New Zealand’s research pipeline remains unusually implementation-oriented. The Health Research Council says it has funded **10 AI-in-healthcare studies worth $4.6 million**, with projects aimed at improving access, monitoring, diagnostics, and service delivery. ([hrc.govt.nz](https://www.hrc.govt.nz/news-and-events/nz-researchers-awarded-714m-tackle-pressing-health-issues?utm_source=openai))

Key funded projects include:

- **Acute stroke:** an HRC-funded project led from Health NZ Capital, Coast and Hutt Valley will measure whether AI-assisted rapid brain-scan interpretation improves intervention rates and reduces treatment delays across New Zealand hospitals; the lay summary says that, if successful, it could help an extra **850 New Zealanders** access critical treatment each year. ([hrc.govt.nz](https://www.hrc.govt.nz/resources/research-repository/optimising-acute-stroke-care-artificial-intelligence))
- **Postoperative monitoring:** a University of Auckland project is using digital tools and AI to improve early warning after surgery, with explicit attention to privacy, equity, and health data sovereignty. ([hrc.govt.nz](https://www.hrc.govt.nz/resources/research-repository/toward-digital-paradigm-postoperative-monitoring))
- **Digital pathology for gastrointestinal cancer:** an Otago-led project aims to combine AI analysis of biopsy images with other patient information to support more precise treatment decisions and avoid unnecessary surgery. ([hrc.govt.nz](https://www.hrc.govt.nz/resources/research-repository/improving-gastrointestinal-cancer-outcomes-ai-enhanced-digital))
- **Youth mental healthcare ethics:** an AUT-led project is co-designing practical guidance for safe, fair, and culturally responsive AI use in youth mental healthcare with young people, clinicians, Māori communities, technology experts, and government agencies. ([hrc.govt.nz](https://www.hrc.govt.nz/resources/research-repository/ethics-ai-driven-technologies-youth-mental-healthcare))
- **Acute asthma prediction:** another HRC-funded project is using machine learning on a decade of New Zealand healthcare data to predict acute asthma events. ([hrc.govt.nz](https://www.hrc.govt.nz/resources/research-repository/predicting-acute-asthma-events-using-machine-learning?utm_source=openai))

The research picture therefore still looks less like speculative AI science and more like a **targeted service-improvement portfolio**, with strong emphasis on stroke, cancer, monitoring, chronic disease, and mental health. ([hrc.govt.nz](https://www.hrc.govt.nz/news-and-events/nz-researchers-awarded-714m-tackle-pressing-health-issues?utm_source=openai))

## Recent Case Studies and Evidence Signals

### Case Study 1: Nationwide ED ambient scribes

The ED rollout remains New Zealand’s clearest proof point that AI can move from pilot to national frontline deployment when the use case is narrow, measurable, and clinician supervised. The Government’s claim of one extra patient per shift on average is operationally significant because it ties AI adoption directly to throughput and workforce pressure. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

### Case Study 2: BreastScreen Aotearoa’s AI pathway

The breast-screening programme has now progressed from exploratory outreach to procurement for a preferred mammogram-reading tool, with testing and validation planned before rollout. This is notable because it suggests Health NZ is willing to use AI in a clinically consequential setting where the workflow is structured, the task is repetitive and high-volume, and human oversight remains built in. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/exploring-ai-support-breast-screening-services?utm_source=openai))

### Case Study 3: Diabetic retinal screening as research-to-policy translation

The National Diabetes Roadmap’s explicit reference to evaluating AI for retinal photoscreening, combined with the completed retinal screening model-of-care pilot and the earlier THEIA evidence base, makes diabetic eye screening one of the strongest examples of a **research-to-implementation pathway** in New Zealand health AI. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/assets/Publications/national-diabetes-roadmap-2026.pdf))

### Case Study 4: AI-assisted colonoscopy in a New Zealand hospital setting

A **2026** randomised controlled trial at **Waitakere Hospital** compared an AI-assisted computer-aided polyp detection module with conventional colonoscopy. While this is not a national deployment story, it is a useful signal that New Zealand hospitals are contributing to real-world evidence in AI-assisted endoscopy, not just consuming overseas studies. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41287376/?utm_source=openai))

## Governance, Regulation, and Digital Foundations

### Governance is becoming more operational

The Medical Council’s final guidance, published on **10 March 2026**, remains the clearest profession-level rule set for AI in patient care. It was a major pre-April change, but it continues to shape the current environment because it formalises clinician accountability and the expectation that AI be used ethically and responsibly. ([mcnz.org.nz](https://www.mcnz.org.nz/our-standards/current-standards/guidance-on-using-artificial-intelligence-in-patient-care/))

Since then, Health NZ has added more operational guardrails. Its May 2026 guidance tells staff not to put sensitive information into unapproved generative AI tools and not to use LLMs for clinical decisions or personalised advice, while the NAIAEAG continues to require that AI development or implementation plans be registered and reviewed. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-professionals/guidance-standards/topic/digital-technologies/using-generative-ai-and-large-language-models))

The April 2026 public update from the TRANSFORM group adds another layer: a nationally endorsed evaluation framework is now being used to assess all proposed AI tools across Health NZ services. In practice, this means governance is shifting from broad principles to **repeatable assessment machinery**. ([transform.auckland.ac.nz](https://www.transform.auckland.ac.nz/2026/04/30/collaboratively-developing-an-evaluation-framework-for-ai-tools-in-the-aotearoa-new-zealand-health-sector/))

### Trust, privacy, and social licence remain central

New Zealand research published in the **New Zealand Medical Journal** in February 2026 found that patient trust depends on strong oversight, data protection, transparency, patient choice, visible clinician responsibility, and Māori representation in AI development and governance. ([nzmj.org.nz](https://nzmj.org.nz/journal/vol-139-no-1629/maintaining-patient-trust-as-artificial-intelligence-s-role-in-healthcare-grows))

Health NZ’s privacy materials and system-design pages reinforce that stance. The Shared Digital Health Record page says the new connector will make key health information available nationwide from **mid-2026**, with opt-out and privacy controls, while the National Primary Care Dataset page says participating practices began sharing encounter and appointment data from **April 2026** under privacy, security, and governance requirements. ([info.health.nz](https://info.health.nz/privacy/wider-sharing-of-your-health-information))

### Infrastructure is still both enabler and constraint

Health NZ’s May 2026 cyber announcement is strategically important for AI because adoption depends on trusted infrastructure, vendor controls, and resilient core systems. Budget 2026 funding includes stronger 24/7 cyber monitoring, annual audits of critical systems, tighter oversight of third-party risks, and AI-enabled assessments to improve cyber maturity in primary care. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/investing-cyber-security-protect-patient-data))

This suggests that New Zealand’s AI trajectory in healthcare will continue to be shaped not only by clinical appetite or vendor availability, but by whether the country can strengthen **data-sharing, interoperability, privacy assurance, and cyber resilience** fast enough to support scale. That is an inference drawn from the funding and platform decisions now visible. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/investing-cyber-security-protect-patient-data))

## Key Patterns and Trends

### 1. Admin-first adoption is still the dominant model
The most mature and scalable AI use case remains **clinical documentation support**, especially ambient scribing. That is where New Zealand has already moved from pilot to national public deployment. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

### 2. Imaging and screening remain the strongest next clinical scale-up lane
Breast screening, retinal screening, stroke imaging, digital pathology, and AI-assisted endoscopy all point in the same direction: New Zealand is most likely to expand clinical AI where there are **high-volume interpretation tasks, specialist bottlenecks, and measurable outcomes**. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-breast-screening-tool-one-step-closer))

### 3. Governance is no longer just policy language
The combination of the Medical Council guidance, NAIAEAG oversight, the new Health NZ generative-AI restrictions, and the endorsed evaluation framework means AI adoption is being pulled into **formal approval, review, and control processes** rather than left to local improvisation. ([mcnz.org.nz](https://www.mcnz.org.nz/our-standards/current-standards/guidance-on-using-artificial-intelligence-in-patient-care/))

### 4. Health-system access tools are emerging as an important AI category
The symptom checker exploration and the Whakarongorau navigation platform show growing interest in AI that helps patients find the right service, rather than diagnosing or treating them directly. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/what-we-do/programmes-and-initiatives/symptom-checker))

### 5. Equity, Māori data sovereignty, and public benefit remain adoption conditions
Across Health NZ governance, HRC-funded research, and local trust research, AI is consistently framed as acceptable only when it is safe, equitable, transparent, and clearly beneficial to New Zealanders. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/who-we-are/expert-groups-and-networks/expert-groups/artificial-intelligence-and-algorithm-expert-advisory-group?utm_source=openai))

## Overall Assessment

As of **29 May 2026**, AI adoption in New Zealand healthcare has advanced, but not in a way that supports claims of system-wide AI transformation. The evidence points instead to a health system moving forward through **selective operational deployment, formalised evaluation, and staged clinical validation**. The live proof point is still ambient scribing in emergency care; the clearest next clinical candidate is breast-screening AI; and the most important new developments since **2 April 2026** are the publication of stricter generative-AI controls, the use of a nationally endorsed evaluation framework, symptom-checker exploration, and stronger digital-security investment. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

The broad New Zealand model remains clear: **AI as supervised augmentation, not autonomous care**. Clinicians remain responsible, public benefit must be visible, privacy and trust are treated as prerequisites, and infrastructure readiness is increasingly recognised as part of AI readiness. That is a cautious model, but it is also increasingly coherent—and, compared with earlier 2026, more operationally defined. ([mcnz.org.nz](https://www.mcnz.org.nz/our-standards/current-standards/guidance-on-using-artificial-intelligence-in-patient-care/))
