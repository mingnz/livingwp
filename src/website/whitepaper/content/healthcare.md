# AI Adoption in Healthcare in New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper  
**Updated: 18 August 2026**

## Executive Summary

As of **18 August 2026**, AI adoption in New Zealand healthcare is still best described as **targeted, clinician-supervised augmentation**, not broad clinical automation. The clearest scaled public deployment remains **ambient documentation**: Health Minister Simeon Brown said on **28 February 2026** that AI scribe technology was live in **all emergency departments**, reaching **1,250** ED doctors and frontline staff, with **more than 1,000 additional licences** being progressed, mainly for mental health teams. Health NZ’s privacy statement continues to say AI is used to assist clinicians, that many uses are still limited or pilot-phase, and that AI is **not** used to make automated decisions about a person’s healthcare. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

The main changes since the previous update on **13 July 2026** are about **governance hardening, front-door experimentation, and better digital plumbing**, rather than a new nationwide clinical rollout. On **27 July 2026**, Health NZ published a symptom checker update saying its proof of concept had found enough potential benefit to justify further exploration, under a newly appointed governance group with Māori, Pacific, disabled, clinical, and AI-safety representation; findings are intended to inform a **mid-to-late 2027** decision on whether to begin formal procurement. On **28 July 2026**, Health NZ publicly highlighted a **new national framework** for assessing AI tools, while University of Auckland TRANSFORM said the framework had already been **endorsed in March 2026** and was being used to assess **all proposed AI tools across Health NZ services**. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/what-we-do/programmes-and-initiatives/symptom-checker?utm_source=openai))

Primary care is also widening beyond note-taking. On **27 July 2026**, Tāmaki Health launched what it describes as **New Zealand’s first AI Health Coach in routine primary care**, reporting more than **4,500 conversations** and early self-reported gains in user confidence. This is not comparable in scale or governance maturity to the national ED rollout, but it is a notable sign that AI in healthcare is starting to move from clinician-facing documentation into **patient engagement and navigation**. ([tamakihealth.co.nz](https://www.tamakihealth.co.nz/news/t%C4%81maki-health-launches-new-zealands-first-ai-health-coach-in-primary-care?utm_source=openai))

Breast screening remains the clearest next-wave public clinical deployment. The latest official milestone still appears to be the **27 May 2026** announcement that procurement was underway for a preferred AI mammogram-reading tool, with testing and validation ahead of a planned rollout from **early 2027**. In the official sources reviewed through 18 August 2026, no later public milestone had overtaken that procurement step. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-breast-screening-tool-one-step-closer?utm_source=openai))

The broader pattern is increasingly clear: New Zealand is building a healthcare AI model around **supervised augmentation, local validation, privacy controls, equity guardrails, and phased implementation**. That is an inference from the current deployment pattern, Health NZ guidance, recent governance updates, and the live research pipeline. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/privacy/privacy-statement?utm_source=openai))

## What Has Changed Since 13 July 2026

- **Health NZ formalised governance around consumer-facing AI navigation tools.** On **27 July 2026**, Health NZ said its symptom checker work had identified enough evidence of potential benefit to justify further exploration, under a governance group spanning clinical safety, AI safety, equity, accessibility, and data sovereignty. The findings are intended to inform a **mid-to-late 2027** decision on whether to proceed to procurement. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/what-we-do/programmes-and-initiatives/symptom-checker?utm_source=openai))
- **The national AI assessment framework became more visible operationally.** Health NZ’s digital technologies page highlighted a “consistent approach to AI tools” on **28 July 2026**, while TRANSFORM said its Aotearoa-specific evaluation framework had been **endorsed in March 2026** and is now used to assess **all proposed AI tools across Health NZ services**. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-professionals/guidance-standards/topic/digital-technologies?utm_source=openai))
- **Primary care added a new AI use case beyond scribing.** On **27 July 2026**, Tāmaki Health launched an AI Health Coach integrated into routine care pathways, saying the service had already handled **4,500+ conversations** and could escalate users into human care pathways when needed. ([tamakihealth.co.nz](https://www.tamakihealth.co.nz/news/t%C4%81maki-health-launches-new-zealands-first-ai-health-coach-in-primary-care?utm_source=openai))
- **Data and access infrastructure moved into implementation mode.** The new **primary care health target** took effect on **1 July 2026**, supported by a new **National Primary Care Dataset**; Health NZ says participating-practice data collected from 1 July will feed the first national results, due by the **end of 2026**. ([health.govt.nz](https://www.health.govt.nz/strategies-initiatives/programmes-and-initiatives/primary-and-community-health-care/primary-care-health-target?utm_source=openai))
- **Regional operations planning is now explicitly using AI tools.** Health NZ’s **Winter Plan 2026: Te Manawa Taki** includes rolling out **Heidi** in the Lakes emergency department to support faster patient flow, suggesting ambient documentation is being treated as an operational throughput tool, not just a pilot innovation. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/news-and-updates/winter-plan-2026-te-manawa-taki-region?utm_source=openai))
- **No later public official milestone has overtaken BreastScreen AI procurement.** As of **18 August 2026**, the latest official public step still appears to be procurement and validation planning for AI mammogram reading ahead of a proposed **early 2027** rollout. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-breast-screening-tool-one-step-closer?utm_source=openai))

## Current State of AI Adoption

## 1. Public hospitals: ambient documentation remains the most mature live deployment

The strongest evidence of scaled adoption in the public hospital system remains the **national ED AI scribe rollout** announced on **28 February 2026**. The Government said the tool was live in **all emergency departments**, reaching **1,250** frontline staff, with more than **1,000** extra licences being progressed, mostly for mental health teams. The same announcement said pilot users could see, on average, **one additional patient per shift**, and reported positive early feedback from Middlemore ED staff. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

What matters strategically is not only the scale, but the **type** of scale. Health NZ’s privacy statement says AI-generated information that could affect the clinical record or decision-making is reviewed by the responsible clinician, and that AI is **not** used to make automated decisions about a person’s healthcare. In other words, the health system’s clearest willingness to scale AI is still in **documentation and workflow support with human review**, not autonomous decision-making. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/privacy/privacy-statement?utm_source=openai))

There are also signs that ambient AI is shifting from “innovation project” to **operational performance tool**. Health NZ’s Te Manawa Taki winter plan explicitly includes rolling out **Heidi** in Lakes ED to support patient flow over winter. That does not equal a new national rollout, but it does show AI being embedded in routine service planning. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/news-and-updates/winter-plan-2026-te-manawa-taki-region?utm_source=openai))

At the same time, Health NZ is cautiously exploring AI-enabled navigation at the system front door. Its symptom checker programme says the proof of concept is under strong clinical oversight and expert governance, and that any future decision on procurement will wait until **mid-to-late 2027**. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/what-we-do/programmes-and-initiatives/symptom-checker?utm_source=openai))

## 2. Primary care: adoption is widening from scribes toward integrated workflow and patient engagement

Primary care remains the fastest-moving non-hospital setting. A New Zealand study published on **26 March 2026** found that, among **197** respondents, **40%** had experience with AI scribes; reported benefits included reduced multitasking, time savings, and improved rapport, but the study also highlighted gaps around consent, terms-and-conditions awareness, and confidence in legal and ethical compliance. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41134014/?utm_source=openai))

The Royal New Zealand College of General Practitioners’ workforce survey reinforces that direction of travel: its current workforce survey page says **41% of GPs** and **16% of rural hospital doctors** had already used, or intended to use, AI, with **note-taking and scribe tools** the most common use case. ([rnzcgp.org.nz](https://www.rnzcgp.org.nz/our-voice/workforce-survey/?utm_source=openai))

The vendor layer is also becoming more deeply embedded in local software. Medtech says **Medtech AI**, launched in New Zealand on **23 February 2026**, is built directly into **Medtech Evolution**, synthesises patient history from the PMS, stores data locally in New Zealand, and requires clinicians to obtain explicit patient consent before recording starts. That is significant because it moves AI from standalone experimentation toward **workflow-native infrastructure** inside widely used practice software. ([medtechglobal.com](https://medtechglobal.com/nz/medtech-ai-launch/?utm_source=openai))

The newest shift is toward **patient-facing augmentation**. On **27 July 2026**, Tāmaki Health launched an AI Health Coach that it says works alongside human Health Coaches and clinical teams, supports behaviour change and chronic-condition management, and can escalate patients into existing care pathways when needed. Tāmaki Health reported more than **4,500 conversations**, a **32%** increase in self-reported confidence after one session, and **75%** by month two. These are company-reported figures, but they are a meaningful signal that AI use in healthcare is broadening beyond documentation. ([tamakihealth.co.nz](https://www.tamakihealth.co.nz/news/t%C4%81maki-health-launches-new-zealands-first-ai-health-coach-in-primary-care?utm_source=openai))

## 3. Aged care: documentation AI is spreading into residential care

Aged residential care is now a clearer part of the adoption story. On **12 May 2026**, Metlifecare said it would roll out the **HEIDI** ambient scribe tool across **three more care homes** after a four-month pilot at Parkside Village involving **12 registered nurses**. Metlifecare said nurses spent less time on documentation and improved handovers, while clinicians remained responsible for reviewing, editing, and approving all documentation. ([metlifecare.co.nz](https://www.metlifecare.co.nz/news/metlifecare-heidi-scribe-tool?utm_source=openai))

This matters because it shows the same AI adoption pattern appearing outside hospitals and GP clinics: **high-documentation environments, workforce pressure, and strong preference for human-reviewed outputs**. ([metlifecare.co.nz](https://www.metlifecare.co.nz/news/metlifecare-heidi-scribe-tool?utm_source=openai))

## 4. Screening, imaging, and diagnostics: still the strongest next-wave clinical lane

Breast screening remains the most visible public clinical AI programme in the pipeline. On **27 May 2026**, the Government said procurement was underway for a preferred AI mammogram-reading tool for testing and validation, ahead of a planned rollout from **early 2027**. The proposed model has AI performing **one of the two reads** in the current screening process, while clinicians remain central to diagnosis and follow-up. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-breast-screening-tool-one-step-closer?utm_source=openai))

The case for this programme is partly clinical and partly operational. The same announcement said around **270,000** women aged **45 to 69** are screened annually through BreastScreen Aotearoa, with a phased age extension to **74** underway. In July, the refreshed **New Zealand Cancer Action Plan 2026 to 2029** also framed advances such as AI as both an opportunity and a system challenge for cancer care. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-breast-screening-tool-one-step-closer?utm_source=openai))

Retinal screening remains the other leading imaging use case. The June 2026 NZMJ paper on diabetic retinal screening argued that New Zealand’s proof of concept showed AI implementation is not plug-and-play; success depends on digital systems, workflow redesign, clinician readiness, and the fit of available tools. Health NZ’s **National Diabetes Roadmap 2026** separately includes a specific action to **evaluate the role of AI in supporting retinal photoscreening**. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42348896/?utm_source=openai))

The wider diagnostics pipeline is substantial. HRC-funded projects are evaluating AI in **chest X-ray interpretation**, **stroke imaging**, **digital pathology for gastrointestinal cancers**, **heart failure management**, and **postoperative monitoring**, with a strong emphasis on implementation, workflow, and equity rather than just model performance. ([hrc.govt.nz](https://www.hrc.govt.nz/resources/research-repository/transforming-radiology-new-zealand-accuracy-implementation-ai?utm_source=openai))

## Governance, Regulation, and Digital Foundations

## 1. The operating model is still “assist clinicians, not automate care”

Health NZ’s privacy statement says AI is used in a closed environment for functions including transcription, consultation summaries, document generation, staff knowledge tools, and preliminary review of images, x-rays, scans, and mammograms. It also says many of these activities remain limited or pilot-phase, and that Health NZ does **not** use AI to make automated decisions about healthcare. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/privacy/privacy-statement?utm_source=openai))

Health NZ’s current guidance on generative AI and large language models remains restrictive. It says staff must not enter patient or other sensitive information into unapproved LLMs and must not use LLMs for clinical decisions or personalised patient advice. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-professionals/guidance-standards/topic/digital-technologies/using-generative-ai-and-large-language-models?utm_source=openai))

The Medical Council’s March 2026 guidance reinforces that professional boundary. It says doctors remain responsible for clinical decisions and actions, must be satisfied that AI is safe and suitable, and must use AI ethically and responsibly to protect patient safety and privacy. ([mcnz.org.nz](https://www.mcnz.org.nz/our-standards/current-standards/guidance-on-using-artificial-intelligence-in-patient-care/?utm_source=openai))

## 2. Governance is becoming more operational and portfolio-based

Health NZ’s **National Artificial Intelligence and Algorithm Expert Advisory Group (NAIAEAG)** page was updated on **22 July 2026** and states that all AI development or implementation plans must be **registered** with the group. The page also says the group maintains an AI register of tools used in Health NZ and that public summaries of advice, recommendations, or approvals will be made available. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/who-we-are/expert-groups-and-networks/expert-groups/artificial-intelligence-and-algorithm-expert-advisory-group?utm_source=openai))

The Aotearoa-specific evaluation framework developed through the University of Auckland’s TRANSFORM programme is now an important part of that governance stack. TRANSFORM says the framework was endorsed by Health NZ’s Executive Leadership Team in **March 2026** and is now being used to assess **all proposed AI tools across Health NZ services**. Health NZ’s digital technologies page then publicly surfaced this as a “consistent approach to AI tools” on **28 July 2026**. ([transform.auckland.ac.nz](https://www.transform.auckland.ac.nz/2026/04/30/collaboratively-developing-an-evaluation-framework-for-ai-tools-in-the-aotearoa-new-zealand-health-sector/?utm_source=openai))

The symptom checker programme adds a second sign of governance maturity: Health NZ has set up a dedicated governance group covering clinical safety, AI safety, risk, quality, accessibility, equity, and data sovereignty before any procurement decision is made. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/what-we-do/programmes-and-initiatives/symptom-checker?utm_source=openai))

## 3. Regulation and data infrastructure are catching up, but still incomplete

At the national policy level, the Ministry of Health says work on the **Medical Products Bill** includes regulatory pathways for **software and artificial intelligence intended for a therapeutic purpose**, and later Ministry material says the Bill is intended to support approval pathways for **AI-enabled medical devices**. That is important for the future regulatory environment, but it is still a **developing framework**, not the main reason current deployments are moving slowly. ([health.govt.nz](https://www.health.govt.nz/information-releases/innovative-medical-products-and-regulatory-pathways-to-market?utm_source=openai))

The more immediate pacing issue is still **data readiness and secure interoperability**. Health NZ’s July 2026 update on the **Shared Digital Health Record** said rollout had been extended for additional checks under the Government Digital Delivery Agency information-sharing standard; **immunisation and medication data** are expected later in **2026**, while **primary care data** is expected from **mid-2027**. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/news-and-updates/shared-digital-health-record-update-july-2026?utm_source=openai))

Primary care data work is moving in parallel. The new **primary care health target** came into effect on **1 July 2026**, supported by a **National Primary Care Dataset** that begins with encounters and appointment data. Health NZ says all data use is governed by a data access framework and dataset governance group, and that first national results are due by the **end of 2026**. For AI adoption, the significance is straightforward: higher-value tools increasingly depend on **timely, standardised, governed data flows**. ([health.govt.nz](https://www.health.govt.nz/strategies-initiatives/programmes-and-initiatives/primary-and-community-health-care/primary-care-health-target?utm_source=openai))

## Research and Innovation Pipeline

New Zealand’s AI research pipeline remains **broader than its live deployment footprint**. The Health Research Council’s June 2026 announcement of the **REVOLUTION** programme added a major decision-support trial to the landscape: a **$5 million** New Zealand-led study across **50 ICUs** in New Zealand and Australia, recruiting more than **24,000 patients**, to test machine-learning-guided oxygen therapy. ([hrc.govt.nz](https://hrc.govt.nz/news-and-events/major-nz-led-clinical-trial-test-ai-guided-treatment-critically-ill-patients?utm_source=openai))

That sits alongside a wider HRC-funded portfolio spanning radiology, retinal screening, stroke care, pathology, heart failure, dementia risk, and postoperative monitoring. The common theme is that most projects are tackling **bounded, measurable clinical problems** with explicit attention to implementation, safety, bias, and workflow. ([hrc.govt.nz](https://www.hrc.govt.nz/resources/research-repository/transforming-radiology-new-zealand-accuracy-implementation-ai?utm_source=openai))

The June NZMJ paper on diabetic retinal screening is especially important because it gives New Zealand a local implementation lesson: even relatively intuitive AI use cases can stall if digital systems, classifications, workflows, resourcing, and clinician readiness are not aligned. That paper helps explain why New Zealand’s public deployments are advancing more cautiously than the rhetoric around AI might imply. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42348896/?utm_source=openai))

## Trust, Equity, and Social Licence

Trust remains a binding constraint on scale. Health NZ’s current symptom checker governance explicitly includes Māori, Pacific, and disabled representation, and names obligations around **te Tiriti o Waitangi**, cultural safety, accessibility, and **data sovereignty**. Health NZ’s LLM guidance also explicitly warns about privacy breaches, inaccuracies, inequities, bias, transparency limits, and Māori data sovereignty. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/what-we-do/programmes-and-initiatives/symptom-checker?utm_source=openai))

Privacy and cyber resilience are now inseparable from AI adoption. The Ministry of Health’s **27 May 2026** review into the Manage My Health cyber incident said it was one of the most serious cyber incidents experienced in the New Zealand health sector, while **Budget 2026** included funding to strengthen digital services and safeguard patient information from cyber threats. The practical implication is that more data-driven healthcare means cyber maturity has to improve alongside AI maturity. ([health.govt.nz](https://www.health.govt.nz/news/independent-review-recommends-stronger-cyber-security-across-health-system?utm_source=openai))

## Key Trends

### 1. Documentation-first adoption is now firmly established
The clearest scaled deployments remain **ambient documentation and transcription** across emergency departments, primary care, and aged care. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

### 2. “Front-door” AI is emerging, but under heavy supervision
Symptom checking and AI health coaching are now active themes, but both sit inside governance, escalation, and pathway design rather than autonomous triage. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/news-and-updates/symptom-checker-update-july-2026?utm_source=openai))

### 3. Imaging and screening remain the strongest near-term clinical scale-up lane
Breast screening procurement, retinal screening evaluation, stroke imaging, and chest X-ray implementation research all point in the same direction. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-breast-screening-tool-one-step-closer?utm_source=openai))

### 4. Governance is shifting from principles to operating machinery
NAIAEAG registration, the national evaluation framework, Health NZ’s LLM rules, and specific governance structures for programmes like the symptom checker show that AI governance is becoming an **operational system**, not just a set of aspirations. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/who-we-are/expert-groups-and-networks/expert-groups/artificial-intelligence-and-algorithm-expert-advisory-group?utm_source=openai))

### 5. Data infrastructure is becoming the main pacing function
The Shared Digital Health Record delay and the staged National Primary Care Dataset rollout both show that secure interoperability and governed data flows are prerequisites for broader clinical AI adoption. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/news-and-updates/shared-digital-health-record-update-july-2026?utm_source=openai))

### 6. New Zealand is still favouring local validation over fast scaling
That is an inference from the current pattern: national ED scribes have scaled, but decision-support and diagnostic tools are mostly moving through procurement, implementation science, or trials rather than rapid clinical automation. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

## Overall Assessment

As of **18 August 2026**, AI adoption in New Zealand healthcare has advanced, but in a distinctly **controlled and selective** way. The most mature live use remains documentation support; the most visible public clinical programme in the pipeline remains AI-assisted mammogram reading; and the most important recent developments are the formalisation of governance, the widening of primary-care use cases, and the continued build-out of the data and security foundations needed for more ambitious deployment. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

The evidence still does **not** support a claim that New Zealand healthcare has moved into broad autonomous clinical AI. It supports a narrower conclusion: the country is building a model of **clinician-supervised augmentation**, with documentation first, imaging and decision support second, and patient-facing navigation tools entering cautiously behind governance, equity, privacy, and interoperability controls. That approach is slower than headline AI narratives elsewhere, but it is becoming more coherent. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/privacy/privacy-statement?utm_source=openai))
