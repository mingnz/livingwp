# AI in Healthcare in New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper

New Zealand healthcare is moving from AI pilots to supervised operational use, led by documentation and patient-access tools. Clinical AI remains mostly in evaluation, procurement or research, with governance, equity, interoperability and cyber resilience determining what scales next.

## Executive Summary

AI adoption in Aotearoa New Zealand healthcare is becoming more structured, but it is not yet broadly autonomous or clinically transformative.

The strongest evidence of operating deployment remains in **administrative and workflow assistance**:

- Health New Zealand has made an AI scribe available in every emergency department, reaching approximately 1,250 doctors and frontline staff.
- Tāmaki Health is operating several AI systems across its private primary-care network, including scribes, appointment and prescription automation, patient-record summarisation and, more recently, an AI health coach.
- Metlifecare is extending ambient documentation technology from an initial aged-care trial into additional care homes.
- Medtech AI is commercially available for New Zealand practices using Medtech Evolution.

The next adoption frontier is closer to direct patient interaction:

- AI health coaching in primary care.
- AI-enabled triage and referral across mental-health helplines.
- Symptom-checker evaluation.
- Digital navigation and self-management tools.

These uses have greater potential to affect access and demand, but they also carry greater risks. The public record does not yet show nationwide operation, independently verified clinical outcomes or autonomous decision-making in these areas.

Clinical AI is progressing through more cautious pathways:

- AI-assisted mammogram reading remains in procurement, testing and validation ahead of a possible 2027 rollout.
- AI-integrated diabetic-retinal screening has exposed practical barriers involving workflow, digital systems, clinician readiness and equity.
- The REVOLUTION trial is testing whether machine-learning-guided oxygen therapy can improve outcomes for critically ill patients, but it is a research programme rather than routine care.

The system’s governance architecture is also becoming more explicit. Health NZ now has a nationally endorsed AI evaluation framework, a formal expert advisory group, published restrictions on generative AI use, and professional guidance from the Medical Council. At the same time, the 2026 public-sector AI survey shows a rapid increase in government use cases without providing a health-specific adoption denominator.

The overall picture is **selective, clinician-supervised and increasingly institutionalised adoption**. New Zealand is scaling tools where the value proposition is bounded and measurable, while requiring substantially stronger evidence before AI influences diagnosis, treatment or patient pathways.

## What Has Changed Since the Last Update

### A national public-sector survey confirms rising adoption, but not health-specific scale

The Government Digital Delivery Agency’s 2026 cross-agency survey, published on **19 August 2026**, provides the clearest new system-wide indicator of AI adoption.

Fifty-nine organisations reported **545 AI use cases**, compared with 272 use cases reported by 70 organisations in 2025. The survey says 167 cases were in operational phases in 2026—three times the 2025 figure. Health was among the sectors reporting the highest number of use cases, alongside social services, justice, central agencies and finance. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/dmsdocument/264~report-2026-cross-agency-survey-for-artificial-intelligence-ai-use-cases/html))

This is meaningful evidence of growing institutional activity, but it should not be treated as a measure of clinical deployment. The survey does not publish a health-sector breakdown showing:

- How many health use cases are operational.
- How many involve patient data.
- How many are clinical rather than administrative.
- How many have been independently evaluated.
- How many are operating at national scale.

The survey is therefore best interpreted as evidence that healthcare is part of a broader public-sector adoption wave, not as proof that New Zealand healthcare has moved into widespread AI-enabled care.

### The launch of NZIAT strengthens the wider AI ecosystem, but is not a healthcare deployment

On **21 August 2026**, the Government formally launched the New Zealand Institute for Advanced Technology. NZIAT is intended to accelerate development and commercialisation of advanced technologies, including AI, quantum technologies and advanced materials. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/new-zealand-institute-advanced-technology-launches-auckland?utm_source=openai))

Its relevance to healthcare is indirect. A stronger domestic research and commercialisation base could improve access to New Zealand-developed health technologies, local technical capability and applied research partnerships. However, the launch itself is not evidence of a healthcare AI product being deployed or adopted.

### Tāmaki Health’s patient-facing rollout is now better documented

The previous update described Tāmaki Health’s AI Health Coach as beginning rollout in August. The provider’s own announcement dates the launch to **27 July 2026**, with deployment initially limited to selected clinics. The subsequent industry reporting on **12 August 2026** confirmed that the service was being introduced as a 24/7 digital front door for health coaching. ([tamakihealth.co.nz](https://www.tamakihealth.co.nz/news/t%C4%81maki-health-launches-new-zealands-first-ai-health-coach-in-primary-care?utm_source=openai))

The clarification matters because the initiative is operating, but not nationally scaled. The available evidence supports the following description:

- It is live in selected clinics.
- It supports behaviour change and self-management.
- It is designed to complement human health coaches and clinical teams.
- It can refer people into existing care pathways.
- Early performance figures are provider-reported and not yet independent clinical-outcome evidence.

### Governance has moved from principle to process

The national AI evaluation framework associated with the University of Auckland’s TRANSFORM programme was endorsed by Health NZ’s Executive Leadership Team in **March 2026** and is now described as being used to assess proposed AI tools across Health NZ services. Health NZ publicly highlighted the framework on **28 July 2026**. ([transform.auckland.ac.nz](https://www.transform.auckland.ac.nz/2026/04/30/collaboratively-developing-an-evaluation-framework-for-ai-tools-in-the-aotearoa-new-zealand-health-sector/?utm_source=openai))

This is a substantive change from a landscape in which adoption could depend heavily on individual hospitals, practices, vendors or clinical champions. The framework is intended to consider patient and community impacts, workforce implications and health-system effects before implementation.

The change is not that every AI project is now independently proven. Rather, Health NZ is developing a repeatable institutional gateway through which proposed tools must pass.

### No additional national clinical deployment is publicly documented after the 19 August update

The latest public Health NZ and Government health releases through **21 August 2026** do not identify another new nationwide clinical AI deployment after the developments already covered in the previous edition. The most significant current activity remains the national emergency-department scribe, selected-clinic primary-care tools, mental-health triage development, imaging procurement and clinical research. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/portfolio/nationalactnew-zealand-first-coalition-government-2023-2026/health?utm_source=openai))

This absence is itself informative. The sector is active, but the pace of announcement is currently faster than the pace of verified national implementation.

## Current State of AI Adoption

### 1. Documentation and workflow assistance: the most mature category

Documentation remains the clearest entry point for AI in New Zealand healthcare because it offers a comparatively bounded use case.

Ambient scribes can record or transcribe a consultation, generate a draft note and prepare related documentation. They do not need to decide the diagnosis or treatment if the clinician reviews and approves the output.

The national emergency-department deployment is the strongest example. Health NZ announced in February that AI scribe technology was live in every emergency department, with approximately 1,250 doctors and frontline staff given access. The initial pilot reported that doctors could see, on average, one additional patient per shift because of time saved on documentation. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department))

The evidence should still be read carefully:

- “Available” or “live” does not mean every clinician uses the tool on every shift.
- Reported productivity figures come from pilot and early rollout feedback.
- The output remains a draft clinical record.
- Clinicians retain responsibility for accuracy and final sign-off.
- The system is not authorised to make autonomous diagnostic or treatment decisions.

The deployment is therefore operationally significant, but it is best described as **nationally available, clinician-supervised documentation assistance**, not autonomous clinical AI.

Primary care is following the same pattern. The Royal New Zealand College of General Practitioners’ 2024 workforce survey found that 41% of GP respondents and 16% of rural hospital doctors had either used or intended to use AI. Note-taking and scribe tools were the most common applications. ([rnzcgp.org.nz](https://www.rnzcgp.org.nz/our-voice/workforce-survey/?utm_source=openai))

Medtech AI became available in New Zealand in February 2026 for practices using Medtech Evolution. The platform combines patient-record context with clinical documentation and related workflow support. It is a commercial product launch rather than evidence of a nationally measured adoption rate. ([dha.org.nz](https://www.dha.org.nz/news/medtech-global-launches-ai-powered-intelligence-layer-moving-beyond-traditional-scribing?utm_source=openai))

The primary-care evidence therefore supports a conclusion of **rapid but uneven diffusion**. Adoption is likely to be concentrated in larger, digitally capable practices and networks, while smaller practices may face greater barriers involving cost, integration, privacy assessment, training and change management.

### 2. Private primary care is moving from individual tools to an AI-enabled operating model

Tāmaki Health provides the most developed public example of multi-use AI adoption in New Zealand primary care.

The organisation has reported using:

- AI scribes.
- An AI call-centre agent for appointment bookings and repeat prescriptions.
- A patient-summary and record-query system.
- An AI Health Coach for patient self-management.

The call-centre system is reported to be operating across three clinics, handling approximately 40% of appointment bookings at participating sites. Tāmaki Health also reported an average 3% reduction in did-not-attend rates, a zero missed-appointment rate at one clinic, and 10–12-point improvements in Net Promoter Scores. These figures were presented by Tāmaki Health executives and reported through Health Informatics New Zealand; they should therefore be treated as self-reported operational results rather than independently verified evaluations. ([hinz.org.nz](https://www.hinz.org.nz/news/726162/AI-call-centre-agent-reduces-DNAs-and-improves-patient-satisfaction-scores.htm?utm_source=openai))

Tāmaki Health has also reported clinician adoption of its AI scribe programme at approximately 91–92%. Again, this is an organisational report rather than a national benchmark. ([hinz.org.nz](https://www.hinz.org.nz/news/726162/AI-call-centre-agent-reduces-DNAs-and-improves-patient-satisfaction-scores.htm?utm_source=openai))

The significance of this case is not any single tool. It is the sequencing:

1. Establish a common patient-management environment.
2. Automate high-volume, repetitive administrative tasks.
3. Reduce documentation burden.
4. Add patient-summary and information-retrieval functions.
5. Extend support to patient coaching and prevention.

This is a more credible route to scale than introducing disconnected AI products one at a time. It also demonstrates why underlying system architecture matters. Tāmaki Health has attributed its ability to integrate tools partly to a standardised data environment across its network. ([cdn.ymaws.com](https://cdn.ymaws.com/www.hinz.org.nz/resource/resmgr/special-reports/events/From_IT_to_Digital_and_AI_in.pdf?utm_source=openai))

### 3. Patient coaching is operating, but outcomes evidence remains early

Tāmaki Health’s AI Health Coach is designed to support patients before, between and after appointments. It covers areas including diabetes, gout, weight management, anxiety and other health goals. The tool provides personalised guidance and action plans and can refer people to health coaches or clinical teams when additional support is needed. ([tamakihealth.co.nz](https://www.tamakihealth.co.nz/news/t%C4%81maki-health-launches-new-zealands-first-ai-health-coach-in-primary-care?utm_source=openai))

The provider reports more than 4,500 conversations, a 32% increase in users’ reported confidence and capability after one coaching session, a 75% increase by the second month, and an average helpfulness rating of 8.5 out of 10. These are encouraging engagement signals, but they are not equivalent to evidence of improved glycaemic control, reduced hospitalisation, improved mental-health outcomes or reduced inequity. ([tamakihealth.co.nz](https://www.tamakihealth.co.nz/news/t%C4%81maki-health-launches-new-zealands-first-ai-health-coach-in-primary-care?utm_source=openai))

Important unanswered questions include:

- Which patients use the tool and which do not?
- Does use persist beyond the initial interaction?
- Does coaching improve measurable health outcomes?
- How well does it perform for Māori, Pacific peoples, older people and people with disabilities?
- How reliably does it identify when human clinical support is required?
- Does it reduce demand, or generate additional contacts and referrals?

The current status is therefore **live, bounded and promising, but not yet clinically validated at population scale**.

### 4. Mental-health AI is moving toward implementation, not yet routine operation

Mental health has become one of the most consequential AI adoption areas because demand is high, access is uneven and incorrect triage can cause serious harm.

The Government and Health NZ have allocated **$3.35 million over four years** to develop and implement an AI-enabled triage and referral tool across publicly funded mental-health and addiction helplines. The intended functions are to assess support needs, identify urgency and improve referrals between services. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/news-and-updates/telehealth-investment-will-help-more-people-access-mental-health-and-addiction-support-faster?utm_source=openai))

This is a funded development and implementation programme. It is not evidence that an AI triage system is already operating nationwide.

The risk profile is materially different from documentation assistance. A triage tool may influence:

- Whether a person is directed to crisis support.
- How urgently a response is required.
- Which service receives the referral.
- Whether a person believes their situation has been adequately assessed.

For this reason, the key implementation tests will include false reassurance, missed suicidality, escalation failure, language and cultural performance, and the handling of people whose needs do not fit standard categories.

Health NZ’s February announcement that more than 1,000 additional scribe licences were being progressed, predominantly for mental-health teams, should also be distinguished from evidence of completed deployment. The public record confirms an intention and approval pathway, but does not establish that all those licences are operating at scale. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department))

### 5. Screening and imaging are the leading clinical scale-up pipeline

Breast screening remains the most advanced proposed clinical AI deployment.

Health NZ began procurement for an AI mammogram-reading tool in May 2026. The proposed model would have AI perform one of the two independent reads currently used in the assessment process, while qualified clinicians retain responsibility for diagnosis and follow-up. A rollout from early 2027 is planned, subject to testing and validation. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-breast-screening-tool-one-step-closer))

This is not yet an operating deployment. Procurement, local validation, workflow testing and clinical governance remain important gates.

The proposed design is also significant. It uses AI to increase workforce capacity without removing the human diagnostic process entirely. This reflects the risk-proportionate pattern emerging in New Zealand:

- AI may provide a preliminary or parallel assessment.
- Clinicians remain responsible for the final interpretation.
- The system must be tested in the local population and workflow.
- Rollout depends on evidence beyond vendor performance claims.

Diabetic-retinal screening provides a cautionary counterpoint. A New Zealand Medical Journal paper described a proof of concept involving mobile retinal cameras in seven Pacific primary-care practices and training kaiāwhina to capture images. The project encountered challenges involving digital integration, model-of-care changes, clinician readiness and whether the available tools were appropriate for the intended setting. ([nzmj.org.nz](https://nzmj.org.nz/journal/vol-139-no-1637/implementation-of-artificial-intelligence-in-healthcare-in-aotearoa-new-zealand-learnings-from-the-diabetic-retinal-screening-us?utm_source=openai))

The lesson is broader than retinal screening. A technically accurate model may still fail to improve care if:

- Images cannot move reliably between systems.
- Staff are not trained or supported.
- Referral pathways are unclear.
- The model is not validated for the intended population.
- The service is not resourced to respond to abnormal results.

### 6. Clinical decision support remains primarily research-led

The REVOLUTION trial is an important signal that New Zealand’s clinical AI research is becoming more outcome-focused.

Led by the Medical Research Institute of New Zealand and supported by the Health Research Council, the five-year trial will involve 50 intensive-care units across New Zealand and Australia and recruit more than 24,000 patients. It will test whether machine-learning-assisted oxygen targets improve survival for critically ill patients receiving life support. ([mrinz.ac.nz](https://www.mrinz.ac.nz/news/hrc-programme-grant-supports-mrinz-led-world-first-ai-intensive-care-research))

This is materially different from demonstrating that an algorithm can predict an outcome. The trial asks whether using the model changes treatment in a way that improves patient outcomes.

However, the initiative remains a research programme. It is not evidence that machine-learning-guided oxygen therapy is already routine in New Zealand intensive-care units.

The distinction between prediction and intervention is becoming increasingly important. New Zealand’s most credible clinical AI pathway currently appears to be:

1. Develop or validate a model.
2. Test it retrospectively or prospectively.
3. Evaluate workflow and clinician interaction.
4. Conduct a controlled clinical trial where appropriate.
5. Consider implementation only after outcome and safety evidence.

## Governance, Policy and Regulation

### Health NZ has a formal approval and oversight structure

Health NZ’s National Artificial Intelligence and Algorithm Expert Advisory Group is responsible for advising on and endorsing AI projects and research involving Health NZ. Its stated scope covers appropriateness, safety, effectiveness, ethics, legality, social and cultural licence, implementation and ongoing improvement. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/who-we-are/expert-groups-and-networks/expert-groups/artificial-intelligence-and-algorithm-expert-advisory-group?utm_source=openai))

The group’s responsibilities include assessing:

- Clinical and operational utility.
- Patient and population perspectives.
- Equity, bias and discrimination.
- Māori health and Māori data sovereignty.
- Privacy, confidentiality and security.
- Ownership, accountability and contracts.
- Monitoring, audit and post-deployment review.

Health NZ says AI development and implementation plans must be registered with the group and that a national register of AI in use will be made publicly available. However, the public record does not yet provide a complete, easily searchable register of all AI tools operating across Health NZ. This is an important transparency gap.

### Health NZ’s generative-AI guidance remains restrictive

Health NZ’s guidance tells employees and contractors not to:

- Enter personal, confidential or sensitive information into unapproved large language models or generative-AI tools.
- Use such tools for clinical decisions.
- Use them to provide personalised advice to patients.

The guidance also requires users to check generated content and acknowledge when generative AI has been used. Health NZ states that it does not currently provide staff with a private general-purpose LLM. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-professionals/guidance-standards/topic/digital-technologies/using-generative-ai-and-large-language-models?utm_source=openai))

Its privacy statement distinguishes approved AI environments from public tools. It says approved uses may include transcription, consultation summaries, document generation, staff knowledge resources and preliminary review of images or scans. Information that could affect the patient record or clinical decision-making must be reviewed by the responsible clinician. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/privacy/privacy-statement?utm_source=openai))

The policy position is therefore consistent: **approved, bounded assistance may be used; unapproved generative AI must not be used with sensitive information or as a substitute for clinical judgement**.

### Professional obligations are becoming clearer

The Medical Council’s **March 2026** guidance on AI in patient care requires doctors to understand the purpose and limitations of tools they use, check AI outputs where reasonably practicable and remain responsible for clinical decisions and actions. ([mcnz.org.nz](https://www.mcnz.org.nz/our-standards/current-standards/guidance-on-using-artificial-intelligence-in-patient-care/?utm_source=openai))

The guidance also addresses:

- Transparency when AI is used.
- Informed consent for consultation recording and scribe tools.
- Consent where identifiable information is shared outside the primary record or used for training.
- Documentation when AI influences clinical decision-making.
- The prohibition on using AI to impersonate a doctor in medical practice.

This gives clinicians a more specific professional framework than general statements about “responsible AI”. It also reinforces that liability and accountability remain with the registered practitioner.

### New Zealand is favouring existing regulatory mechanisms over a standalone AI Act

The proposed Medical Products Bill is intended to replace the Medicines Act 1981 and modernise regulation of medicines, medical devices and related products. Government policy material indicates that software performing a medical purpose, such as analysing medical images to diagnose cancer, could fall within the proposed software-as-a-medical-device framework. General note-taking or practice-management software would generally not. ([health.govt.nz](https://www.health.govt.nz/regulation-legislation/medicines-legislation/regulating-medicines-medical-devices-and-natural-health-products?utm_source=openai))

The policy direction is a proportionate, risk-based approach using existing or sector-specific mechanisms rather than creating a standalone AI Act. This may reduce duplication, but it also creates boundary questions for systems that sit between administration, patient engagement and clinical decision support.

The most difficult cases are likely to involve tools that:

- Generate personalised patient advice.
- Rank or prioritise referrals.
- Recommend treatment options without formally diagnosing.
- Predict deterioration or hospitalisation.
- Combine administrative automation with clinical workflow decisions.

### Cybersecurity and privacy are adoption constraints, not separate issues

Budget 2026 allocated **$153.6 million** to strengthen Health NZ’s cyber-security monitoring, expertise, data-security processes and critical IT upgrades. A further **$300 million** was allocated to support the first three years of the Health Digital Investment Plan, including modernising radiology systems, replacing ageing devices and upgrading core platforms. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/investing-cyber-security-protect-patient-data?utm_source=openai))

These investments are not all AI-specific, but they are prerequisites for AI at scale. AI systems depend on:

- Reliable identity and access controls.
- Secure data exchange.
- Stable cloud and network services.
- Auditable system interfaces.
- Strong vendor security.
- Resilient clinical systems when connectivity fails.

The significance of this investment is heightened by the Manage My Health breach. The Privacy Commissioner found that sensitive information relating to 99,416 patients was affected and concluded that both Manage My Health and Health NZ had breached obligations under the Privacy Act and Health Information Privacy Code. ([privacy.org.nz](https://www.privacy.org.nz/tuhono-connect/statements-media-releases/privacy-commissioner-finds-manage-my-health-and-health-nz-breached-privacy-act/?utm_source=openai))

The breach was not itself an AI incident, but it demonstrates why health-sector confidence in data governance cannot be assumed. AI adoption may be slowed not by model performance, but by concerns about whether the surrounding data environment is secure enough to trust.

### Equity, Māori data sovereignty and language remain central

Health NZ’s generative-AI guidance explicitly identifies risks of under-representation and misrepresentation of Māori, Pacific peoples, women, older people, disabled people and gender-diverse communities. It also warns that public generative-AI tools are unlikely to adequately respect Māori Data Sovereignty and that te reo Māori and other minority languages may be poorly supported. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-professionals/guidance-standards/topic/digital-technologies/using-generative-ai-and-large-language-models?utm_source=openai))

Health NZ’s AI advisory group includes expertise in mātauranga and tikanga Māori, Māori data sovereignty, health inequities, disability and consumer perspectives. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/who-we-are/expert-groups-and-networks/expert-groups/artificial-intelligence-and-algorithm-expert-advisory-group?utm_source=openai))

The challenge is to turn these requirements into operational practice. Strong governance should result in:

- Population-specific performance testing.
- Māori and Pacific participation in design and evaluation.
- Clear decisions about data ownership and reuse.
- Culturally safe escalation and communication.
- Non-digital alternatives where necessary.
- Monitoring for differential error rates after deployment.

The diabetic-retinal-screening experience suggests that equity cannot be added after the technical design is complete. It may require changing the care model, workforce mix, physical location of services and referral process.

### Public trust is a measurable adoption variable

The Privacy Commissioner’s 2026 research found that 67% of respondents were concerned about government agencies or businesses using AI to make decisions about them using their personal information. Fifty-six percent were concerned about the security of health information. ([privacy.org.nz](https://www.privacy.org.nz/assets/DOCUMENTS/Corporate-Reports/20260703-Statement-of-Intent-2026.pdf?utm_source=openai))

These results are not a direct survey of healthcare AI acceptance, but they indicate the trust environment in which healthcare deployments operate. Consent, transparency and human accountability are therefore not merely compliance tasks. They influence whether patients will agree to recording, use patient-facing tools or accept AI involvement in care.

## Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Health NZ AI scribe in emergency departments

**Use case:** Ambient transcription and clinical documentation.

**Status:** Live and available across all New Zealand emergency departments.

**Reported scale:** Approximately 1,250 doctors and frontline staff.

**Reported benefits:**

- Reduced documentation time.
- More time available for patient care.
- An average of one additional patient seen per doctor per shift in the initial pilot.
- Positive early feedback on productivity and clinician experience.

**Limitations:**

- Output requires clinician review.
- Errors and fabricated content remain possible.
- Patient consent and transparency are required.
- Public evidence about sustained national utilisation and patient outcomes remains limited.

The deployment demonstrates that a bounded administrative use case can move from pilot to national availability faster than clinical decision support. It also shows why adversarial testing and post-deployment monitoring matter: a documentation system can still create clinical risk if inaccurate content enters the record or if users rely on it outside its intended scope.

### Case Study 2: Tāmaki Health’s integrated AI operating model

**Use case:** Primary-care administration, documentation, record summarisation and patient access.

**Status:** Multiple tools operating across a private network, with some use cases limited to selected clinics.

**Reported components:**

- AI scribes used by a high proportion of clinicians.
- An AI call-centre agent for bookings and repeat prescriptions.
- A patient-summary system connected to the practice-management environment.
- AI-supported appointment reminders and preventive-care workflows.

**Reported results:** Approximately 40% of appointments booked by the call-centre agent at participating clinics, an average 3% reduction in missed appointments, and improved patient-satisfaction measures.

**Assessment:** Tāmaki Health is one of the clearest examples of AI moving from isolated experimentation to an organisational operating model. The reported results are promising but self-reported. The case’s strongest lesson is architectural and organisational: standardised data, workflow integration and frontline buy-in are as important as the model itself.

### Case Study 3: Tāmaki Health and Groov AI Health Coach

**Use case:** Patient self-management and behaviour-change support between appointments.

**Status:** Operating in selected primary-care clinics since July 2026.

**Conditions and goals:** Diabetes, gout, weight management, anxiety and related lifestyle goals.

**Operating model:**

- 24/7 digital access.
- Personalised action plans.
- Support before, between and after appointments.
- Escalation to human health coaches and clinical teams.

**Assessment:** This is a meaningful shift from clinician-facing AI to patient-facing AI. It may help extend scarce health-coaching capacity, but outcome evidence is currently based on user feedback and provider reporting. The central evaluation question is whether engagement produces sustained health improvement without widening digital or cultural inequities.

### Case Study 4: Metlifecare HEIDI rollout

**Use case:** Ambient documentation for registered nurses in aged residential care.

**Status:** Four-month trial at Parkside Village followed by rollout to three additional care homes.

**Reported benefits:**

- Less time spent on administrative documentation.
- More time for resident care and whānau conversations.
- Improved documentation and handover experience.

The system transcribes conversations with resident consent and generates structured notes for staff review. ([metlifecare.co.nz](https://www.metlifecare.co.nz/news/metlifecare-heidi-scribe-tool?utm_source=openai))

The case shows that ambient documentation is spreading beyond hospitals and general practice. Aged care may be a particularly relevant setting because staff spend substantial time recording observations and coordinating care, but resident consent, cognitive impairment, family involvement and privacy require careful implementation.

### Case Study 5: AI-integrated diabetic-retinal screening

**Use case:** Expanding access to diabetic-retinopathy screening for Pacific communities.

**Approach:** Mobile retinal cameras in Pacific primary-care practices, with kaiāwhina trained to capture images.

**Status:** Proof of concept, not a scaled national service.

**Key lesson:** Technical capability was not enough. The project encountered barriers involving system integration, workflow redesign, clinician readiness, resourcing and tool suitability. ([nzmj.org.nz](https://nzmj.org.nz/journal/vol-139-no-1637/implementation-of-artificial-intelligence-in-healthcare-in-aotearoa-new-zealand-learnings-from-the-diabetic-retinal-screening-us?utm_source=openai))

This is one of the most useful New Zealand implementation cases because it shows that an equity-focused deployment can be harder, not easier, than a narrow technology demonstration. The service must work for patients, frontline staff, specialists, data systems and local communities simultaneously.

## Trends

### 1. Administrative AI is scaling faster than clinical AI

Documentation, appointment booking, record summarisation and routine communications are easier to bound than diagnosis or treatment. They can deliver value while preserving clinician control.

This does not make them risk-free. Errors in notes, missed appointments, inaccurate summaries and poor escalation can affect care. But their risk can generally be managed through review, access controls and workflow design.

### 2. Adoption is shifting from standalone tools to integrated platforms

The most advanced private-sector example is not a single product. It is a network of systems connected to a common practice-management environment.

This suggests that future adoption will depend on whether vendors can integrate securely with:

- Practice-management systems.
- Electronic health records.
- Radiology platforms.
- Scheduling and contact-centre systems.
- Shared health-information services.
- National clinical terminology.

Health NZ’s digital-services hub and its continuing work with SNOMED CT and interoperability standards are relevant foundations. SNOMED CT is already used across clinical documentation, shared records, clinical decision support, analytics and AI scribes. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-professionals/guidance-standards/topic/data-and-standards/health-information-standards/snomed-ct-national-release-centre/snomed-ct-new-zealand-edition?utm_source=openai))

### 3. Patient-facing AI is the next major governance test

AI coaching, symptom checking and mental-health triage are likely to grow because they address access and workforce constraints. They also change the risk profile.

Patient-facing systems need more than accuracy testing. They require:

- Clear communication about what the system is and is not.
- Safety-netting and emergency escalation.
- Human handoff.
- Accessibility and language support.
- Protection against over-reliance.
- Monitoring for unequal performance.
- A clear answer to who is accountable when the system fails.

### 4. Healthcare AI evidence remains fragmented

The public evidence base contains a mixture of:

- Government announcements.
- Vendor claims.
- Provider-reported performance.
- Research protocols.
- Proofs of concept.
- Early operational evaluations.

These are not interchangeable.

The sector still lacks a comprehensive public dashboard covering:

- Number of deployed tools.
- Active users.
- Patient volumes.
- Clinical domains.
- Evaluation status.
- Reported incidents.
- Equity performance.
- Costs and savings.
- Tools that were discontinued.

Without such information, it is difficult to determine whether AI is improving care system-wide or simply generating pockets of local productivity.

### 5. Implementation science is becoming as important as model accuracy

The diabetic-retinal-screening paper and the national evaluation framework point toward a more mature question: not “Can the model work?” but “Can this service work in the New Zealand health system?”

That requires attention to:

- Workforce capability.
- Procurement and contracts.
- Data quality.
- Interoperability.
- Clinical ownership.
- Patient and whānau experience.
- Equity.
- Training and support.
- Post-deployment monitoring.
- Business continuity.

### 6. International partnerships are being used to reduce adoption risk

Health NZ’s partnership with UCLPartners is intended to exchange evidence, identify promising innovations and learn how technologies have been introduced safely in another public health system. Health NZ has stated that the partnership does not commit it to purchasing or adopting any particular product. ([healthcareitnews.com](https://www.healthcareitnews.com/news/anz/te-whatu-ora-taps-nhs-partner-ai-development?utm_source=openai))

This is a sensible response to New Zealand’s limited scale. The country does not need to independently repeat every international experiment, but it does need to determine whether external evidence transfers to its own populations, institutions and care models.

### 7. Foundations are likely to determine the speed of clinical scale

The Health Digital Investment Plan identifies digital models of care, intelligence and insights, data and interoperability, cybersecurity and modern core systems as connected priorities. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/sites/default/files/2025-11/Health%20Digital%20Investment%20Plan.pdf?utm_source=openai))

That means AI adoption will be constrained by the condition of the health system around it. Fragmented records, poor data quality, ageing devices and insecure interfaces can prevent a technically capable tool from producing reliable clinical value.

## Outlook

Over the next 12–18 months, the most likely areas of progress are:

- Continued expansion of AI scribes into primary care, mental health and aged care.
- Further rollout of Tāmaki Health’s patient-coaching model if engagement and safety results remain acceptable.
- Development and staged testing of AI-enabled mental-health helpline triage.
- Ongoing evaluation of symptom-checker and digital-navigation approaches.
- Local validation and possible implementation of AI-assisted mammogram reading from 2027.
- More AI tools entering HealthX’s assessment and international learning pipeline.
- Expansion of AI-supported appointment, prescription and preventive-care workflows in private primary care.
- Greater integration of AI products with practice-management systems and national data services.
- More formal reporting through Health NZ’s AI governance and evaluation processes.
- Clinical research results from AI-guided intensive-care treatment programmes.

The least likely near-term development is widespread autonomous diagnosis or treatment. Health NZ policy, Medical Council guidance and the current deployment pattern all support human-supervised use, especially when AI affects a patient’s record, diagnosis, treatment or referral.

The main risks to the outlook are not only technical. They include:

- Loss of public trust after privacy incidents.
- Weak or inconsistent consent practices.
- Poor performance for Māori, Pacific and minority-language communities.
- Vendor lock-in.
- Failure to measure real clinical outcomes.
- Productivity claims that do not survive independent evaluation.
- Clinician over-reliance on plausible but incorrect outputs.
- Insufficient funding for integration, training and monitoring.

## Overall Assessment

New Zealand healthcare has entered a phase of **controlled operationalisation**, but not broad clinical automation.

The evidence supports five conclusions:

1. **AI scribes are the leading mature use case.** They have reached national availability in emergency departments and are spreading through primary care and aged care.
2. **Private primary care is currently the most active innovation environment.** Tāmaki Health demonstrates how documentation, contact-centre automation, patient summaries and coaching can be combined into a broader operating model.
3. **Patient-facing AI is emerging faster than its outcome evidence.** Coaching and triage systems are moving into implementation, but independent evidence on safety, equity and health outcomes remains limited.
4. **Clinical AI is progressing cautiously.** Breast screening, retinal screening and intensive-care research are moving through procurement, validation or trials rather than routine autonomous use.
5. **Governance and infrastructure are becoming the decisive factors.** Health NZ now has a more formal evaluation and approval architecture, while cyber resilience, interoperability, data sovereignty and public trust remain major constraints.

The most accurate description of the sector is therefore:

> **AI assists; clinicians review, decide and remain accountable.**

The strategic question for the next stage is not whether New Zealand healthcare will adopt AI. It already is. The question is whether individual deployments can be converted into safe, equitable and measurable improvements across the health system.
