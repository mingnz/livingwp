# AI in Healthcare in New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper

AI adoption in Aotearoa New Zealand healthcare is moving from isolated pilots to a managed portfolio: documentation is operating nationally, while cancer data, screening, education and workforce projects remain in validation or early deployment.

## Executive Summary

The main change since the 1 October 2026 edition is stronger evidence of breadth and scale, rather than a major shift toward autonomous clinical care.

Health New Zealand’s HealthX programme now reports twelve initiatives, including AI-assisted documentation and remote patient monitoring. Its emergency-department AI scribe has reached 1,250 clinicians across 39 hospitals. This is the clearest evidence of national-scale healthcare AI adoption, although reported benefits remain largely based on clinician experience and programme monitoring rather than independent evaluation. ([Public Service Commission](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/news/creating-positive-change-through-new-approaches-and-technologies)) ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/news/creating-positive-change-through-new-approaches-and-technologies?_searchAnalytics=eyJlbmdpbmVOYW1lIjoicHJvZC10a20iLCJxdWVyeVN0cmluZyI6IiIsImRvY3VtZW50SWQiOiJzaWx2ZXJzdHJpcGVfY21zX21vZGVsX3NpdGV0cmVlXzI2MjciLCJyZXF1ZXN0SWQiOiJCRVlDM3RJYlEyT0ZidUxCN1YxN3V3In0%3D&utm_source=openai))

Other developments are more significant for future capability than for current clinical impact:

- Te Aho o Te Kahu has expanded an AI-assisted pathology-coding pilot after processing 117 cancer pathology cases within a defined scope.
- BreastScreen Aotearoa has moved toward validation and possible piloting of AI mammogram reading and breast-density reporting, but there is no public evidence yet of a national operating deployment.
- The Royal New Zealand College of General Practitioners is testing AI-supported simulated consultations for GP registrars.
- Health NZ’s public guidance and privacy material now describe a wider range of AI uses, while continuing to restrict unapproved generative AI in clinical decision-making and patient advice.
- The proposed Medical Products Bill remains future regulation: drafting is expected to begin by the end of 2026, with introduction planned for 2027.

The overall pattern remains **supervised expansion**. AI is being used or tested to document, structure, prioritise, coach, schedule and support research. There is still no evidence of broad autonomous diagnosis or treatment in New Zealand.

## What Has Changed Since the Last Update

### HealthX has provided a clearer picture of operating scale

The previous edition identified Heidi’s AI scribe as the strongest live deployment. That assessment still holds, but the reported scale is now clearer.

The Public Service Commission says HealthX has introduced twelve digital and AI initiatives since launching in September 2025. These range from AI-assisted clinical documentation to remote patient monitoring. The AI scribe is operating across New Zealand emergency departments and reaches 1,250 clinicians in 39 hospitals. ([Public Service Commission](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/news/creating-positive-change-through-new-approaches-and-technologies)) ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/news/creating-positive-change-through-new-approaches-and-technologies?_searchAnalytics=eyJlbmdpbmVOYW1lIjoicHJvZC10a20iLCJxdWVyeVN0cmluZyI6IiIsImRvY3VtZW50SWQiOiJzaWx2ZXJzdHJpcGVfY21zX21vZGVsX3NpdGV0cmVlXzI2MjciLCJyZXF1ZXN0SWQiOiJCRVlDM3RJYlEyT0ZidUxCN1YxN3V3In0%3D&utm_source=openai))

This is evidence of national operating access, not proof that all twelve initiatives are operating at comparable scale. The public account does not provide a complete register of each initiative, its status, user numbers, clinical scope or evaluation results.

The reported benefits at Middlemore include better documentation, improved clinician wellbeing and more time for patient care. These findings are useful operational signals, but they remain programme- and clinician-reported. They do not yet establish effects on patient safety, length of stay, documentation error rates, clinician retention or total system cost.

The deployment model remains bounded:

- Patients are expected to consent to recording where required.
- The system produces draft documentation.
- Clinicians review and amend the output.
- The responsible clinician remains accountable for the final record.
- The tool is not authorised to make autonomous clinical decisions.

### AI-assisted cancer pathology has moved from proof of concept to expansion

The October CanShare newsletter provides the clearest new example of AI being used to turn unstructured clinical information into reusable national data.

The AI-assisted Structured Anatomical Pathology Reporting pilot, known as ASAP, processed 117 breast and colorectal pathology cases. Pathologists narrated or transferred reports into Health NZ’s Tuhi application, which converted the text into SNOMED CT-coded information and made it available through CanShare. Te Aho o Te Kahu reports near-100% accuracy for SNOMED CT coding within the pilot’s defined scope. ([Te Aho o Te Kahu](https://teaho.govt.nz/index.php/our-work/canshare/canshare-updates/canshare-october-2026-newsletter)) ([teaho.govt.nz](https://teaho.govt.nz/index.php/our-work/canshare/canshare-updates/canshare-october-2026-newsletter))

The important achievement is not that AI diagnosed cancer. The pilot used AI-assisted extraction and coding to make pathology information more structured, interoperable and reusable for:

- Clinical care.
- Multidisciplinary meetings.
- Cancer intelligence.
- Service planning.
- Research.
- Equity monitoring.

The programme is now expanding to structured surgical, pathology, radiology and multidisciplinary-meeting reporting for ovarian cancer and brain tumours. This remains an implementation programme, not an autonomous diagnostic service.

The result should also be treated as provider-reported. The sample was small, the accuracy measure applied within a defined coding scope, and the public report does not provide independent replication, error analysis by cancer type or evidence of impact on patient outcomes.

### BreastScreen AI remains in validation and procurement

Health NZ’s BreastScreen Aotearoa AI procurement is further advanced than a general exploratory announcement, but it has not become evidence of live deployment.

The [GETS tender](https://www.gets.govt.nz/HEALTHNZ/ExternalTenderDetails.htm?id=34058803) sought commercially available systems for mammogram reading and breast-density reporting. The proposed pathway has three stages:

1. Validation trials.
2. Implementation through a pilot.
3. Phased post-implementation rollout and support.

The systems must fit BreastScreen Aotearoa’s existing blind double-reading process. The tender excludes unapproved or still-in-development systems, and requires solutions that can operate with the programme’s existing imaging infrastructure. ([GETS](https://www.gets.govt.nz/HEALTHNZ/ExternalTenderDetails.htm?id=34058803)) ([gets.govt.nz](https://www.gets.govt.nz/HEALTHNZ/ExternalTenderDetails.htm?id=34058803&utm_source=openai))

The tender closed on 22 June 2026. The public tender record reviewed for this edition does not show a supplier award or confirm that a system is operating in production.

A recent Health and Disability Commissioner decision also indicates that Health NZ is still assessing the benefits, costs, requirements and timing of breast-density measurement. The decision records an expectation that this work could take three years, with AI forming one of several workstreams. ([Health and Disability Commissioner](https://www.hdc.org.nz/decisions/search-decisions/2026/cas-14382-g8m0d0/)) ([hdc.org.nz](https://www.hdc.org.nz/decisions/search-decisions/2026/cas-14382-g8m0d0/?utm_source=openai))

The practical conclusion is unchanged: BreastScreen AI is a serious national pathway, but it remains a validation and implementation project rather than a deployed clinical service.

### Health NZ’s public AI inventory is becoming more explicit

Health NZ’s current privacy statement lists several AI-related activities:

- Transcription of letters and clinical reports.
- Automatic transcription and summarisation of consultations.
- Document summarisation and generation for staff.
- Knowledge and training tools, including chatbots and generative AI.
- Preliminary review of images, X-rays, scans and mammograms.

Health NZ says many of these activities remain in pilot phases and that information generated by AI which could affect a patient record or clinical decision is reviewed by the responsible clinician. It also says that, where AI operates in a closed Health NZ environment, personal information does not leave Health NZ’s technology or become available to train commercial AI systems. ([Health NZ privacy statement](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/privacy/privacy-statement)) ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/privacy/privacy-statement?utm_source=openai))

This is a useful update because it distinguishes the health system’s AI portfolio from the better-publicised AI scribe rollout. It also reinforces the need to distinguish between an acknowledged use case, a limited pilot and routine service operation. Public information does not yet provide enough detail to establish the maturity or scale of each listed activity.

### The regulatory timetable has become clearer

The previous edition correctly identified the Medical Products Bill as the intended future framework for software as a medical device, but its timetable was uncertain.

The Ministry of Health’s 2025/26 annual report now says drafting is expected to commence by the end of 2026, with introduction planned for 2027. The proposed framework is intended to include risk-proportionate regulation of medical-device software, including artificial intelligence used for a therapeutic purpose. ([Ministry of Health annual report](https://www.health.govt.nz/system/files/2026-10/ministry-health-annual-report-year-ended-30-june-2026.pdf)) ([health.govt.nz](https://www.health.govt.nz/system/files/2026-10/ministry-health-annual-report-year-ended-30-june-2026.pdf?utm_source=openai))

This is progress in policy development, not a change in current legal status. The Bill has not yet become an operative AI-health regulation regime. Current healthcare AI continues to be governed through privacy law, professional obligations, clinical governance, procurement controls, consumer law and any existing medical-device requirements that may apply.

### New activity is appearing in medical education and information exchange

The Royal New Zealand College of General Practitioners has begun a pilot with New Zealand-built platform Gestalt. GP registrars, including international medical graduates, use simulated consultations, work through differential diagnoses and receive structured feedback. The College says it will assess engagement, user experience and educator feedback before deciding whether the tool should have a wider role in training. ([RNZCGP](https://www.rnzcgp.org.nz/news/media-releases/college-of-gps-and-gestalt-trial-ai-powered-practice-for-future-gps/)) ([rnzcgp.org.nz](https://www.rnzcgp.org.nz/news/media-releases/college-of-gps-and-gestalt-trial-ai-powered-practice-for-future-gps/))

ACC has also completed a request for information about its Medical Notes Service. The RFI sought information about secure digital requests and transfers of medical information, privacy, security, delivery models and future innovation opportunities. It was explicitly market research and not a procurement process or contract award. ([GETS](https://www.gets.govt.nz/ACC/ExternalTenderDetails.htm?id=35045525)) ([gets.govt.nz](https://www.gets.govt.nz/ACC/ExternalTenderDetails.htm?id=35045525))

The ACC activity is not evidence of an AI deployment. It does, however, show how the health sector’s AI pathway depends on more basic digital information flows.

## Current State of AI Adoption

### Documentation is the only clearly scaled national use case

AI-assisted documentation remains the most mature application in New Zealand healthcare.

The emergency-department rollout has national reach, a defined workflow and a clear human-review requirement. The latest public account confirms use across 39 hospitals and 1,250 clinicians. ([Public Service Commission](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/news/creating-positive-change-through-new-approaches-and-technologies)) ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/news/creating-positive-change-through-new-approaches-and-technologies?_searchAnalytics=eyJlbmdpbmVOYW1lIjoicHJvZC10a20iLCJxdWVyeVN0cmluZyI6IiIsImRvY3VtZW50SWQiOiJzaWx2ZXJzdHJpcGVfY21zX21vZGVsX3NpdGV0cmVlXzI2MjciLCJyZXF1ZXN0SWQiOiJCRVlDM3RJYlEyT0ZidUxCN1YxN3V3In0%3D&utm_source=openai))

Primary-care adoption is broader than the public national rollout but more fragmented. A 2025 peer-reviewed survey of New Zealand primary-care providers found that 40% of respondents had experience with AI scribes. Among those with experience, 80% found the tools helpful or very helpful, while 56% said the tool changed consultation dynamics. The main concerns were legal and ethical compliance, data security, errors or omissions, and information leaving New Zealand. Only 59% of respondents with experience reported seeking patient consent. ([University of Otago research repository](https://ourarchive.otago.ac.nz/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Using-AI-scribes-in-New-Zealand/9926780884301891)) ([ourarchive.otago.ac.nz](https://ourarchive.otago.ac.nz/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Using-AI-scribes-in-New-Zealand/9926780884301891))

The survey was exploratory and based on a self-selected sample from 2024, so it is not an adoption-rate estimate. Its significance is that it documents both enthusiasm and uneven governance in a part of the sector where use grew faster than formal national measurement.

### Patient engagement remains promising but weakly evaluated

The Tāmaki Health and Groov AI Health Coach pilot remains the strongest publicly reported example of patient-facing AI in primary care.

The provider reported that AI-supported outreach engaged seven times more priority patients than a comparable previous human-only text campaign, generated 105 additional patient touchpoints and increased collection of patient-reported information by 8%. Māori and Pacific peoples represented 56% of enrolments. ([Tāmaki Health](https://www.tamakihealth.co.nz/news/early-t%C4%81maki-health-trial-shows-human-plus-ai-model-reaching-7x-more-priority-patients))

These results remain preliminary and provider-reported. There is no public controlled evaluation demonstrating improved diabetes control, reduced hospital use or durable improvement in health equity.

The use case also carries a different risk profile from documentation. A clinician can correct a draft note before it enters the record. A patient-facing system may shape behaviour or delay escalation before a clinical team becomes involved. The model therefore requires active monitoring of:

- Escalation for acute symptoms.
- Mental-health and self-harm content.
- Language and cultural fit.
- Digital exclusion.
- False reassurance.
- Patient understanding of the tool’s limits.
- Safety for people with complex or unstable conditions.

### Imaging is advancing through infrastructure and validation

Imaging is the leading candidate for the next infrastructure-scale phase of AI adoption.

The national radiology modernisation procurement described in the previous edition remains open to interpretation: it includes an imaging-AI orchestrator but does not itself establish deployment. BreastScreen Aotearoa’s separate procurement similarly creates a route for AI-assisted mammogram reading, but the public record does not yet show that validation has translated into routine clinical use.

This staged approach is appropriate for a high-consequence use case. It allows the health system to assess:

- Performance against historical cases.
- Prospective comparison with radiologists.
- Performance across Māori, Pacific, Asian and other populations.
- Integration with different imaging systems.
- Effects on recall rates and interval cancers.
- Human override and escalation.
- Model updates and change control.

The key risk is that capacity gains could be measured without showing who benefits. Breast-screening coverage is not evenly distributed across ethnic groups, so an AI system that makes reading more efficient will not automatically improve access or equity. This concern is also highlighted in recent New Zealand policy analysis of AI and breast screening. ([BERL](https://www.berl.co.nz/economic-insights/risks-and-opportunities-artificial-intelligence-ai-healthcare)) ([berl.co.nz](https://www.berl.co.nz/economic-insights/risks-and-opportunities-artificial-intelligence-ai-healthcare))

### Structured data capture may be more immediately valuable than autonomous diagnosis

The ASAP cancer pathology pilot illustrates a less visible but strategically important direction.

AI is being used to extract structured information from reports and align it with national terminology and interoperability standards. This may create value before AI is trusted to make or recommend a diagnosis. Better data can support multidisciplinary review, service planning, research and equity monitoring without transferring final clinical responsibility to an algorithm.

This pattern is likely to be repeated elsewhere. In many settings, the first practical benefit of AI may come from converting information into a form that health systems can use reliably, rather than from replacing specialist judgement.

### Workforce optimisation remains at pilot stage

The HealthX and RosterLab pilot continues to test AI-assisted rostering for medical radiation technologists and related teams.

RosterLab’s earlier work at Middlemore reportedly reduced roster-production and management time from about 120 hours a month to less than 20 hours. Those figures are company-reported and relate to previous work. The current HealthX pilot has not yet produced public independent results on staffing gaps, overtime, fairness, fatigue, service capacity or staff satisfaction. ([University of Auckland](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/09/15/the-hidden-healthcare-problem-rosterlab-is-trying-to-solve.html)) ([auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/09/15/the-hidden-healthcare-problem-rosterlab-is-trying-to-solve.html?utm_source=openai))

The use case remains strategically important because workforce operations can affect patient access without placing an algorithm directly in the diagnostic or treatment chain. It is also a reminder that “AI adoption” includes optimisation and administrative systems, not only clinical decision support.

### Clinical decision support remains research-led

The REVOLUTION trial remains the most ambitious New Zealand-linked clinical AI evaluation. It is designed to test whether machine-learning guidance can improve personalised oxygen therapy for critically ill patients across 50 intensive-care units in New Zealand and Australia, with more than 24,000 patients expected to be recruited. ([Health Research Council](https://www.hrc.govt.nz/news-and-events/major-nz-led-clinical-trial-test-ai-guided-treatment-critically-ill-patients)) ([hrc.govt.nz](https://www.hrc.govt.nz/news-and-events/major-nz-led-clinical-trial-test-ai-guided-treatment-critically-ill-patients?utm_source=openai))

New research activity is also expanding into cardiac imaging. University of Auckland researcher Debbie Zhao has received Heart Foundation funding to investigate AI and computer modelling for recognising and measuring heart structures in echocardiograms. The work is intended to reduce manual workload and improve consistency, but it is a research project rather than a clinical deployment. ([University of Auckland](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/10/07/heart-foundation-grants-for-university.html)) ([auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/10/07/heart-foundation-grants-for-university.html))

The distinction remains essential:

- A funded trial is not a live service.
- A model-development project is not a validated clinical tool.
- A procurement is not deployment.
- A vendor-reported accuracy result is not evidence of patient benefit.

## Governance, Policy and Regulation

### Health NZ has formalised internal AI oversight

Health NZ’s National Artificial Intelligence and Algorithm Expert Advisory Group is responsible for oversight and advice across AI research, procurement, partnerships and implementation.

Its stated functions include:

- Reviewing AI initiatives using Health NZ data.
- Assessing clinical utility, ethics, equity and Māori health implications.
- Advising on legal, technical and operational risks.
- Establishing a register and review process for algorithms in use.
- Setting expectations for communication with staff and the public.
- Working with appropriate Māori governance bodies.

([Health NZ](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/who-we-are/expert-groups-and-networks/expert-groups/national-artificial-intelligence-and-algorithm-expert-advisory-group)) ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/who-we-are/expert-groups-and-networks/expert-groups/national-artificial-intelligence-and-algorithm-expert-advisory-group?utm_source=openai))

This is a material governance capability. Its effectiveness will depend on whether the register becomes visible and maintained, whether projects can be paused or rejected, and whether monitoring continues after procurement and implementation.

### Generative AI guidance remains cautious

Health NZ’s current guidance says staff and contractors must not enter personal, confidential or sensitive information into unapproved large language models or generative AI tools. It also prohibits use of generative AI for clinical decisions or personalised advice to patients.

The guidance says users remain responsible for checking generated information and must seek advice from the national advisory group for proposed uses. It also states that Health NZ does not currently provide a private large language model for general use. ([Health NZ](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-professionals/guidance-standards/topic/digital-technologies/using-generative-ai-and-large-language-models)) ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-professionals/guidance-standards/topic/digital-technologies/using-generative-ai-and-large-language-models?utm_source=openai))

This creates a clear distinction between approved, bounded tools such as the AI scribe and general-purpose experimentation by staff. The existence of an approved AI deployment should not be interpreted as permission to use public chatbots with patient information.

### Privacy and cybersecurity are now part of clinical safety

The 2025 Manage My Health breach and subsequent compliance notices remain relevant to AI adoption because AI adds data flows, suppliers, cloud environments, application interfaces and subcontractors to already complex health-information systems.

The October University of Auckland analysis of agentic AI and cybersecurity argued that organisations will need to test not only conventional attackers but also AI systems capable of adapting their behaviour and navigating digital interfaces. The analysis specifically connected this risk to the security improvements required after the New Zealand health-data breach. ([University of Auckland](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/10/07/medicare-cybersecurity-ai-agents.html)) ([auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/10/07/medicare-cybersecurity-ai-agents.html?utm_source=openai))

For healthcare organisations, the practical requirements include:

- Strong identity and access management.
- Multifactor authentication.
- Least-privilege access for AI tools and agents.
- Audit logs that capture system actions.
- Clear data-retention and deletion rules.
- Supplier and subcontractor assurance.
- Model and prompt-injection testing.
- A safe way to suspend or disable the system.
- Incident reporting that covers both privacy and clinical harm.

### Medical-device regulation is still future-facing

The proposed Medical Products Bill is intended to regulate software and artificial intelligence when used for a therapeutic purpose. The Ministry’s published policy material describes software-as-a-medical-device settings as part of the proposed framework. ([Ministry of Health](https://www.health.govt.nz/regulation-legislation/our-legislation/regulatory-documents/innovative-medical-products-and-regulatory-pathways-to-market)) ([health.govt.nz](https://www.health.govt.nz/regulation-legislation/our-legislation/regulatory-documents/innovative-medical-products-and-regulatory-pathways-to-market?utm_source=openai))

The Bill has not yet been introduced. Until the framework is enacted and implemented, organisations must work across existing regimes. This creates uncertainty for systems that sit between administrative software, clinical decision support and regulated medical devices.

The absence of a standalone AI Act does not mean healthcare AI is unregulated. It means obligations are distributed and may depend on the tool’s purpose, data, deployment setting and level of clinical influence.

### Patient communication is becoming an implementation control

Patient communication is most developed in imaging, where professional bodies have published resources explaining how AI may support imaging and radiotherapy.

The relevant question is not simply whether AI is used, but what role it plays:

- Recording a consultation.
- Producing a draft note.
- Prioritising a referral.
- Reconstructing or enhancing an image.
- Providing a second read.
- Measuring breast density.
- Suggesting a clinical action.
- Communicating directly with a patient.

Consent and explanation should be proportionate to that role. A transcription tool may require a different communication approach from an AI system that influences diagnosis, treatment or access to care.

## Trends

### 1. Adoption is widening before clinical authority deepens

AI is now visible in documentation, patient engagement, rostering, pathology data, imaging procurement, medical education and research. Yet most applications remain assistive.

The sector is adding tools faster than it is transferring responsibility to them. This reflects a rational risk gradient: administrative and operational tools are easier to supervise than systems that make or materially influence clinical decisions.

### 2. The workflow is becoming more important than the model

The strongest projects connect AI to a defined workflow:

- Scribes connect conversations to clinical records.
- ASAP connects narrative pathology reports to coded cancer information.
- BreastScreen proposals must integrate with existing double-reading processes.
- RosterLab connects workforce preferences and service constraints to staffing schedules.

This suggests that implementation quality, integration and change management may matter more than marginal improvements in model capability.

### 3. Structured data is emerging as a strategic priority

The CanShare work shows how AI can support national data infrastructure. Standardised information is valuable not only for analytics but also for multidisciplinary care, research, planning and equity monitoring.

This is particularly important in New Zealand, where data is distributed across primary care, hospitals, laboratories, screening programmes and community services. AI cannot compensate for missing or poorly structured information, but it may reduce some of the manual work required to make existing information usable.

### 4. Evidence remains concentrated in productivity and engagement

The public evidence base is strongest for:

- Time saved.
- Documentation workload.
- Staff-reported experience.
- Patient engagement.
- Data-extraction accuracy within a defined task.

It remains much weaker for:

- Reduced mortality.
- Improved disease control.
- Fewer hospital admissions.
- Better diagnostic outcomes.
- Long-term workforce retention.
- Improved equity.
- Reduced total cost after implementation and maintenance.

This is not a reason to reject all adoption. It is a reason to avoid presenting operational indicators as proof of clinical benefit.

### 5. Equity is becoming a technical and operational requirement

Equity cannot be assessed only through a general statement that a system should be fair.

The BreastScreen pathway raises questions about performance across ethnicities and how additional capacity will be allocated. The Tāmaki Health pilot raises questions about language, digital access and sustained engagement. The pathology programme raises questions about whether national data structures accurately represent all populations and cancer pathways.

Organisations should expect to provide evidence on:

- Māori and Pacific performance.
- Rural and remote reliability.
- Language and accessibility.
- Digital exclusion.
- Representation in validation datasets.
- Māori data governance.
- Patient understanding and choice.
- Whether benefits reach people who are currently underserved.

### 6. Security is shifting from data protection to system behaviour

Traditional health-data security asks who can access information and whether it is disclosed appropriately. Agentic systems add a second question: what actions can an AI system take once it has access?

This matters for systems that can search records, call tools, write to applications, generate referrals, send messages or alter workflows. The safety boundary must cover not only the model’s output but also the actions it is permitted to take.

### 7. National infrastructure is advancing, but evaluation capacity is scarce

Health NZ now has a formal AI advisory group, HealthX provides an adoption pathway, and national programmes are using interoperability standards and shared data platforms.

The constraint is likely to be implementation capacity. Health services need clinical informatics, cybersecurity, data engineering, Māori governance, procurement, change management and evaluation expertise. Without those capabilities, pilots may proliferate without producing comparable evidence or reusable implementation knowledge.

### 8. Education is becoming an early adoption pathway

The GP registrar pilot indicates that AI may become part of workforce development before it becomes a major part of clinical decision-making.

Training applications can provide repeatable practice, feedback and simulation while preserving a clear boundary between education and patient care. They also offer a relatively safe environment for examining hallucinations, bias, cultural fit and over-reliance.

## Outlook

The most plausible developments over the next 12 to 18 months are:

- Supplier selection or further validation activity for BreastScreen Aotearoa AI.
- Continued procurement and design work for national radiology modernisation.
- Expansion of the ASAP pathology-data programme into additional cancer pathways.
- More HealthX deployments in documentation, remote monitoring, workforce operations and diagnostic support.
- Publication of outcomes from the RosterLab, Gestalt and Tāmaki Health pilots.
- Wider use of AI registers, privacy impact assessments and clinical-assurance processes.
- Further primary-care adoption of AI scribes and integrated practice-management tools.
- Drafting of the Medical Products Bill and possible introduction in 2027.
- Greater scrutiny of model updates, data residency, subcontractors and agentic capabilities.
- Progression of the REVOLUTION trial and other prospective clinical studies.

The most likely near-term pattern is continued supervised augmentation. New Zealand is unlikely to see widespread autonomous diagnosis or treatment before the health system has stronger evidence, clearer regulation and more mature monitoring infrastructure.

The strategic risk is not simply that the health system will adopt too little AI. It is that disconnected tools will be introduced faster than organisations can measure their effects, secure their data, manage changes and ensure that benefits reach underserved communities.

## Overall Assessment

AI adoption in New Zealand healthcare is now more operational and more diverse than it was a year ago, but it is not yet clinically transformative.

The sector has:

- A nationally operating AI documentation service in emergency departments.
- A growing but fragmented primary-care AI-scribe market.
- Patient-facing AI coaching in early supervised use.
- National imaging and breast-screening pathways moving through procurement and validation.
- AI-assisted pathology data extraction connected to national cancer information systems.
- Workforce-rostering pilots.
- AI-supported clinical education.
- Research into AI-guided intensive-care treatment and cardiac imaging.
- A formal Health NZ advisory and assurance structure.
- A clearer policy timetable for future software-as-a-medical-device regulation.

The evidence still supports a cautious hierarchy:

1. **Operating at national scale:** AI-assisted documentation in emergency departments.
2. **Operating in selected services:** primary-care scribes and patient-engagement tools.
3. **In pilot or validation:** breast imaging, pathology reporting, rostering and GP education.
4. **In research:** AI-guided treatment, predictive imaging and other clinical decision-support applications.
5. **Without evidence of broad deployment:** autonomous diagnosis or treatment.

The most important development is the emergence of a more coherent adoption system around the technology. HealthX, the AI advisory group, national data programmes, procurement frameworks and proposed medical-device regulation are beginning to connect experimentation with governance.

The next test is whether that system can produce independent evidence, not just more deployments. Progress should be measured by:

- Reliable use in real workflows.
- Patient safety and clinical outcomes.
- Equity across Māori, Pacific, rural and other populations.
- Data and cybersecurity resilience.
- Transparent human accountability.
- Evidence that released capacity improves access rather than only throughput.
- The ability to monitor, correct and stop systems when they fail.

New Zealand’s healthcare AI trajectory is best described as **nationally coordinated, clinically supervised expansion under evidence and infrastructure constraints**. The sector is moving beyond experimentation, but the strongest case for AI remains assistance with documentation, coordination and information management—not autonomous clinical authority.
