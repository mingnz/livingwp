# AI in Healthcare in New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper

AI adoption in New Zealand healthcare is moving into controlled, clinician-supervised use. Documentation tools are operating at scale, while patient-facing AI, screening and clinical decision support remain in early deployment, evaluation or research.

## Executive Summary

As of 21 August 2026, New Zealand healthcare has one clear example of national-scale AI deployment: ambient documentation in public emergency departments. Adoption is also expanding through primary care, aged residential care and selected patient-coaching services.

The evidence does not support a conclusion that healthcare is becoming broadly automated. Instead, the sector is developing a layered model:

- **Operating now:** AI scribes and documentation assistants.
- **Early deployment:** patient coaching and other bounded conversational tools.
- **Under evaluation:** symptom checkers, mental-health triage and breast-screening AI.
- **In research:** AI-guided treatment and advanced medical imaging.
- **Not authorised as a general operating model:** autonomous diagnosis, treatment or care decisions.

The strategic shift is from isolated experimentation towards **institutionalised, supervised adoption**, with greater attention to evaluation, consent, equity, interoperability, cybersecurity and post-deployment accountability.

## What Has Changed Since the Last Update

The previous edition was updated on 18 August 2026. There has been no new healthcare-specific national AI go-live or clinical outcome announcement published between 19 and 21 August. The latest material therefore mainly strengthens or qualifies the previous assessment rather than changing its direction.

### The national evaluation framework predates its July public announcement

The previous edition described Health NZ’s AI evaluation framework as introduced in July. The more precise timeline is that the framework was endorsed by Health NZ’s Executive Leadership Team in **March 2026** and publicly reported on 28 July. It is now being used to assess proposed AI tools across Health NZ services. ([transform.auckland.ac.nz](https://www.transform.auckland.ac.nz/2026/04/30/collaboratively-developing-an-evaluation-framework-for-ai-tools-in-the-aotearoa-new-zealand-health-sector/?utm_source=openai))

This distinction matters. The change is not simply the publication of guidance; it is the move from developing a framework to using it as part of Health NZ’s decision process.

### New primary-care evidence indicates faster adoption, but not national prevalence

A 2025 survey of 256 primary-care participants found that:

- 68% reported using AI tools.
- 40% reported using AI daily.
- 50% reported using note-taking or scribe tools.
- 16% reported using virtual assistants or chatbots.

Privacy, data storage, consent, reliability, cost and integration with practice-management systems remained major concerns. The survey was organised by primary-care sector bodies and was not a probability sample of all New Zealand practices, so its results should be treated as an adoption signal rather than a national prevalence estimate. ([gpnz.org.nz](https://gpnz.org.nz/media-releases/joint-media-release-round-two-ai-in-primary-care-survey-highlights-growing-adoption/))

This newer pulse-check complements, rather than replaces, the earlier University of Otago survey of 197 respondents. That study found that 40% had experience with an AI scribe, but only 59% of those reporting relevant responses said they had sought patient consent. The different samples, dates and questions mean the results should not be combined into a single adoption rate. ([doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1071/HC25079?utm_source=openai))

### Professional expectations are now clearer

The Medical Council of New Zealand published guidance in March 2026 stating that doctors remain responsible for AI-assisted clinical decisions and must check AI output before using it in care or clinical records.

The guidance specifically identifies informed consent requirements for:

- Recording a consultation with an AI scribe.
- Sharing personal details outside the primary medical record.
- Using identifiable information for AI training.
- Using AI in a significant way in diagnosis, treatment or care delivery.

It also says patients should know when AI is used in their care and prohibits doctors from using AI to impersonate themselves through avatars or chatbots. ([mcnz.org.nz](https://www.mcnz.org.nz/assets/standards/Guidance-on-using-artificial-intelligence-AI-in-patient-care-March-2026.pdf))

### The health-regulation position remains transitional

The proposed Medical Products Bill had not been introduced as of 21 August 2026. Government material indicates that it is intended to establish risk-proportionate regulation for some medical software and AI-enabled medical devices, particularly where products have a therapeutic purpose. The current policy direction is not to regulate general administrative tools such as note-taking software or AI scribes as medical devices. ([health.govt.nz](https://www.health.govt.nz/system/files/2026-06/H2026079057-Aide-Memoire-Minister-Costello-meeting-with-representatives-of-American-Chamber-of-Commerce-Australia-%28AmCHam%29.pdf))

The result is a transitional environment: clinical governance, professional standards, privacy law, procurement controls and institutional approval currently carry much of the burden.

## Current State of AI Adoption

### Emergency departments: the most mature national deployment

AI scribe technology is now live across all New Zealand emergency departments, with Health NZ reporting access for approximately 1,250 doctors and frontline staff. The deployment followed pilots in Hawke’s Bay and Whanganui and is intended to reduce documentation time while leaving clinicians responsible for reviewing and approving the final note. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

Reported pilot benefits include:

- Reduced time spent preparing clinical documentation.
- More direct interaction between clinicians and patients.
- The capacity to see approximately one additional patient per shift in the initial trial.
- Reduced after-hours administrative work.

These results come primarily from Health NZ, clinicians and the vendor. They are operationally relevant but should not be treated as independent evidence of improved clinical outcomes.

The deployment also demonstrates the limits of the current model:

- AI-generated notes remain drafts.
- Clinicians must identify omissions, errors and fabricated details.
- Patients may need to consent to recording.
- The tool is intended for documentation, not diagnosis or treatment.
- Access to a system does not establish that every clinician is using it routinely.

The most accurate description is therefore **national access to a human-reviewed documentation service**, not autonomous clinical AI.

### Primary care: widespread experimentation and growing product integration

Primary care appears to have the broadest grassroots adoption outside emergency medicine. The available surveys indicate that scribes are the dominant use case, followed by patient communication, administrative support and virtual assistants. ([gpnz.org.nz](https://gpnz.org.nz/media-releases/joint-media-release-round-two-ai-in-primary-care-survey-highlights-growing-adoption/))

Commercial platforms are also moving beyond standalone transcription. Medtech AI, launched in February 2026, is integrated with Medtech Evolution and is designed to use patient-record context to produce summaries, clinical documentation and correspondence. The platform requires clinician review before information is written back to the patient record. This is a vendor announcement and does not demonstrate system-wide adoption or independently measured benefit. ([medtechglobal.com](https://medtechglobal.com/nz/medtech-ai-launch/?utm_source=openai))

The primary-care pattern is therefore mixed:

- Adoption is advancing faster than formal national measurement.
- Many tools remain separate from practice-management systems.
- Practices vary in their understanding of data storage, consent and vendor terms.
- Smaller providers may face greater cost, training and integration barriers.
- Locally relevant performance, including accents, clinical terminology, te reo Māori and Pacific languages, remains an open issue.

The sector’s own survey work suggests that the next adoption barrier is not interest in AI but **whether tools can be integrated without creating additional workflow, privacy or liability problems**. ([gpnz.org.nz](https://gpnz.org.nz/media-releases/joint-media-release-round-two-ai-in-primary-care-survey-highlights-growing-adoption/))

### Aged residential care: documentation is spreading beyond hospitals

Metlifecare has moved from a four-month trial of the HEIDI AI scribe at Parkside Village to a phased rollout across three additional care homes.

The system transcribes conversations between nurses, residents and whānau and generates structured notes for review. Metlifecare reports improvements in documentation quality, staff efficiency and resident interaction. All generated notes remain subject to staff review and approval. ([metlifecare.co.nz](https://www.metlifecare.co.nz/news/metlifecare-heidi-scribe-tool?utm_source=openai))

This is a useful example of the portability of the documentation use case. Aged residential care has many of the same pressures as hospitals and general practice—high documentation loads, workforce constraints and the need for continuity across shifts—but the context creates additional requirements around consent, cognitive impairment, whānau involvement and sensitive conversations.

The rollout is significant as an implementation signal, but the published evidence remains provider-reported. Independent measures of documentation accuracy, resident outcomes, staff workload and equity are not yet available.

### Patient-facing coaching: bounded conversational AI is entering routine service delivery

Tāmaki Health began rolling out an AI Health Coach at selected clinics in August 2026. Developed with Groov, the tool is designed to provide support before, between and after appointments for goals including diabetes management, gout, weight management and anxiety.

Its operating model includes:

- 24/7 digital access.
- Personalised action plans.
- Behaviour-change support.
- Referral to human health coaches or clinical teams where additional support is required.
- Co-design input from patients, clinicians and health coaches.

The provider describes the service as an extension of the existing workforce rather than a replacement for it. Expansion will depend on patient uptake, engagement, clinical feedback and outcomes evaluation. ([healthcareitnews.com](https://www.healthcareitnews.com/news/anz/tamaki-health-extends-health-coaching-ai?utm_source=openai))

Groov has reported more than 4,500 previous conversations and an average helpfulness rating of 8.5 out of 10. These are self-reported or provider-reported indicators, not independent evidence of improved health outcomes, reduced demand or safe performance across population groups.

The significance of the deployment is its position between administrative automation and clinical care. It does not formally diagnose or prescribe, but it does interact directly with patients and can influence whether, when and where they seek care.

### Symptom checking and mental-health triage remain programmes, not mature national services

Health NZ is conducting a controlled proof of concept for online symptom-checking tools. The evaluation is intended to examine:

- Accuracy and effectiveness.
- User experience.
- Changes in patient behaviour.
- Effects on care pathways.
- Effects on demand for health services.
- Safety, quality and equity.

Health NZ says the findings will inform a decision in mid-to-late 2027 on whether to proceed to formal procurement. This is not evidence of a nationally deployed symptom checker. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/news-and-updates/symptom-checker-update-july-2026?utm_source=openai))

The Government has also allocated $3.35 million over four years to develop and implement an AI-enabled triage and referral tool across publicly funded mental-health and addiction helplines. The funding supports development and implementation activity; it does not show that a tool is already operating nationwide. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/telehealth-investment-deliver-faster-access-support?utm_source=openai))

Both programmes move AI closer to the health system’s front door. That increases the importance of safety-netting, escalation, cultural competence and clear communication about the limits of automated guidance.

### Screening and diagnostics: significant pipeline, limited operational evidence

Breast screening is the most prominent planned clinical deployment. Health NZ is pursuing an AI tool that could perform one of the independent mammogram reads, with clinicians retaining responsibility for diagnosis, follow-up and treatment decisions. The Government has described the process as careful and evidence-based, but the public record still concerns procurement, validation and implementation planning rather than a completed national rollout. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-breast-screening-tool-one-step-closer?utm_source=openai))

The diabetic-retinal-screening proof of concept provides a more cautionary implementation example. A New Zealand Medical Journal report found that the project encountered barriers involving:

- Legacy systems and poor interoperability.
- Manual and paper-based workflows.
- Local grading requirements that did not map neatly to overseas tools.
- Workforce readiness.
- Changes to models of care.
- Resourcing and organisational support.

The project illustrates why overseas accuracy studies are not enough. A tool can perform well in controlled testing and still fail to produce a scalable service when it cannot connect reliably to existing systems or fit the local clinical pathway. ([nzmj.org.nz](https://nzmj.org.nz/journal/vol-139-no-1637/implementation-of-artificial-intelligence-in-healthcare-in-aotearoa-new-zealand-learnings-from-the-diabetic-retinal-screening-us?utm_source=openai))

### Clinical decision support: research is ahead of deployment

The REVOLUTION trial is the strongest current example of New Zealand-led research moving from prediction towards treatment evaluation. Funded through a nearly $5 million HRC programme grant, it is planned to involve 50 intensive-care units in New Zealand and Australia and recruit more than 24,000 patients.

The trial will test whether machine-learning-guided oxygen targets improve survival for critically ill patients receiving life support. It is therefore evaluating the effect of AI-informed treatment, not simply the accuracy of a model. ([hrc.govt.nz](https://hrc.govt.nz/news-and-events/major-nz-led-clinical-trial-test-ai-guided-treatment-critically-ill-patients?utm_source=openai))

This remains research activity, not routine clinical deployment. Its importance lies in the design: randomised evaluation, clinically meaningful outcomes and explicit testing of whether algorithmic recommendations improve care.

## Governance, Policy and Regulation

### Health NZ has a formal oversight mechanism

Health NZ’s National Artificial Intelligence and Algorithm Expert Advisory Group guides and endorses AI projects and research involving Health NZ. Its remit includes:

- Clinical utility and feasibility.
- Patient and population perspectives.
- Ethics and transparency.
- Equity, bias and discrimination.
- Māori health and Māori data sovereignty.
- Privacy, confidentiality and security.
- Contracts, accountability, intellectual property, audit and monitoring.

Health NZ states that AI development or implementation plans must be registered with the group. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/who-we-are/expert-groups-and-networks/expert-groups/artificial-intelligence-and-algorithm-expert-advisory-group?utm_source=openai))

The national pre-implementation framework adds a more structured assessment layer. It was developed through a review of 31 international frameworks and co-design involving researchers, health-sector participants and other experts. It takes a whole-of-system view, rather than evaluating model accuracy in isolation. ([transform.auckland.ac.nz](https://www.transform.auckland.ac.nz/2026/04/30/collaboratively-developing-an-evaluation-framework-for-ai-tools-in-the-aotearoa-new-zealand-health-sector/?utm_source=openai))

### Health NZ’s operating policy remains cautious

Health NZ guidance tells employees and contractors not to:

- Enter sensitive patient or organisational information into unapproved generative-AI tools.
- Use general-purpose LLMs for clinical decisions.
- Use those tools to provide personalised clinical advice to patients.

Where approved AI is used, staff remain responsible for checking its output. Health NZ’s public privacy statement says AI-generated information that may affect patient records or clinical decisions must be reviewed by the responsible clinician. It also says many uses remain pilots or are being tested on a limited basis. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-professionals/guidance-standards/topic/digital-technologies/using-generative-ai-and-large-language-models?utm_source=openai))

Health NZ describes approved AI environments as closed or controlled, while acknowledging that some short-term disclosure to technology providers may occur for support or service operation. The practical implication is that organisations need to understand the specific data flows of each tool rather than relying on the generic label “secure AI”. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/privacy/privacy-statement?utm_source=openai))

### Professional guidance places responsibility on clinicians

The Medical Council’s March guidance reinforces a human-accountability model:

- AI must be clinically justified and suitable for the patient.
- Doctors must understand the tool’s main capabilities and limitations.
- AI output must be checked before it influences care or enters the record.
- AI use that affects clinical decision-making should be documented.
- Consent is required in specified higher-risk situations.
- Doctors cannot transfer professional responsibility to an AI system.

This provides a clearer baseline for clinicians, but it does not by itself resolve institutional questions about vendor liability, model updates, audit rights, incident reporting or long-term retention of recordings and transcripts. ([mcnz.org.nz](https://www.mcnz.org.nz/assets/standards/Guidance-on-using-artificial-intelligence-AI-in-patient-care-March-2026.pdf))

### New Zealand still lacks a dedicated AI health statute

The OECD’s 2026 assessment describes New Zealand’s current approach as relying on the Health Information Privacy Code, the Medicines Act, clinical governance and procurement rather than a dedicated AI law.

It identifies several gaps:

- No specific regulatory category for high-risk clinical AI.
- Uneven implementation of interoperability standards.
- No national mechanism for monitoring model drift or AI-related safety incidents.
- Variable provider-level data governance.
- Limited requirements to disclose training-data characteristics.
- Ad hoc rather than nationally coordinated AI sandboxes. ([oecd.org](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-economic-surveys-new-zealand-2026_3ec5de98-en/full-report/harnessing-digital-tools-to-improve-health-system-performance_ee123dce.html))

The proposed Medical Products Bill is intended to address part of this gap by regulating certain software as medical devices where it has a therapeutic purpose, such as diagnosis, treatment guidance or disease monitoring. Government advice indicates that AI-enabled medical devices may receive tailored approval pathways, but the bill’s timing remains dependent on broader legislative priorities. ([health.govt.nz](https://www.health.govt.nz/system/files/2026-06/H2026079057-Aide-Memoire-Minister-Costello-meeting-with-representatives-of-American-Chamber-of-Commerce-Australia-%28AmCHam%29.pdf))

### Interoperability and cybersecurity remain adoption prerequisites

The Shared Digital Health Record programme has extended its timeline to allow further checks against the Government Digital Delivery Agency’s information-sharing standard. Immunisation and medication data are expected to be integrated later in 2026, while access to primary-care data is expected from mid-2027. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/news-and-updates/shared-digital-health-record-update-july-2026?utm_source=openai))

This sequencing matters for AI. Documentation tools can operate with relatively narrow data flows. More advanced applications—longitudinal decision support, population-risk prediction and cross-setting care coordination—require reliable access to standardised information across primary, community and hospital services.

Cybersecurity is similarly foundational. The independent review of the Manage My Health breach recommended stronger assurance of third-party suppliers, rather than reliance solely on supplier self-assessment. The same principle applies to AI vendors handling consultation audio, transcripts, clinical records and model inputs. ([health.govt.nz](https://www.health.govt.nz/news/independent-review-recommends-stronger-cyber-security-across-health-system?utm_source=openai))

## Case Studies

### Case Study 1: HEIDI in emergency departments

**Use case:** Ambient transcription and clinical documentation.

**Status:** Live across New Zealand emergency departments, with access reported for approximately 1,250 frontline staff.

**Evidence:** Health NZ and Government reporting indicate reduced documentation time and the potential to see an additional patient per shift during the pilot. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

**What it demonstrates:**

- Documentation is currently the most scalable AI use case.
- Bounded applications are easier to govern than diagnostic systems.
- National procurement and central endorsement can accelerate deployment.
- Clinician review remains essential.
- Security testing must consider misuse and scope expansion, not only intended functionality.

The March 2026 jailbreak exercise showed that a user could manipulate the model into generating content outside its intended scribe role. Health NZ and Heidi reported no patient-data exposure, system impact or patient harm. The incident was contained, but it demonstrated why red-team testing and ongoing monitoring are necessary even for administrative tools used in clinical environments. ([nzherald.co.nz](https://www.nzherald.co.nz/nz/emergency-department-ai-gives-meth-recipe-in-jailbreak-testing/MPVUIDAZTBEKRCZIB46YWQMT7Q/?utm_source=openai))

### Case Study 2: Tāmaki Health and Groov AI Health Coach

**Use case:** Patient self-management and behaviour-change support between appointments.

**Status:** Early rollout at selected clinics.

**Operating model:**

- Digital coaching is available around the clock.
- The system supports defined lifestyle and self-management goals.
- Human health coaches and clinical teams remain available for escalation.
- Further expansion depends on uptake, feedback and outcome evaluation. ([healthcareitnews.com](https://www.healthcareitnews.com/news/anz/tamaki-health-extends-health-coaching-ai?utm_source=openai))

**Assessment:** This is a meaningful move into patient-facing AI, but it remains a bounded service rather than an autonomous clinical pathway. Evidence of benefit is currently based on provider and vendor reporting. The key evaluation questions are whether the service improves sustained behaviour change, reduces avoidable demand, reaches underserved groups and escalates safely when risk is higher than the tool can manage.

### Case Study 3: AI-enabled diabetic-retinal-screening proof of concept

**Use case:** Improving access to diabetic-retinopathy screening, including for Pacific communities.

**Status:** Proof of concept with significant implementation barriers.

**Lessons:**

- Existing clinical and administrative systems may not support real-time AI workflows.
- Overseas grading standards may not align with New Zealand practice.
- Manual workarounds can make a pilot function but prevent scale.
- Workforce readiness and organisational support are as important as model performance.
- Equity objectives require service redesign and community involvement. ([nzmj.org.nz](https://nzmj.org.nz/journal/vol-139-no-1637/implementation-of-artificial-intelligence-in-healthcare-in-aotearoa-new-zealand-learnings-from-the-diabetic-retinal-screening-us?utm_source=openai))

This is one of the clearest New Zealand examples of the difference between a technically plausible use case and a deployable health service.

### Case Study 4: REVOLUTION ICU trial

**Use case:** Machine-learning-guided oxygen therapy for critically ill patients.

**Status:** Funded clinical trial; not routine care.

**Scale:** Planned recruitment of more than 24,000 patients across 50 intensive-care units in New Zealand and Australia.

**Why it matters:** The trial is designed to test whether AI-informed treatment improves survival. That is a stronger standard than demonstrating retrospective prediction accuracy or clinician satisfaction. It may also provide a model for how New Zealand can evaluate higher-risk clinical AI through prospective, outcome-focused research. ([hrc.govt.nz](https://hrc.govt.nz/news-and-events/major-nz-led-clinical-trial-test-ai-guided-treatment-critically-ill-patients?utm_source=openai))

## Trends

### 1. Documentation remains the gateway to adoption

Scribes have a relatively clear value proposition: reduce administrative work while retaining clinical decision-making with qualified staff. They can be evaluated through time, note quality, clinician workload and patient-experience measures without immediately transferring diagnostic authority to a model.

This explains why scribes have progressed faster than autonomous or semi-autonomous clinical systems.

### 2. Patient-facing AI is the next governance test

Health coaching, symptom checking and mental-health triage all interact directly with patients. They can affect behaviour even when they do not formally diagnose or treat.

Their risk profile therefore depends on more than accuracy. It also includes:

- Whether users understand the tool’s limitations.
- Whether urgent symptoms are escalated correctly.
- Whether the system works across languages and cultural contexts.
- Whether digital access is equitable.
- Whether human alternatives remain available.
- Whether patients can identify when they are interacting with AI.

### 3. Adoption evidence is stronger for activity than outcomes

New Zealand now has credible evidence that AI tools are being used or tested. It has much less independent evidence that they improve:

- Clinical outcomes.
- Equity.
- Patient safety.
- Total health-system cost.
- Workforce retention.
- Access for rural communities.
- Long-term patient engagement.

Most reported benefits come from vendors, providers, sector surveys or early pilots. These are useful for identifying signals, but they should not be confused with controlled evaluations or national performance data.

### 4. Governance is moving upstream

The national evaluation framework, NAIAEAG process and Medical Council guidance all push assessment earlier in the lifecycle. The relevant question is increasingly not “Can the model produce a good result?” but:

- Is the use case clinically justified?
- Is the intended role clearly bounded?
- Are the data flows lawful and acceptable?
- Is the tool suitable for the local population?
- Can the organisation monitor it after deployment?
- Can it be paused or withdrawn?
- Who is accountable when it fails?

This is a more mature approach than treating AI as an ordinary software purchase.

### 5. Integration is replacing novelty as the main constraint

New Zealand’s primary-care survey data identifies practice-management integration as a growing concern. The retinal-screening experience shows how legacy systems and manual workflows can prevent scale. The OECD similarly identifies interoperability, vendor lock-in and uneven implementation of standards as system-wide constraints. ([gpnz.org.nz](https://gpnz.org.nz/media-releases/joint-media-release-round-two-ai-in-primary-care-survey-highlights-growing-adoption/))

The next phase of adoption will depend less on the number of available models and more on:

- Secure APIs.
- Standardised terminology.
- Reliable identity and access controls.
- Consistent data structures.
- Practical integration with clinical records.
- Procurement terms that preserve auditability and exit options.

### 6. Local fit and equity are becoming operational requirements

Health NZ guidance specifically identifies risks for Māori, women, older people, disabled people and gender-diverse people, as well as the under-representation of te reo Māori and other minority languages in many generative-AI systems. It also warns that public LLMs may not respect Māori data sovereignty. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-professionals/guidance-standards/topic/digital-technologies/using-generative-ai-and-large-language-models?utm_source=openai))

The retinal-screening case shows how equity-oriented projects can fail without investment in service design, community relationships and local workflow adaptation. Equity cannot be added after the model is selected; it must shape the use case, data, testing and operating model from the beginning.

### 7. The health system is building capability while under financial pressure

The Health Digital Investment Plan and broader digital-infrastructure work create an institutional path for future AI, but implementation capacity remains a concern. The OECD identifies fragmented governance, short budget cycles, workforce capability and uneven delivery capacity as ongoing constraints. ([oecd.org](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-economic-surveys-new-zealand-2026_3ec5de98-en/full-report/harnessing-digital-tools-to-improve-health-system-performance_ee123dce.html))

This creates a tension. Financial pressure increases the appeal of automation, but constrained budgets can also make it harder to fund integration, training, clinical safety work and independent evaluation—the activities that determine whether a pilot is safe and sustainable.

## Outlook

Over the next 12 to 18 months, the most likely developments are:

- Broader use of AI scribes in primary care, mental health and aged care.
- Continued rollout and evaluation of Tāmaki Health’s AI coaching service.
- Development of the AI-enabled mental-health helpline triage and referral tool.
- Controlled testing of symptom-checker products.
- Further procurement and validation activity for breast-screening AI.
- Continued progress on the REVOLUTION trial and other AI-related health research.
- Increased use of Health NZ’s national evaluation framework.
- More formal guidance on approved tools, data flows, monitoring and procurement.
- Integration of additional data into the Shared Digital Health Record.

The less likely near-term development is widespread autonomous diagnosis or treatment. Health NZ policy, professional guidance and the current project pipeline continue to favour clinician-supervised augmentation. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/privacy/privacy-statement?utm_source=openai))

The most important indicators to watch are not the number of pilots announced, but whether projects can demonstrate:

- Safe use in real clinical workflows.
- Measurable patient or workforce benefit.
- Performance across Māori, Pacific, rural and other underserved populations.
- Reliable escalation to human care.
- Clear accountability for errors.
- Independent post-deployment monitoring.
- Sustainable integration with national and local health systems.

## Overall Assessment

As of 21 August 2026, AI adoption in New Zealand healthcare is best described as **selective, supervised and increasingly institutionalised**.

The strongest evidence concerns documentation:

- AI scribes are available across public emergency departments.
- Primary-care adoption is expanding, although national prevalence is not yet known.
- Aged-care providers are beginning to use the same technology.
- Integration with practice-management systems is becoming more important.

The next layer—patient-facing coaching, symptom checking and mental-health triage—is developing but remains comparatively immature. These applications require stronger controls because they influence patient behaviour and access to care directly.

Clinical AI is progressing through screening programmes, medical-imaging research and large prospective trials, but most higher-risk uses remain in procurement, validation or research rather than routine deployment.

New Zealand’s central challenge is no longer whether AI tools exist. It is whether the health system can create the infrastructure, governance, workforce capability and public trust needed to use them safely and equitably.

The operating principle remains:

> **AI assists; clinicians review, decide and remain accountable.**

That principle is not a temporary limitation. For the foreseeable future, it is the foundation of New Zealand’s healthcare AI adoption model.
