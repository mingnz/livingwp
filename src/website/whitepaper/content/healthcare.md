# AI Adoption in Healthcare in New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper  
**Updated: 18 August 2026**  
**Article slug:** `healthcare`  
**Previous update:** 13 July 2026  

## Executive Summary

AI adoption in New Zealand healthcare remains **selective, supervised and implementation-focused**. The most mature nationwide deployment is still ambient documentation, particularly the AI scribe rollout across emergency departments. More than **1,250 emergency-department clinicians and frontline staff** had access to the technology by February 2026, with more than **1,000 additional licences** being progressed, mainly for mental health teams. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

Since the previous update on **13 July 2026**, the most important change has been the further institutionalisation of AI governance:

- Health NZ listed a **28 July 2026** update on a “consistent approach to AI tools backed by new national framework.”
- The Aotearoa-specific AI evaluation framework developed by the University of Auckland’s TRANSFORM programme had already been endorsed by Health NZ’s Executive Leadership Team in March and was being used to assess proposed AI tools across Health NZ services. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-professionals/guidance-standards/topic/digital-technologies?utm_source=openai))
- Health NZ’s proposed symptom-checker programme moved into structured consumer and system-navigation engagement rather than immediate deployment. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/what-we-do/programmes-and-initiatives/symptom-checker?utm_source=openai))
- The BreastScreen Aotearoa AI procurement process progressed from an open procurement announcement to a **closed tender on 22 June 2026**. No public supplier award or implementation announcement had superseded the planned **early 2027** rollout by 18 August. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-breast-screening-tool-one-step-closer?utm_source=openai))
- The University of Auckland strengthened its AI-enabled medical imaging capability with the appointment of an internationally recognised heart-imaging researcher, signalling continued investment in clinical AI research and translation. ([auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/07/20/global-heart-imaging-expert-joins-auckland-initiative.html?utm_source=openai))

The broader assessment is unchanged but now better evidenced: New Zealand is moving from isolated pilots toward **controlled adoption**, but it has not entered an era of broad autonomous clinical AI. AI is being used or evaluated primarily to:

- reduce administrative workload;
- improve documentation and information access;
- support screening and image interpretation;
- assist care navigation and patient education;
- test predictive clinical decision support in research settings.

The OECD’s 2026 assessment is consistent with this picture. New Zealand has established important readiness elements, including national oversight and workforce-capability initiatives, but still faces gaps in interoperability, post-deployment monitoring, and the ability to scale proven tools across the health system. ([oecd.org](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-economic-surveys-new-zealand-2026_3ec5de98-en/full-report/harnessing-digital-tools-to-improve-health-system-performance_ee123dce.html))

## What Has Changed Since 13 July 2026

### 1. AI evaluation became more visibly embedded in Health NZ operating practice

Health NZ’s digital technologies information now records a **28 July 2026** update focused on achieving a consistent national approach to AI tools. This follows the March endorsement of the Aotearoa-specific AI evaluation framework developed through the University of Auckland’s TRANSFORM programme. The framework takes a whole-of-system approach, considering patients and communities, the workforce and the health system before implementation. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-professionals/guidance-standards/topic/digital-technologies?utm_source=openai))

**Why it matters:**

- AI approval is increasingly being treated as a health-system decision rather than a local software purchase.
- Evaluation is expanding beyond accuracy to include workflow fit, equity, safety, acceptability, privacy and implementation cost.
- The approach creates a potential pathway for consistent assessment across hospitals, primary care and other Health NZ services.

### 2. Breast-screening AI procurement reached a clearer milestone

Health NZ’s procurement notice for BreastScreen Aotearoa AI solutions closed on **22 June 2026**. The tender sought tools for:

- AI-assisted mammogram reading;
- breast-density reporting.

The Government’s public position remains that AI would perform one of the two independent reads currently required, while clinicians would retain responsibility for diagnosis and follow-up decisions. The planned rollout remains targeted for **early 2027**, subject to testing and validation. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-breast-screening-tool-one-step-closer?utm_source=openai))

As of 18 August 2026, the public record reviewed for this update did not identify a later announcement naming the preferred supplier or confirming live deployment.

### 3. The symptom-checker programme moved into consultation and design work

Health NZ’s **27 July 2026** symptom-checker update describes a governance structure involving clinicians, digital-health specialists, AI-safety experts and representatives of Māori, Pacific and disabled communities. The next stage includes focus groups with consumers and system navigators to explore:

- the role of online symptom checkers;
- care-navigation expectations;
- concerns about AI;
- integration with the wider health system.

The programme therefore remains in a **governed discovery and engagement phase**, not a nationally deployed clinical-service phase. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/what-we-do/programmes-and-initiatives/symptom-checker?utm_source=openai))

### 4. New investment strengthened New Zealand’s medical-imaging research capability

On **20 July 2026**, the University of Auckland announced the appointment of Professor Alistair Young, a heart-imaging researcher returning from King’s College London. His work includes AI algorithms that analyse large volumes of cardiac scans and create detailed three-dimensional models of heart structure and movement.

The Auckland programme is particularly focused on applying advanced modelling to echocardiography, which is more widely available and less expensive than some other imaging modalities. ([auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/07/20/global-heart-imaging-expert-joins-auckland-initiative.html?utm_source=openai))

### 5. Workforce capability is emerging as a formal adoption requirement

Whitecliffe launched a Level 8, 30-credit **Applied AI in Healthcare micro-credential** in July 2026. The programme covers AI, healthcare data, ethics, governance, equity, Te Tiriti o Waitangi and data sovereignty. Its launch reflects growing demand for healthcare professionals who can evaluate and govern AI rather than simply operate AI products. ([whitecliffe.ac.nz](https://www.whitecliffe.ac.nz/news-events/applied-ai-in-healthcare-micro-credential-no-fees-for-july-2026-intake-only/))

## Current State of AI Adoption

## 1. Public hospitals: documentation remains the most mature use case

The strongest evidence of scaled AI deployment remains the nationwide emergency-department AI scribe rollout.

By February 2026:

- AI scribe technology was live in every emergency department;
- approximately **1,250 doctors and frontline staff** had access;
- more than **1,000 further licences** were being progressed;
- the additional licences were expected to support mental health teams in particular. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

The model is deliberately assistive:

- AI generates a transcript or draft clinical note;
- the clinician reviews and edits the output;
- the clinician remains responsible for the clinical record and all decisions;
- AI is not positioned as an autonomous diagnostic or treatment authority.

This documentation-first pattern is strategically important. It shows that New Zealand is most willing to scale AI where:

- the task is repetitive and administratively burdensome;
- the output can be reviewed by a clinician;
- the risk of an incorrect output can be controlled;
- the tool does not independently determine a patient’s treatment.

## 2. Primary care: rapid uptake, but uneven governance maturity

Primary care remains one of the fastest-moving areas of AI adoption.

A 2026 New Zealand survey of **197 primary-care respondents** found that approximately **40%** had experience using AI scribes. Respondents reported:

- less multitasking;
- time savings;
- reduced cognitive load;
- improved patient rapport.

The same research identified concerns about:

- patient consent;
- security of patient information;
- errors or omissions in generated notes;
- legal and ethical compliance;
- whether data might leave New Zealand;
- limited recognition of te reo Māori by some tools. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41134014/?utm_source=openai))

The Royal New Zealand College of General Practitioners has separately reported that **41% of GPs** and **16% of rural hospital doctors** had used, or intended to use, AI, with note-taking and scribe applications the most common use case. ([transform.auckland.ac.nz](https://www.transform.auckland.ac.nz/2026/04/30/collaboratively-developing-an-evaluation-framework-for-ai-tools-in-the-aotearoa-new-zealand-health-sector/?utm_source=openai))

Product integration is also becoming more sophisticated. Medtech AI was launched in New Zealand as an integrated feature within Medtech Evolution, with local data-storage positioning, explicit patient consent before recording, and no retention of consultation audio according to the vendor. ([whitecliffe.ac.nz](https://www.whitecliffe.ac.nz/news-events/applied-ai-in-healthcare-micro-credential-no-fees-for-july-2026-intake-only/?utm_source=openai))

**Assessment:** Primary-care adoption is moving beyond informal experimentation, but national standards for consent, monitoring, clinical validation and accountability are still developing.

## 3. Aged residential care: ambient documentation is spreading

Metlifecare’s HEIDI trial at Parkside Village involved **12 registered nurses** over four months. Following the trial, the organisation announced a rollout to three additional care homes.

Reported benefits included:

- reduced time spent on administrative documentation;
- improved continuity of care;
- better handovers;
- more time for resident interaction and professional development.

Residents’ consent was required, and nurses remained responsible for reviewing, editing and approving all AI-generated documentation. ([metlifecare.co.nz](https://www.metlifecare.co.nz/news/metlifecare-heidi-scribe-tool?utm_source=openai))

Aged care demonstrates that AI scribes are not limited to emergency medicine or general practice. The same underlying value proposition—reducing documentation burden and improving information continuity—is relevant across care settings.

## 4. Patient-facing AI: promising, but still at an early stage

### Symptom checking and care navigation

Health NZ is exploring an AI-supported online symptom checker, but the programme remains under clinical and community governance. The July 2026 update emphasises engagement with consumers and system navigators before decisions are made about the tool’s role. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/what-we-do/programmes-and-initiatives/symptom-checker?utm_source=openai))

### Surgical information assistant

A University of Auckland-developed AI medical assistant is being rolled out across a small number of clinics in New Zealand and the United States. It communicates with patients by SMS and is designed to answer procedure-specific questions, provide preparation and recovery information, and escalate red-flag situations.

The assistant is customised to surgeon protocols and includes supervisory checks and escalation rules. Its role is primarily patient education and service support rather than diagnosis or autonomous treatment. ([auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/02/03/ai-assistant-eases-surgical-anxiety-one-question-at-a-time.html))

These tools illustrate a different adoption pathway from clinical decision support: AI is being used as a **controlled communication layer** around care, helping patients access consistent information outside the consultation.

## 5. Imaging, screening and diagnostics: the strongest next-wave clinical pathway

### Breast screening

Breast screening remains the clearest national clinical AI deployment in the pipeline. The programme processes approximately **270,000 women annually**, while the eligible age range is being extended. AI is being considered as one of the two independent mammogram readers, with clinicians remaining responsible for diagnosis and follow-up. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-breast-screening-tool-one-step-closer?utm_source=openai))

The procurement process has now closed, but public implementation remains subject to:

- supplier selection;
- local testing;
- validation;
- workflow integration;
- clinician acceptance;
- equity and performance monitoring.

### Diabetic retinal screening

The New Zealand Medical Journal’s 26 June paper remains one of the most important local implementation studies. A proof-of-concept intended to improve timely diabetic-retinal screening for Pacific peoples encountered difficulties involving:

- legacy digital systems;
- image sharing;
- redesign of models of care;
- clinician readiness;
- the suitability of available AI tools;
- resourcing and organisational support.

The authors’ conclusion was that AI implementation cannot be treated as a technical plug-in. It requires changes to systems, workforce roles, workflows and service design. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42348896/?utm_source=openai))

### Cardiac imaging

The University of Auckland’s renewed heart-imaging programme points to longer-term potential in:

- earlier detection of heart disease;
- improved monitoring of treatment response;
- analysis of heart failure and cardiomyopathy;
- extracting clinically useful measurements from echocardiography.

This remains a research and translation pipeline rather than a nationally deployed clinical service. ([auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/07/20/global-heart-imaging-expert-joins-auckland-initiative.html))

## Research and Innovation Pipeline

## 1. Clinical trials are moving closer to treatment decisions

The REVOLUTION programme is a New Zealand-led clinical trial testing machine-learning-guided oxygen therapy for critically ill patients. The programme is supported by nearly **$5 million** and is expected to involve:

- 50 intensive-care units across New Zealand and Australia;
- more than 24,000 patients;
- evaluation of whether AI-guided oxygen treatment can improve outcomes for patients receiving life support. ([mrinz.ac.nz](https://www.mrinz.ac.nz/news/hrc-programme-grant-supports-mrinz-led-world-first-ai-intensive-care-research?utm_source=openai))

This is a significant step beyond documentation support because the AI is being evaluated in a treatment-management pathway. However, it remains a controlled clinical trial and should not be interpreted as evidence of routine autonomous treatment.

## 2. Research is increasingly focused on social licence and end-user expectations

A New Zealand interview study published in May 2026 involved **51 participants**, including health professionals, patients with chronic, mental-health and rare conditions, and people undergoing routine screening.

Participants broadly accepted the use of personal health information for AI when it was intended to serve the wider public good. Across groups, participants also emphasised:

- transparency;
- appropriate data use;
- consent;
- security;
- strong governance;
- preservation of trust in health services.

The study found that perspectives were broadly aligned but not identical, meaning that a single generic approach to public engagement is unlikely to be sufficient. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42161573/?utm_source=openai))

## 3. AI research capacity is expanding in imaging and precision health

The University of Auckland’s imaging programme is strengthening its combination of:

- engineering;
- machine learning;
- clinical medicine;
- computational modelling;
- echocardiography;
- cardiovascular research.

The work is representative of New Zealand’s research strategy: use international expertise, apply it to locally relevant clinical settings, and focus on technologies that could improve access as well as accuracy. ([auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/07/20/global-heart-imaging-expert-joins-auckland-initiative.html?utm_source=openai))

## 4. Prototyping and software development are accelerating

A March 2026 “Code for Care” hackathon brought together Deloitte New Zealand, AWS and Health NZ. Teams built three healthcare software prototypes in two days using secure test data and sandboxed environments.

The case study demonstrated how AI-assisted software development can:

- reduce the time from concept to prototype;
- involve clinicians directly in product design;
- explore administrative and workflow problems quickly;
- maintain privacy by using mocked or secure test data during development. ([deloitte.com](https://www.deloitte.com/nz/en/Industries/life-sciences-health-care/perspectives/code-for-care.html))

The significance is not that the prototypes are already deployed, but that Health NZ and its partners are developing a more structured method for testing AI-enabled solutions without moving immediately into live clinical use.

## Governance, Regulation and Digital Foundations

## 1. National oversight is becoming an operating mechanism

Health NZ’s National Artificial Intelligence and Algorithm Expert Advisory Group provides advice and endorsement for AI projects intended for use within the health system. Its remit includes:

- safety;
- effectiveness;
- ethics;
- legality;
- social and cultural licence;
- technical and operational suitability;
- ongoing improvement.

AI proposals seeking Health NZ approval or funding are expected to go through the group’s review process. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/who-we-are/expert-groups-and-networks/expert-groups/artificial-intelligence-and-algorithm-expert-advisory-group?utm_source=openai))

This is a notable shift from high-level AI principles toward a more operational approval and assurance model.

## 2. The Aotearoa evaluation framework is central to adoption

The TRANSFORM framework was developed after reviewing 31 international frameworks and determining that existing approaches did not adequately reflect the New Zealand health-system context.

Its whole-of-system approach considers:

- patients and communities;
- health professionals;
- service delivery;
- health-system impacts;
- equity;
- safety;
- implementation feasibility.

The framework’s endorsement by Health NZ and reported use across proposed tools indicate that evaluation is becoming a standard part of AI adoption rather than an optional research activity. ([transform.auckland.ac.nz](https://www.transform.auckland.ac.nz/2026/04/30/collaboratively-developing-an-evaluation-framework-for-ai-tools-in-the-aotearoa-new-zealand-health-sector/?utm_source=openai))

## 3. Health NZ’s operating position remains human-supervised augmentation

The Ministry of Health’s current precision-health guidance says AI adoption should be:

- accessible;
- safe and effective;
- accountable;
- good value;
- equitable;
- transparent;
- supervised and evaluated.

The Ministry also describes New Zealand as being in the early stages of assessing how AI can be safely adopted to improve healthcare access and quality. ([health.govt.nz](https://www.health.govt.nz/strategies-initiatives/programmes-and-initiatives/precision-health-ai-and-genomics))

That position aligns with the practical deployment pattern:

- AI scribes generate drafts, not final records;
- imaging tools support human readers;
- symptom checkers are being clinically governed;
- predictive models are being tested through research;
- no public evidence indicates that Health NZ has delegated autonomous clinical decision-making to AI.

## 4. Regulation of therapeutic AI is still developing

Current Ministry proposals under the Medical Products Bill include risk-proportionate pathways for software and AI intended for a therapeutic purpose, including software as a medical device. ([health.govt.nz](https://www.health.govt.nz/information-releases/innovative-medical-products-and-regulatory-pathways-to-market))

This suggests that future regulation is likely to distinguish between:

- low-risk administrative and documentation tools;
- patient-facing information systems;
- diagnostic and screening tools;
- high-risk clinical decision-support or treatment systems.

The precise regulatory pathway and implementation timetable remain important watchpoints for suppliers and healthcare organisations.

## 5. Interoperability remains a major constraint

The Shared Digital Health Record programme has been extended to allow additional information-sharing and security checks. Health NZ expects immunisation and medication data to become available later in **2026**, while access to primary-care data is expected from **mid-2027**. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/news-and-updates/shared-digital-health-record-update-july-2026?utm_source=openai))

The programme’s security model includes:

- role-based access;
- access logging;
- approved systems and providers;
- privacy and security assurance;
- compliance with the Health Information Privacy Code;
- alignment with the Government Digital Delivery Agency Information Sharing Standard. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-professionals/guidance-standards/topic/digital-technologies/digital-health-initiatives/shared-digital-health-record/shared-digital-health-record-data-privacy-and-security?utm_source=openai))

This infrastructure is important for AI because many higher-value use cases depend on longitudinal, linkable and consistently structured data. The retinal-screening experience demonstrates the problem directly: even a clinically promising model can struggle when data systems, workflows and local contracts do not align.

The OECD similarly identifies interoperability, ongoing post-deployment monitoring and system-wide scaling as areas where New Zealand needs further coordination. ([oecd.org](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-economic-surveys-new-zealand-2026_3ec5de98-en/full-report/harnessing-digital-tools-to-improve-health-system-performance_ee123dce.html))

## Trust, Equity and Māori and Pacific Data Governance

Equity and cultural safety remain among the most important determinants of whether healthcare AI will be accepted and effective in Aotearoa.

At a June 2026 Pacific health symposium, University of Auckland researchers highlighted that:

- many international AI tools are trained on datasets that inadequately represent Māori and Pacific populations;
- local validation is essential;
- outdated infrastructure can restrict access;
- bias, data sovereignty and community trust require active governance;
- generative AI outputs must be checked because fluent responses can still be inaccurate. ([auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/06/29/ai-in-health-scholar-brings-clarity-and-caution-to-pacific-symposium.html))

The local interview study found broad support for AI where it serves the public good, but only alongside transparency, consent, data security and responsible governance. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42161573/?utm_source=openai))

The implications are practical:

- a tool that performs well in overseas datasets may not perform equally well for Māori, Pacific or rural populations;
- language capability, including te reo Māori, matters for usability and safety;
- data sovereignty must be considered during model development, procurement and deployment;
- equity should be measured as an outcome, not treated only as an ethical statement.

## Case Studies

## Case Study 1: Nationwide emergency-department AI scribes

**Setting:** Public emergency departments  
**Use case:** Ambient documentation  
**Status:** Nationwide deployment  

The rollout to approximately 1,250 clinicians is New Zealand’s clearest example of moving from pilot to national operational use. Early pilot reporting suggested that clinicians could see approximately one additional patient per shift due to time saved on documentation. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

**Key lesson:** AI is most readily scaled where it augments an existing task, produces a reviewable output and does not independently determine care.

## Case Study 2: AI-enabled diabetic retinal screening

**Setting:** South Auckland and Health NZ screening services  
**Use case:** AI-supported retinal image assessment  
**Status:** Proof of concept and implementation research  

The local experience exposed barriers involving infrastructure, workflow, clinician readiness and tool suitability. The case has become an important counterweight to technology-led narratives that assume diagnostic accuracy automatically translates into service impact. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42348896/?utm_source=openai))

**Key lesson:** Implementation capacity can be more limiting than model performance.

## Case Study 3: Metlifecare HEIDI rollout

**Setting:** Aged residential care  
**Use case:** Nurse documentation and handover support  
**Status:** Expanded rollout after trial  

The trial reported less administrative work and better continuity, with residents’ consent and clinician review built into the model. ([metlifecare.co.nz](https://www.metlifecare.co.nz/news/metlifecare-heidi-scribe-tool?utm_source=openai))

**Key lesson:** Ambient documentation has applicability across the broader care continuum, not just hospitals and general practice.

## Case Study 4: AI surgical-information assistant

**Setting:** Specialist clinics  
**Use case:** Patient education, preparation and post-operative information  
**Status:** Early rollout across a small number of clinics  

The SMS-based assistant is customised to surgeon protocols, includes escalation mechanisms and is designed to answer questions that patients may forget or feel uncomfortable asking during a short consultation. ([auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/02/03/ai-assistant-eases-surgical-anxiety-one-question-at-a-time.html))

**Key lesson:** Patient-facing AI may gain traction first as a controlled information and navigation layer rather than as an autonomous diagnostic tool.

## Case Study 5: Code for Care prototyping programme

**Setting:** Health NZ innovation and software development  
**Use case:** AI-assisted development of healthcare workflow prototypes  
**Status:** Prototype stage  

The programme demonstrated that secure, sandboxed AI-assisted development can help clinicians and engineers explore service problems rapidly without exposing live patient data. ([deloitte.com](https://www.deloitte.com/nz/en/Industries/life-sciences-health-care/perspectives/code-for-care.html))

**Key lesson:** AI adoption is also occurring behind the scenes in how health services design, test and improve digital tools.

## Key Trends

### 1. Documentation-first adoption is now established

AI scribes are active or being evaluated in:

- emergency departments;
- general practice;
- mental health services;
- aged residential care;
- specialist and allied-health settings.

This remains the most mature category because it offers visible productivity benefits while retaining clinician review.

### 2. Clinical AI is progressing through validation rather than immediate scale

Breast screening, retinal screening, cardiac imaging, radiology and ICU treatment optimisation are advancing, but through:

- procurement;
- research trials;
- local validation;
- workflow testing;
- clinician engagement;
- governance approval.

### 3. The centre of gravity is shifting from accuracy to implementation

New Zealand’s strongest local evidence now concerns:

- interoperability;
- organisational readiness;
- workflow redesign;
- consent;
- clinician trust;
- data sovereignty;
- equity;
- ongoing monitoring.

This is a more mature adoption question than simply asking whether an algorithm performs well in a laboratory dataset.

### 4. AI governance is becoming part of normal health-system management

The combination of:

- NAIAEAG oversight;
- the TRANSFORM evaluation framework;
- Health NZ’s national AI approach;
- professional guidance from the Medical Council;
- privacy and information-sharing controls;
- emerging medical-product regulation;

is creating a more structured environment for AI procurement and deployment. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/who-we-are/expert-groups-and-networks/expert-groups/artificial-intelligence-and-algorithm-expert-advisory-group?utm_source=openai))

### 5. Interoperability and cyber resilience are pacing factors

The Shared Digital Health Record timeline, security requirements and continuing data-integration work show that AI scale depends on the underlying data environment. New Zealand’s current challenge is not simply obtaining better models; it is making sure those models can access appropriate, secure and representative information within real clinical workflows. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/news-and-updates/shared-digital-health-record-update-july-2026?utm_source=openai))

### 6. Workforce capability is becoming a strategic issue

The health system needs clinicians, managers, researchers, procurement specialists and privacy professionals who understand:

- what AI tools can and cannot do;
- how to evaluate model performance;
- how to identify bias and drift;
- how to redesign workflows;
- how to obtain meaningful consent;
- how to manage clinical accountability.

The emergence of dedicated AI-in-healthcare education reflects this growing requirement. ([whitecliffe.ac.nz](https://www.whitecliffe.ac.nz/news-events/applied-ai-in-healthcare-micro-credential-no-fees-for-july-2026-intake-only/))

### 7. Māori and Pacific perspectives are increasingly shaping the adoption model

AI adoption in Aotearoa is being framed not only as an efficiency or innovation issue, but also as a question of:

- health equity;
- cultural safety;
- representation in training data;
- data sovereignty;
- public trust;
- community benefit.

This is likely to remain a defining feature of New Zealand’s approach. ([auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/06/29/ai-in-health-scholar-brings-clarity-and-caution-to-pacific-symposium.html))

## Outlook: The Next 12–18 Months

The most likely developments are:

- completion of BreastScreen Aotearoa supplier selection, testing and validation;
- further expansion of AI scribes into mental health, primary care and other clinical teams;
- continued evaluation of Health NZ’s symptom-checker and care-navigation options;
- late-2026 integration of immunisation and medication information into the Shared Digital Health Record;
- progression of the REVOLUTION ICU trial;
- increased translation of AI-enabled imaging research into locally tested clinical tools;
- further clarification of regulatory pathways for therapeutic AI and software as a medical device;
- greater emphasis on post-deployment monitoring and performance reporting.

These are projections based on announced programmes and current implementation direction, not confirmed future outcomes. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-breast-screening-tool-one-step-closer?utm_source=openai))

## Overall Assessment

As of **18 August 2026**, AI adoption in New Zealand healthcare is best described as **early but increasingly institutionalised**.

The system has moved beyond isolated experimentation in some areas, particularly ambient documentation. However, clinical AI remains cautious and conditional. The dominant operating model is:

> **AI supports clinicians, while humans retain responsibility for care, records and decisions.**

New Zealand’s distinguishing feature is not the speed of autonomous deployment. It is the development of a more deliberate adoption model that combines:

- targeted operational use;
- local validation;
- national governance;
- patient and community engagement;
- attention to Māori and Pacific equity;
- privacy and security controls;
- staged investment in digital infrastructure.

The central strategic conclusion is that New Zealand is building the foundations for broader healthcare AI adoption, but those foundations are not yet complete. Documentation tools are already operational at scale. Imaging, screening, patient navigation and predictive clinical support are moving through evaluation and research. The next phase will depend less on whether AI can produce impressive outputs and more on whether the health system can integrate those outputs safely, equitably and consistently into everyday care.
