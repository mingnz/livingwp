# AI Adoption in Healthcare in New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper  
**Updated: 13 June 2026**

## Executive Summary

AI adoption in New Zealand healthcare still looks like **selective operational rollout under tight clinical and privacy control**, not broad automation. The most mature scaled deployment remains ambient clinical documentation in public hospitals, with AI scribe technology live in every emergency department for **1,250 frontline staff**. Health NZ’s current privacy position also confirms active use cases now include transcription, consultation summaries, document generation, staff knowledge tools, and preliminary review of clinical images, while stating that many uses are still limited pilots and that AI is **not** used to make automated decisions about patient care. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

The main substantive change since the previous update on **10 June 2026** is in the **research-to-clinical pipeline**, not frontline deployment. On **11 June 2026**, the Health Research Council announced a **$5 million** programme grant for the **REVOLUTION** trial, a New Zealand-led study across **50 ICUs** in New Zealand and Australia that will test whether machine-learning-guided oxygen therapy improves survival for more than **24,000** critically ill patients. That is one of the strongest signs yet that New Zealand’s next AI phase could include higher-acuity decision support, but only through formal clinical trials rather than immediate operational rollout. ([hrc.govt.nz](https://hrc.govt.nz/news-and-events/major-nz-led-clinical-trial-test-ai-guided-treatment-critically-ill-patients))

Breast screening remains the clearest next-wave national clinical AI programme. Health Minister Simeon Brown said on **27 May 2026** that procurement was underway for an AI mammogram-reading tool, and the live GETS tender now shows a more concrete delivery path: **validation trials, pilot implementation, then phased national rollout**, with Health NZ seeking in-market tools for mammography machine reading and breast-density reporting that can fit BreastScreen Aotearoa’s blind double-read workflow. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-breast-screening-tool-one-step-closer?utm_source=openai))

The broad pattern is therefore unchanged but clearer: **documentation-first adoption is scaling fastest; imaging is the strongest clinical expansion lane; and governance, consent, privacy, equity, and Māori data sovereignty remain central conditions for scale.** ([health.govt.nz](https://www.health.govt.nz/strategies-initiatives/programmes-and-initiatives/precision-health-ai-and-genomics))

## What Has Changed Since 10 June 2026

- **A major new AI clinical trial has entered the national pipeline.** On **11 June 2026**, HRC announced funding for the REVOLUTION trial, which will test AI-guided oxygen therapy in ICUs across New Zealand and Australia. HRC describes it as the first major clinical trial worldwide to evaluate whether machine-learning-guided treatment improves ICU survival. ([hrc.govt.nz](https://hrc.govt.nz/news-and-events/major-nz-led-clinical-trial-test-ai-guided-treatment-critically-ill-patients))
- **Breast screening procurement is now more transparent operationally.** The live GETS tender confirms Health NZ is seeking in-market AI solutions for mammography reading and breast-density reporting, with a phased model of validation, pilot, then national rollout. The tender opened on **22 May 2026** and closes on **22 June 2026**. ([gets.govt.nz](https://www.gets.govt.nz/HEALTHNZ/ExternalTenderDetails.htm?id=34058803))
- **No equally material new public announcement was found on nationwide frontline deployment between 10 and 13 June.** The adoption picture still centres on emergency-department scribes, primary-care documentation tools, aged-care pilots, and cautious movement toward imaging deployment. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

## Current State of AI Adoption

## 1. Public hospitals: documentation remains the most mature live deployment

The most advanced public-sector AI deployment is still the nationwide emergency-department scribe rollout announced on **28 February 2026**. The Government said the system was live in all emergency departments and used by **1,250 ED doctors and frontline staff**, with more than **1,000 additional licences** being progressed, largely for mental health teams. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

This remains strategically important because it shows where Health NZ is currently comfortable scaling AI: **workflow support with clinician oversight**, not autonomous diagnosis or treatment. Health NZ’s privacy statement says any AI-generated information that could affect patient records or clinical decisions must be reviewed by the responsible clinician, and that Health NZ does **not** use AI to make automated healthcare decisions. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/privacy/privacy-statement))

Health NZ has also added a stronger transparency layer around this rollout through an OIA release published on **28 April 2026**, which it says covers governance, risk management, privacy safeguards, and early evaluation findings relating to Heidi AI in clinical settings. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/publications/information-relating-to-the-use-and-evaluation-of-heidi-ai-oia))

## 2. Primary care: uptake is fast, but governance is still catching up

Primary care remains the fastest-moving non-hospital adoption environment. A New Zealand survey published on **26 March 2026** found that **40%** of respondents had experience with AI scribes, but only **59%** reported seeking patient consent and only **66%** had read the tool’s terms and conditions. The paper’s core conclusion was that uptake is strong, but legal, ethical, and privacy guidance remains underdeveloped. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41134014/?utm_source=openai))

Commercial tooling is also becoming more integrated. Medtech says its New Zealand platform is built into **Medtech Evolution**, keeps data stored in New Zealand, does not retain consultation audio, does not store patient names, dates of birth, or NHI numbers, and requires clinicians to confirm explicit patient consent before recording begins. Medtech positions the tool as broader than scribing, including structured notes, referral letters, summaries, and direct write-back into workflow systems. ([medtechglobal.com](https://medtechglobal.com/nz/medtech-ai/))

The implication is that primary care is moving from ad hoc experimentation toward **embedded, workflow-level adoption**, but still with obvious consent, compliance, and clinician-accountability gaps. That is an inference from the combination of the survey findings and current vendor positioning. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41134014/?utm_source=openai))

## 3. Aged care: AI documentation is spreading beyond hospitals and GP clinics

Aged residential care is now a visible adoption setting. On **12 May 2026**, Metlifecare said it would roll out the HEIDI ambient scribe tool across **three more care homes** after a four-month trial at Parkside Village involving **12 registered nurses**. It reported reduced documentation time, improved continuity and handovers, and rapid movement to full staff adoption at the pilot site. ([metlifecare.co.nz](https://www.metlifecare.co.nz/news/metlifecare-heidi-scribe-tool))

This matters because it shows that AI documentation is no longer only a hospital or general-practice story in New Zealand. It is also becoming an **aged-care workforce and care-quality tool**, again with a model where clinicians retain responsibility for reviewing and approving documentation. ([metlifecare.co.nz](https://www.metlifecare.co.nz/news/metlifecare-heidi-scribe-tool))

## 4. Screening and imaging: still the strongest next-wave clinical scale-up lane

Breast screening is the most important national clinical AI programme now moving toward implementation. The Government said on **27 May 2026** that procurement was underway for a preferred AI mammogram-reading tool, ahead of testing and validation and a planned rollout from **early 2027**. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-breast-screening-tool-one-step-closer?utm_source=openai))

The tender details now make the shape of that programme clearer. Health NZ is seeking **in-market** AI tools for mammography machine reading and breast-density reporting, not custom development, and requires solutions that fit BreastScreen Aotearoa’s **blind double-read** workflow. The official procurement path is phased: **validation trials, pilot implementation, then phased national rollout**. ([gets.govt.nz](https://www.gets.govt.nz/HEALTHNZ/ExternalTenderDetails.htm?id=34058803))

This imaging lane also aligns with Health NZ’s broader public position. Its privacy statement says AI is already being used to read, analyse, or review clinical information for preliminary results, including **images, x-rays, scans, or mammograms**, though many of these uses are still limited pilots. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/privacy/privacy-statement))

## Research and Innovation Pipeline

The research pipeline is now more visibly moving toward high-stakes clinical decision support. The clearest new example is the **REVOLUTION** ICU study announced on **11 June 2026**, which will use machine learning to personalise oxygen targets for patients on life support and compare AI-assisted decisions with standard clinician-led care. HRC says the trial spans every major ICU in New Zealand and recruits over **24,000** patients across New Zealand and Australia. ([hrc.govt.nz](https://hrc.govt.nz/news-and-events/major-nz-led-clinical-trial-test-ai-guided-treatment-critically-ill-patients))

That new trial sits alongside a broader New Zealand research base focused on **clinician-supervised, measurable use cases**. Existing examples include AI work in primary-care scribing, postoperative monitoring, AI-nurse partnerships, and community-facing AI design for Pasifika asthma care. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41134014/?utm_source=openai))

The pattern is notable: New Zealand’s research ambition is already extending into critical care and predictive support, while live operational deployment is still concentrated in documentation and tightly controlled imaging use. ([hrc.govt.nz](https://hrc.govt.nz/news-and-events/major-nz-led-clinical-trial-test-ai-guided-treatment-critically-ill-patients))

## Governance, Regulation, and Digital Foundations

Health NZ’s operational stance remains clear: AI may assist clinicians, but it does not replace them. Its privacy statement says AI is used in a **closed environment**, that personal information is not used to build commercial AI or third-party generative models, and that clinicians must review any AI-generated material that could affect records or decisions. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/privacy/privacy-statement))

Health NZ’s staff guidance on generative AI is also restrictive. It says employees and contractors must **not** enter patient, confidential, or sensitive organisational information into unapproved LLMs, must **not** use LLMs for clinical decisions or personalised patient advice, and that Health NZ does **not currently have any private LLMs available**. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-professionals/guidance-standards/topic/digital-technologies/using-generative-ai-and-large-language-models))

At the profession level, the Medical Council’s **10 March 2026** guidance says doctors remain responsible for all clinical decisions, must check AI outputs, should tell patients when AI is used in their care, and must obtain informed consent in specific situations including AI consultation recording. ([mcnz.org.nz](https://www.mcnz.org.nz/assets/standards/Guidance-on-using-artificial-intelligence-AI-in-patient-care-March-2026.pdf))

Governance is also becoming more formalised institutionally. Health NZ’s NAIAEAG requires all AI development or implementation plans to be registered, and its terms of reference say a public register of AI in use within Health NZ will be made available. Separately, a University of Auckland-led framework endorsed in **March 2026** is now being used to assess all proposed AI tools across Health NZ services. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/who-we-are/expert-and-advisory-groups/advisory-groups/artificial-intelligence-and-algorithm-expert-advisory-group))

On regulation, the wider legal environment is still evolving. The Ministry of Health’s Medical Products Bill work explicitly contemplates regulatory pathways for **software and artificial intelligence intended for a therapeutic purpose** as software-as-a-medical-device. ([health.govt.nz](https://www.health.govt.nz/information-releases/innovative-medical-products-and-regulatory-pathways-to-market))

## Trust, Equity, and Social Licence

Trust remains one of the biggest adoption constraints. A February 2026 NZMJ viewpoint argued that patient trust is central to successful healthcare AI implementation in Aotearoa New Zealand, especially around public benefit, governance, data protection, choice, clinician responsibility, and Māori representation. ([nzmj.org.nz](https://nzmj.org.nz/journal/vol-139-no-1629/maintaining-patient-trust-as-artificial-intelligence-s-role-in-healthcare-grows?utm_source=openai))

More targeted community research points in the same direction. A 2026 study on Pasifika perspectives on AI for asthma management found interest in AI’s potential, but also recurring concerns around privacy, accuracy, access, and age-related digital divides. ([openrepository.aut.ac.nz](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/06287e76-ec90-4a5c-9a99-1c86e57f4124?utm_source=openai))

Those concerns map closely onto Health NZ’s own warnings about privacy, bias, data sovereignty, language limitations, and the risks of public generative AI tools. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-professionals/guidance-standards/topic/digital-technologies/using-generative-ai-and-large-language-models))

## Key Trends

- **Documentation remains the main scale story.** Emergency departments, primary care, and aged care all show the same dominant model: ambient documentation with clinician review. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))
- **Imaging is the strongest clinical expansion lane.** BreastScreen procurement is now the clearest nationally structured move beyond documentation support. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-breast-screening-tool-one-step-closer?utm_source=openai))
- **Research is pushing ahead of deployment.** The new ICU machine-learning trial shows New Zealand is willing to explore higher-stakes AI, but through formal evidence generation rather than rapid operational release. ([hrc.govt.nz](https://hrc.govt.nz/news-and-events/major-nz-led-clinical-trial-test-ai-guided-treatment-critically-ill-patients))
- **Governance has become more operational.** Privacy rules, LLM restrictions, Medical Council standards, NAIAEAG oversight, and the new evaluation framework now form a more complete health-sector governance stack than New Zealand had even a few months ago. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/privacy/privacy-statement))
- **Equity and data governance are not side issues.** Māori data sovereignty, bias, consent, privacy, and community trust are now built into both policy language and evaluation expectations. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-professionals/guidance-standards/topic/digital-technologies/using-generative-ai-and-large-language-models))

## Overall Assessment

As of **13 June 2026**, AI in New Zealand healthcare is best understood as **maturing supervised augmentation**. The public system’s most mature deployments remain in documentation and workflow support; the most credible national clinical expansion is breast screening; and the most important new development since **10 June 2026** is the HRC-backed move toward a large, rigorous ICU decision-support trial. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

The sector is still early-stage overall. The Ministry of Health continues to describe New Zealand as being in the early stages of assessing how AI can be safely adopted, and Health NZ still says many current uses are being tested on a limited basis. But the direction of travel is now clearer than it was earlier in 2026: **New Zealand is not treating healthcare AI as open-ended automation. It is building it as clinically supervised, procurement-led, evidence-tested augmentation.** ([health.govt.nz](https://www.health.govt.nz/strategies-initiatives/programmes-and-initiatives/precision-health-ai-and-genomics))
