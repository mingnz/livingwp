# AI in Public Sector in Aotearoa New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper
**Updated: August 18, 2026**

## Introduction

AI adoption in New Zealand’s public sector is continuing to mature, but the story since the **July 13, 2026** edition is more about **operationalisation and control** than headline-grabbing expansion. The state’s direction remains anchored in the **Digital Government Target State**, the **Government Digital Delivery Agency (GDDA)**, and a public-service AI work programme built around shared tools, assurance, and workforce capability. The practical use pattern also remains conservative: AI is being used mainly for search, summarisation, documentation, workflow support, analysis, and service navigation rather than autonomous decision-making. ([dns.govt.nz](https://dns.govt.nz/digital-government/digitising-government-programme-dgp/digital-target-state?utm_source=openai))

What has advanced in the last month is the surrounding machinery. The **Govt.nz app** and digital identity stack moved closer to live citizen use, with GDDA accreditation recorded on **July 15, 2026** and public messaging now pointing to the first accredited credentials becoming available from **late August 2026**. In health, Health NZ has made its AI governance architecture more explicit, updated public material on its **AI and algorithm expert advisory group**, issued clearer privacy language around closed-environment AI use, and moved its AI-enabled **symptom checker** into a proof-of-concept phase. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/about-the-commission/government-digital-delivery-agency/trust-framework-for-digital-identity/trust-framework-authority/trust-framework-register?utm_source=openai))

The result is a public sector that is still cautious, but increasingly structured. New Zealand is not yet showing broad autonomous government AI. It is showing something else: a deliberate move toward **shared rails, bounded use cases, domain-specific governance, and procurement-led scaling**. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/news/rapid-review-proposes-reset-for-digital-delivery))

## Executive Snapshot

- **The centre of gravity is still shared public infrastructure, not agency-by-agency AI buildouts.** The Digital Government Target State explicitly includes an **AI broker/gateway**, **AI platform services**, **semantic search**, and **data and AI safeguards** inside a shared Digital Public Infrastructure model. ([dns.govt.nz](https://dns.govt.nz/digital-government/digitising-government-programme-dgp/digital-target-state?utm_source=openai))
- **The strongest whole-of-system evidence base remains the 2025 cross-agency survey.** It records **272 AI use cases across 70 agencies**, including **55 operational deployments**, with most activity in automation, assisted search, detection/analysis, and summarisation. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/dmsdocument/263~report-2025-cross-agency-survey-for-artificial-intelligence-ai-use-cases/html))
- **The state’s AI posture is still assistive rather than autonomous.** Official guidance and case studies point to human-in-the-loop use in drafting, triage support, analytics, documentation, and workflow redesign. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/dmsdocument/263~report-2025-cross-agency-survey-for-artificial-intelligence-ai-use-cases/html))
- **Health remains the most advanced operating domain.** Emergency-department AI scribes are live nationwide; HealthX is building an evidence-and-scaling pathway; Health NZ has formal expert oversight via NAIAEAG; and the symptom checker programme has moved into controlled proof-of-concept testing. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))
- **Digital identity and the Govt.nz app are now materially closer to production-scale public use.** GDDA was accredited on **July 15, 2026** for Govt.nz app authentication and facilitation services, and the public app page says credentials are expected to begin appearing from **late August 2026**. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/about-the-commission/government-digital-delivery-agency/trust-framework-for-digital-identity/trust-framework-authority/trust-framework-register?utm_source=openai))
- **Procurement is becoming a clearer signal of next-wave adoption.** Recent public tenders show NZTA seeking an **AI-enabled forecasting and delivery confidence tool**, while Police has run an RFP to replace its **Automated Biometric Identification System** with a modern platform including facial-recognition capability. ([gets.govt.nz](https://www.gets.govt.nz/NZTAHNO/ExternalTenderDetails.htm?id=34538552))
- **Capability is improving, but unevenly.** In the 2025 Public Service Census, **33%** of public servants had used AI for work and **14%** used it regularly. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/data/public-service-census/delivery/innovation?utm_source=openai))
- **Trust and legitimacy remain the binding constraints.** Kantar’s research found only **4%** of New Zealanders felt well informed about how government uses AI, while Kiwis Count still showed strong general trust in public services in the **March 2026** quarter. ([kantarnewzealand.com](https://www.kantarnewzealand.com/ai-in-the-public-sector/?utm_source=openai))
- **Biometrics is now an immediate compliance issue, not a pending one.** The transition period for pre-existing biometric processing under the **Biometric Processing Privacy Code 2025** ended on **August 3, 2026**. ([privacy.org.nz](https://www.privacy.org.nz/privacy-principles/codes-of-practice/biometric-processing-privacy-code/?utm_source=openai))

## What Changed Since the July 13, 2026 Edition

### 1) The Govt.nz app moved from architecture to accredited delivery rail

The most important operational change since mid-July is that the **Government Digital Delivery Agency** appears on the **Digital Identity Services Trust Framework Register** as an accredited provider from **July 15, 2026**, with services for **Govt.nz app authentication** and **Govt.nz app facilitation**. The register describes the facilitation service as a secure and privacy-preserving way for customers to present digital credentials online or in person. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/about-the-commission/government-digital-delivery-agency/trust-framework-for-digital-identity/trust-framework-authority/trust-framework-register?utm_source=openai))

At the same time, the public-facing Govt.nz app material says users can already set up the wallet and that the first accredited digital credentials are expected to become available from **late August 2026**. That is significant because it turns the state’s digital identity and service-access strategy into a more tangible AI-adjacent service platform, even if the AI layer itself remains mostly behind the scenes. ([govt.nz](https://www.govt.nz/about/the-govt-nz-app/?utm_source=openai))

### 2) Health NZ made its AI governance model more visible

Health NZ’s public materials now make the **National Artificial Intelligence and Algorithm Expert Advisory Group (NAIAEAG)** much clearer. The group’s published remit covers oversight of AI research, development, partnerships, contracts, and implementation across Health NZ, and all AI development or implementation plans must be registered with it. The group is explicitly tasked with assessing safety, effectiveness, ethics, legality, and social and cultural licence. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/about-us/who-we-are/expert-groups-and-networks/expert-groups/artificial-intelligence-and-algorithm-expert-advisory-group?utm_source=openai))

Health NZ’s privacy statement also now states that when AI is used, it operates in a **closed environment**, that personal information will not leave Health NZ’s technology environment for commercial model-building, and that clinical decisions remain with qualified healthcare professionals. This is one of the clearest official descriptions yet of New Zealand’s preferred operating model for sensitive public-sector AI. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/privacy/privacy-statement?utm_source=openai))

### 3) The symptom checker programme advanced, but stayed tightly bounded

On **July 27, 2026**, Health NZ said its symptom checker work had identified enough potential benefit to justify further exploration and would move into a **proof-of-concept** phase. The organisation says one or more tools will be tested in controlled conditions, with strong clinical oversight, and that the findings will inform a decision in **mid-to-late 2027** on whether to begin a formal procurement process. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/news-and-updates/symptom-checker-update-july-2026?utm_source=openai))

This matters because it shows New Zealand’s public health system testing a public-facing AI navigation use case without jumping straight to national deployment. That is consistent with the wider pattern: limited scope first, governance first, and evidence before scale. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/news-and-updates/symptom-checker-update-july-2026?utm_source=openai))

### 4) The pipeline is expanding through procurement, not just pilots

Recent GETS notices show public agencies moving from experimentation into formal market engagement. NZTA opened an **Expression of Interest** on **July 23, 2026** for an **AI-enabled project forecasting and delivery confidence tool** designed to analyse cost, risk, schedule, and assurance data and generate explainable early-warning signals. ([gets.govt.nz](https://www.gets.govt.nz/NZTAHNO/ExternalTenderDetails.htm?id=34538552))

Separately, New Zealand Police released an RFP for a replacement **Automated Biometric Identification System (ABIS)**, describing biometrics as a critical operational capability and seeking a modern, secure, scalable platform that includes fingerprint and image management, including facial-recognition capability. ([gets.govt.nz](https://www.gets.govt.nz/NZP/ExternalTenderDetails.htm?id=34381453&utm_source=openai))

### 5) The biometrics deadline has now passed

In the previous edition, the biometric code deadline was an approaching governance test. As of **August 18, 2026**, that date is now in the past: the **Biometric Processing Privacy Code 2025** transition period for biometric processing already in place before **November 3, 2025** ended on **August 3, 2026**. ([privacy.org.nz](https://www.privacy.org.nz/privacy-principles/codes-of-practice/biometric-processing-privacy-code/?utm_source=openai))

That does not mean all biometric systems are problematic; it means biometric uses across the public sector now sit in a stricter live compliance environment at exactly the moment agencies are also procuring new biometric and identity infrastructure. ([privacy.org.nz](https://www.privacy.org.nz/privacy-principles/codes-of-practice/biometric-processing-privacy-code/?utm_source=openai))

## Current State of AI Adoption

## Governance and Operating Model

The public-sector governance stack is still led by the **Public Service AI Framework**, the **Responsible AI Guidance for the Public Service: GenAI**, and the **Public Service AI Work Programme**. The work programme describes **15 initiatives across 4 focus areas**: common use tools, safe and responsible AI, customer and partnerships, and AI workforce. Published deliverables include a **Public Service AI Hub**, a sandbox, annual use-case measurement, AI marketplace categories, public AI events, and executive and practitioner training. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/standards-and-guidance/technology-and-architecture/artificial-intelligence/public-service-ai-work-programme))

Institutionally, AI stewardship now sits more firmly inside the broader state redesign effort. The **GDDA** was established on **April 1, 2026** inside the Public Service Commission, taking on the functions of the former Government Chief Digital Office. The **July 2, 2026** rapid review then reinforced that the centre needs stronger system leadership, better prioritisation, and more influence over funding, design, and procurement. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/news/government-digital-delivery-agency-established?utm_source=openai))

The Digital Government Target State remains the clearest official expression of where AI fits. It requires agencies to align with shared Digital Public Infrastructure and explicitly includes **AI broker/gateway**, **AI platform services**, **semantic search**, and **data and AI safeguards**. In effect, New Zealand’s intended model is increasingly “common AI rails, agency-specific use cases.” ([dns.govt.nz](https://dns.govt.nz/digital-government/digitising-government-programme-dgp/digital-target-state?utm_source=openai))

## Scale and Maturity

The latest published whole-of-government baseline is still the **2025 cross-agency AI survey**. It records **272 AI use cases across 70 agencies**, including **55 operational deployments**, up from **15 operational use cases in 2024**. The report says agencies are seeing productivity and efficiency gains and that reuse of existing solutions is increasing. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/dmsdocument/263~report-2025-cross-agency-survey-for-artificial-intelligence-ai-use-cases/html))

The maturity profile is still uneven. Reported barriers remain **lack of skills and capability**, **anticipated costs**, **security**, and **privacy**. The survey also notes that direct customer-facing AI still represents only a small share of reported use cases, with most adoption concentrated in supporting functions such as administration, communications, and data work. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/dmsdocument/263~report-2025-cross-agency-survey-for-artificial-intelligence-ai-use-cases/html))

Workforce readiness is rising, but depth is limited. The 2025 Public Service Census found **33%** of public servants had used AI for work and **14%** used it regularly. That is meaningful uptake, but not yet broad institutional normalisation. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/data/public-service-census/delivery/innovation?utm_source=openai))

## Shared Infrastructure and Service Delivery

The **Govt.nz app** is now one of the most important practical expressions of the shared-infrastructure strategy. Official programme material says **Version 2.0** is live, the **Government Credential Issuance Platform** has been released, and the app currently includes a secure digital wallet plus in-app emergency alert capability. The same programme page says digital credentials are to be issued progressively and secure messaging has been positioned for agency onboarding. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/digital-government/key-areas-of-work/government-app-programme))

Public-facing Govt.nz app guidance is more specific on near-term timing: it says the wallet is available to set up now and that the first accredited credentials are expected from **late August 2026**. ([govt.nz](https://www.govt.nz/about/the-govt-nz-app/?utm_source=openai))

The significance for AI is architectural. The app is not itself the public sector’s main AI story; it is the channel through which future AI-assisted discovery, messaging, identity, and service navigation can be standardised across agencies. The AI Work Programme explicitly says a **Govt.nz AI Assistant** is intended to help people find services and information and to be available on the app. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/standards-and-guidance/technology-and-architecture/artificial-intelligence/public-service-ai-work-programme))

## Public Trust and Social Licence

New Zealand still has a relatively strong trust base in public services overall. In the **March 2026** Kiwis Count quarter, **84%** of New Zealanders trusted the government service they most recently used, and **64%** trusted the Public Service brand. The same results also showed higher satisfaction among people who used exclusively digital channels. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/data/kiwis-count?utm_source=openai))

But AI-specific trust is much thinner. Kantar’s **November 2025** research found only **4%** of New Zealanders felt well informed about how government agencies use AI, while **66%** wanted the option to deal with a human rather than AI and **60%** supported independent oversight. ([kantarnewzealand.com](https://www.kantarnewzealand.com/ai-in-the-public-sector/?utm_source=openai))

The trust issue is therefore not simple rejection of government digital services. It is a narrower legitimacy problem around **transparency, explainability, and human fallback** in AI-enabled services. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/data/kiwis-count?utm_source=openai))

## Recent News and Policy Developments

### Digital delivery is still in reset mode

The **rapid review** released on **July 2, 2026** remains the strongest recent whole-of-system policy signal. It found fragmentation, duplication, weak prioritisation, and limited central leverage over funding and procurement. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/news/rapid-review-proposes-reset-for-digital-delivery))

The accompanying reset plan goes further, arguing that digital public infrastructure and AI should be treated as foundational capabilities. It explicitly frames **AI** as the “new enabling capability” that can help realise the benefits of digital public infrastructure sooner. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/assets/DirectoryFile/Digital-Reset-Plan-2026.pdf))

### Health NZ is shifting from isolated deployments to governed scale

Health NZ’s July and late-July materials show a maturing model: **HealthX** is connected to international evidence through **UCLPartners**, **NAIAEAG** provides national oversight, and the symptom checker work is being held inside controlled testing rather than rushed implementation. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/news-and-updates/health-nz-international-partnership-strengthens-approach-to-digital-and-ai-innovation?utm_source=openai))

A second sign of maturation is internal productivity adoption. Health NZ’s **BroPilot** story says the HealthX Microsoft Copilot initiative expanded to **more than 2,050 licences** across Health NZ from January, positioning AI adoption as secure exploration and workforce enablement rather than automation of accountable judgement. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/news-and-updates/bropilot-grounding-digital-tools-in-whanau-culture-and-care?utm_source=openai))

### Local government continues to act as an experimentation layer

Hutt City Council remains the clearest visible local-government adopter. Its public AI page describes formal strategy and policy documents, public disclosure of use, and reported gains such as turning handwritten City Summit submissions into a detailed report in **two days** instead of work that could have taken weeks. ([huttcity.govt.nz](https://www.huttcity.govt.nz/council/our-projects/ai-at-council))

Environment Canterbury represents a governance-first version of the same trend. Its re-established AI Working Group is explicitly aimed at understanding AI use, trends, and governance, with workshops generally open to the public. ([ecan.govt.nz](https://www.ecan.govt.nz/get-involved/news-and-events/2026/council-re-establishes-ai-governance-working-group))

## Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Health NZ emergency-department AI scribe

Health New Zealand’s emergency-department AI scribe remains the clearest example of production AI at national scale in New Zealand’s public sector. Announced on **February 28, 2026**, it was rolled out to all emergency departments and to **1,250** doctors and frontline staff, with pilot evidence that doctors using it saw **one additional patient per shift on average**. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

**Why it matters:** it is large-scale AI in real frontline operations, but still firmly in the augmentation category: documentation support, not autonomous clinical judgement. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

### Case Study 2: HealthX, UCLPartners, and BroPilot

Health NZ says **HealthX** has entered an agreement with **UCLPartners** to support the assessment, testing, and scaling of digital and AI innovation, including AI scribes, mental-health use cases, AI-enabled diagnostics, and better information integration. Separately, Health NZ says the HealthX Microsoft Copilot initiative expanded to more than **2,050 licences**, with **BroPilot** showing how adoption can be culturally grounded and workforce-focused. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/news-and-updates/health-nz-international-partnership-strengthens-approach-to-digital-and-ai-innovation?utm_source=openai))

**Why it matters:** HealthX increasingly looks like a national AI adoption function, not just an innovation brand. It connects validation, workflow design, secure rollout, and scale. ([healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/news-and-updates/health-nz-international-partnership-strengthens-approach-to-digital-and-ai-innovation?utm_source=openai))

### Case Study 3: Govt.nz app and accredited digital identity infrastructure

GDDA’s **July 15, 2026** accreditation for Govt.nz app authentication and facilitation services, combined with the app’s live wallet and expected late-August credential rollout, makes the app the strongest example of shared digital public infrastructure moving into public use. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/about-the-commission/government-digital-delivery-agency/trust-framework-for-digital-identity/trust-framework-authority/trust-framework-register?utm_source=openai))

**Why it matters:** while not a standalone AI deployment, it is the kind of standardised service layer on which future AI-assisted search, service discovery, identity-mediated interactions, and secure messaging can be built across agencies. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/standards-and-guidance/technology-and-architecture/artificial-intelligence/public-service-ai-work-programme))

### Case Study 4: Hutt City Council’s transparent local-government model

Hutt City Council publicly documents why it is using AI, its policy and strategy, and where it is applying the technology. It reports concrete administrative gains, including turning handwritten submissions into a report in days and using AI to support faster, clearer service delivery. ([huttcity.govt.nz](https://www.huttcity.govt.nz/council/our-projects/ai-at-council))

**Why it matters:** Hutt shows what mature sub-national adoption looks like in New Zealand: public disclosure, governance artefacts, bounded use cases, and tangible workflow benefits. ([huttcity.govt.nz](https://www.huttcity.govt.nz/council/our-projects/ai-at-council))

### Case Study 5: Environment Canterbury’s governance-first approach

Environment Canterbury re-established its AI Working Group in **May 2026** to examine how AI is being used to analyse data, support decision-making, and serve communities, with public-facing workshops intended as part of that work. ([ecan.govt.nz](https://www.ecan.govt.nz/get-involved/news-and-events/2026/council-re-establishes-ai-governance-working-group))

**Why it matters:** some of New Zealand’s most important public-sector AI work is still upstream of deployment. Governance capability itself is becoming a formal public function. ([ecan.govt.nz](https://www.ecan.govt.nz/get-involved/news-and-events/2026/council-re-establishes-ai-governance-working-group))

## Adoption Patterns and Trends

### 1) Shared capability is still beating fragmented experimentation

The Digital Government Target State, the app architecture, and the rapid review all point in the same direction: common platforms, common assurance, and reusable components are becoming the preferred state model. ([dns.govt.nz](https://dns.govt.nz/digital-government/digitising-government-programme-dgp/digital-target-state?utm_source=openai))

### 2) Human accountability remains the hard boundary

Across regulator guidance, Health NZ rules, and real deployments, AI is still positioned as support for human work rather than a substitute for accountable decision-makers. ([regulation.govt.nz](https://www.regulation.govt.nz/news/helping-regulators-use-ai-with-confidence/?utm_source=openai))

### 3) Health is still the sector’s most advanced AI domain

No other public domain currently combines national production deployment, explicit expert oversight, domain-specific privacy framing, international evidence partnerships, and internal workforce AI scaling in the way health now does. ([beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/ai-scribe-now-every-emergency-department?utm_source=openai))

### 4) Procurement is becoming a more important maturity signal

Recent GETS notices suggest the next stage of AI adoption will be shaped less by isolated pilots and more by formal sourcing of enterprise tools and infrastructure, especially in transport, policing, and identity-linked systems. ([gets.govt.nz](https://www.gets.govt.nz/NZTAHNO/ExternalTenderDetails.htm?id=34538552))

### 5) Compliance and legitimacy pressures are rising alongside adoption

The biometric privacy deadline has passed, public understanding of government AI remains weak, and the MBIE biometric project failure remains a live reminder that poor governance can damage confidence as much as the technology itself. ([privacy.org.nz](https://www.privacy.org.nz/privacy-principles/codes-of-practice/biometric-processing-privacy-code/?utm_source=openai))

## Pressure Points and Risks

### Skills, cost, security, and privacy still constrain uptake

These remain the most consistently reported barriers in the official cross-agency survey. They explain why use-case growth has outpaced truly even maturity across the system. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/dmsdocument/263~report-2025-cross-agency-survey-for-artificial-intelligence-ai-use-cases/html))

### Social licence is still thinner than general trust in government

The gap between strong service trust and weak public understanding of AI use remains the most important strategic constraint on expansion, especially for public-facing services. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/data/kiwis-count?utm_source=openai))

### Delivery credibility remains fragile

The **MBIE Biometric Capability Update** failure, which MBIE says consumed **$33 million** without delivering anything, and the associated Public Service Commission investigation into integrity concerns continue to cast a shadow over higher-risk digital and biometric programmes. ([mbie.govt.nz](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/about/news/mbie-welcomes-public-service-commission-investigation-after-critical-independent-review?utm_source=openai))

### Biometrics is the sharpest immediate governance edge

Police procurement activity shows biometrics remains operationally important, but the legal and trust settings around biometrics are now tighter after the **August 3, 2026** code transition deadline. ([gets.govt.nz](https://www.gets.govt.nz/NZP/ExternalTenderDetails.htm?id=34381453&utm_source=openai))

## Overall Assessment

As of **August 18, 2026**, AI adoption in New Zealand’s public sector is best described as **institutionalising under shared governance**. The sector is no longer in a framework-only phase. It has a central AI work programme, a shared digital architecture, a stronger institutional home in GDDA, visible production deployments, formal health-sector oversight, and a more tangible citizen-facing service layer through the Govt.nz app and digital wallet. ([digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/standards-and-guidance/technology-and-architecture/artificial-intelligence/public-service-ai-work-programme))

The major change since the **July 13, 2026** edition is not a sudden expansion in autonomous AI. It is the steady conversion of strategy into operating infrastructure: accredited identity rails, clearer health AI governance, controlled proof-of-concept testing for public-facing tools, and a more visible procurement pipeline for next-wave use cases. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/about-the-commission/government-digital-delivery-agency/trust-framework-for-digital-identity/trust-framework-authority/trust-framework-register?utm_source=openai))

New Zealand’s public sector remains cautious by design. But it is becoming more deliberate, more centralised, and more operationally serious about AI. The near-term question is no longer whether agencies will try AI. It is whether the state can scale **shared platforms, trusted safeguards, and public legitimacy** quickly enough to keep up with adoption pressure already building inside the system. ([publicservice.govt.nz](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/news/rapid-review-proposes-reset-for-digital-delivery))
