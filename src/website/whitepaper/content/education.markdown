# AI in Education in New Zealand: A Living Whitepaper  
*Updated for publication on 29 May 2026*

## Executive Snapshot

AI adoption in New Zealand education has advanced further since the previous update on **2 April 2026**, but the center of gravity has shifted. The most important developments over the past eight weeks have not been fresh nationwide usage surveys; they have been **curriculum design, assessment infrastructure, and system-level governance**. In practical terms, AI in education is becoming more embedded in official tools, qualification design, school policy, and tertiary governance. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/our-work/publications/corporate-documents/budget-2026/investing-secondary-achievement))

Three developments define the current moment. First, the school system now has another live operational AI use case: the Ministry’s **SMART** assessment platform says it uses AI assistance for marking open-ended questions, while keeping teacher judgement central and placing student data under Ministry stewardship. Second, the senior secondary reform programme has moved from broad direction to **active consultation and qualification detail**, including consultation on the first 26 Years 11–13 subjects and confirmed details of the qualifications replacing NCEA from 2028. Third, **Budget 2026** has made AI in assessment more explicit by funding NZQA pilots and proof-of-concepts for auto text scoring, AI and machine learning in marking, moderation, and exam development. ([newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz](https://newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz/smart-overview-and-access/5637300577.p?activeTab=tab%3A3))

Overall, the sector is best described as **broadly adopted, unevenly governed, and increasingly institutionalised**. The strongest evidence still shows widespread teacher use and strong demand for AI-related professional learning, while the newest developments show government agencies and universities building the systems, rules, and oversight needed for more durable adoption. ([educationcounts.govt.nz](https://www.educationcounts.govt.nz/publications/series/teaching-and-learning-international-survey/talis-2024-publications/talis-2024-first-report?utm_source=openai))

## What Has Changed Since 2 April 2026

- **The Years 0–10 consultation window closed, and the rollout timetable changed.** Consultation on the remaining Years 0–10 curriculum content closed on **24 April 2026**. On **14 May 2026**, the Government extended the implementation timeframe for most Years 0–8 learning areas to **2029**, while keeping **all Years 9–10 content** on track for implementation from **2027**. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/news/whats-new-term-2-2026))
- **Senior secondary curriculum work moved into live consultation.** On **15 May 2026**, consultation opened on the first group of **26 Years 11–13 subjects**, with feedback open until **15 June 2026**. The work is being developed with input from more than **300 teachers and education leaders**, following earlier work involving **212 subject advisors**. ([tahurangi.education.govt.nz](https://tahurangi.education.govt.nz/Phase-5-consultation-group-1))
- **The post-NCEA system became more concrete.** On **16 May 2026**, the Government confirmed further details of the new senior secondary qualifications: **NZCE** for Year 12, **NZACE** for Year 13, plus a **Foundational Award** at Year 11 focused on literacy and numeracy. Tāhūrangi’s detail adds that students will need three passed subjects plus the Foundational Award for NZCE/NZACE, and that subjects will use a nationwide assessment model with a new **A+ to E** grading scale. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/news/more-details-about-new-senior-secondary-qualifications))
- **Budget 2026 put money behind AI-enabled assessment operations.** On **28 May 2026**, the Ministry published Budget 2026 measures including **$20.1 million** for Years 11–13 teacher PLD, **$61.2 million** for Years 9–13 curriculum resources, **$29.5 million** for NZQA’s Strategic Technology Enhancement Project, and **$2.1 million** for NZQA pilots using AI and machine learning in marking, moderation, and exam development. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/our-work/publications/corporate-documents/budget-2026/investing-secondary-achievement))
- **School AI literacy delivery continued to move from pilot to visible programme.** The Education Gazette’s **24 April 2026** feature on Day of AI Aotearoa reported a nine-school pilot, localisation for New Zealand, and measurable gains in student understanding and ethical awareness. ([gazette.education.govt.nz](https://gazette.education.govt.nz/articles/the-day-of-ai-a-teachers-drive-for-responsible-ai-literacy-in-new-zealand-schools/))

## Overall State of Adoption

### Schools and kura: AI is mainstream in teacher practice, but still strongest in augmentation

The best national benchmark remains **TALIS 2024**. It found that **69% of New Zealand Year 7–10 teachers** had used AI, versus **36%** across the OECD lower-secondary average. Among New Zealand teachers who had used AI, the most common uses were generating lesson plans (**78%**) and learning about or summarising a topic (**73%**), while only **12%** used AI to assess students’ work. ([educationcounts.govt.nz](https://www.educationcounts.govt.nz/publications/series/teaching-and-learning-international-survey/talis-2024-publications/talis-2024-first-report?utm_source=openai))

NZCER’s school-level evidence points in the same direction. Its AI-focused primary study found teachers were using generative AI chiefly for **lesson planning**, **assessment design**, and **personalising learning materials**, while also highlighting major gaps in policy, resourcing, and professional learning. Its nationally representative 2024 primary teacher survey found **46%** of teachers reported using AI tools in teaching, and **53%** selected AI as a professional learning priority. ([nzcer.org.nz](https://www.nzcer.org.nz/research/publications/generative-artificial-intelligence-aotearoa-new-zealand-primary-schools?utm_source=openai))

The pattern is consistent across sources: most use is still **assistive rather than substitutive**. Teachers are using AI for planning, drafting, summarising, adaptation, and support, while official guidance continues to insist that teachers remain central, outputs must be checked, and personal data should not be entered into tools. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/education-professionals/schools-year-0-13/digital-technology/generative-ai/?utm_source=openai))

### Assessment infrastructure: operational AI is now part of the system

One of the clearest changes since the prior update is that operational AI is no longer confined to isolated pilots. The Ministry’s **SMART** platform, which schools can use to meet the new twice-yearly assessment requirements for Years 3–8 in 2026, explicitly says it uses AI assistance for marking open-ended questions, while stressing that it is not intended to replace teacher professional judgement. The same FAQ says the Ministry is kaitiaki of student data in SMART and that the platform provider cannot use the data for its own purposes. ([newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz](https://newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz/smart-overview-and-access/5637300577.p?activeTab=tab%3A3))

At national qualification level, NZQA remains the most mature AI operator in the sector. NZQA says it used automated text scoring to mark **more than 55,000 writing literacy assessments** in **May 2025**, with experienced human markers double-checking over a third of results, especially near achievement boundaries. Budget 2026 now extends that direction by funding first-phase pilots and proof-of-concepts for AI and machine learning in marking, moderation, and exam development. ([www2.nzqa.govt.nz](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/about-us/news/embracing-ai-in-student-assessments/?utm_source=openai))

This makes assessment the most institutionally advanced area of AI adoption in New Zealand education. That is an inference from the current source base, but it is strongly supported by the combination of live school assessment tooling, NZQA’s production use, and fresh Budget 2026 funding for expanded pilots. ([newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz](https://newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz/smart-overview-and-access/5637300577.p?activeTab=tab%3A3))

### Tertiary: governed use is replacing blanket caution

The tertiary sector continues to move toward **managed, documented, assessment-specific AI use**. Massey University’s AI Use Framework ranges from **No AI** to **AI planning**, **AI collaboration**, and **AI exploration**, while still prohibiting AI in secured assessments such as exams and practicals. Students must keep records of the tools, prompts, outputs, and modifications they used. ([massey.ac.nz](https://www.massey.ac.nz/study/study-and-assignment-support-and-guides/academic-integrity-student-guide/artificial-intelligence-ai-usage-and-detection/))

Lincoln University’s current guidance is similarly rules-based. Students are told to follow examiner guidance, assume AI is not allowed where guidance is absent, acknowledge and cite all AI use, and avoid over-reliance on tools that can hallucinate, reproduce bias, or weaken genuine learning. Lincoln also warns that grammar and paraphrasing tools can themselves count as GenAI use. ([ltl.lincoln.ac.nz](https://ltl.lincoln.ac.nz/learning-and-research-skills/getting-started/ai-and-assessments/))

A notable step up in institutional maturity is the University of Otago’s **AI Governance Policy**, approved on **10 March 2026**. It applies across development, deployment, procurement, and use of AI; embeds Māori data sovereignty and Te Ao Māori principles; places ultimate accountability with Council; and requires risk assessment before AI is used on confidential or restricted information. ([otago.ac.nz](https://www.otago.ac.nz/about/administration/policies/policy-collection/ai-governance-policy))

## Policy, Governance, and Curriculum

### Curriculum embedding is now more explicit than it was in early April

The refreshed curriculum is showing AI more clearly across stages. In Years 9–10 Technology, Computer Science is described as focusing on algorithms, data, logic in digital systems, and AI, with explicit attention to the ethical implications of intelligent technologies and a glossary that defines machine learning. ([newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz](https://newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz/5637293836.p))

At Years 11–13, published subject descriptors show AI being embedded across multiple subjects rather than isolated in one niche course. **Computer Science** includes “artificial intelligence and intelligent systems”; **Media, Journalism & Communications** includes AI-generated texts, bias, misinformation, and source verification; and Digital Technologies addresses social, cultural, and ethical implications of digital tools. ([newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz](https://newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz/years-11-13-new-zealand-curriculum-subject-descriptors/5637308828.p))

The latest shift is procedural as well as curricular: the first group of Years 11–13 draft subjects is already out for consultation, which means AI’s place in the senior curriculum is no longer just implied by descriptors but is now part of live national curriculum drafting. ([tahurangi.education.govt.nz](https://tahurangi.education.govt.nz/Phase-5-consultation-group-1))

### Governance is tightening around privacy, authenticity, and cultural legitimacy

Ministry guidance remains principles-based but firm: schools should not enter personal data into AI tools, should check outputs for bias and error, must keep teachers central, and should maintain a school policy on acceptable use. The Ministry also states that **GenAI is not permitted in NCEA external assessment**, and that schools must have a policy on acceptable GenAI use for NCEA practice. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/education-professionals/schools-year-0-13/digital-technology/generative-ai/?utm_source=openai))

Privacy expectations have also become more salient. The Privacy Commissioner’s AI guidance says the Privacy Act applies to everyone using AI tools in New Zealand and recommends a **Privacy Impact Assessment** before use. A refreshed AI-and-privacy guidance PDF was published in **April 2026**, and the education-sector privacy guidance notes that **Principle 3A** on indirect collection applies from **1 May 2026**. ([privacy.org.nz](https://www.privacy.org.nz/resources-and-learning/a-z-topics/ai/?utm_source=openai))

New Zealand’s distinct governance feature remains the explicit treatment of **Māori data sovereignty**. Tāhūrangi’s AI introduction states that Māori data is a taonga and that AI cannot replace engagement with local whānau, hapū, and iwi. Otago’s governance policy likewise embeds Māori data sovereignty and Te Ao Māori principles in institution-wide AI oversight. ([newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz](https://newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz/introduction-to-artificial-intelligence/5637235331.p))

## Research Overview

No new nationwide AI-use dataset was identified after the previous **2 April 2026** update. The strongest current benchmarks therefore remain **TALIS 2024** and NZCER’s 2025 primary-school studies. That means the evidence base is still slightly lagging the pace of implementation. This is an inference from the source set reviewed, not a formal Ministry statement. ([educationcounts.govt.nz](https://www.educationcounts.govt.nz/publications/series/teaching-and-learning-international-survey/talis-2024-publications/talis-2024-first-report?utm_source=openai))

What those benchmarks show is clear. Teachers are using AI at meaningful scale, especially for preparation and support tasks; demand for professional learning is high; and concern about accuracy, bias, privacy, and authenticity remains substantial. TALIS also shows that New Zealand teachers are ahead of OECD peers in AI use, while NZCER’s work shows that even AI-interested teachers often lack confidence and want more training. ([educationcounts.govt.nz](https://www.educationcounts.govt.nz/publications/series/teaching-and-learning-international-survey/talis-2024-publications/talis-2024-first-report?utm_source=openai))

More recent evidence is narrower but still useful. The Day of AI Aotearoa pilot reported that **92%** of students had tried AI tools at baseline, while many could not explain how AI works; after the programme, one primary school saw self-reported ability to explain AI rise from **20% to 64%**, and one secondary school from **50% to 82%**. That is not a national dataset, but it is one of the clearest current indicators that structured AI literacy programmes can shift student understanding quickly. ([gazette.education.govt.nz](https://gazette.education.govt.nz/articles/the-day-of-ai-a-teachers-drive-for-responsible-ai-literacy-in-new-zealand-schools/))

## Case Studies

### 1) SMART: AI inside routine school assessment operations

SMART is significant because it moves AI from optional teacher experimentation into a Ministry-supported assessment tool that schools can use in required reporting cycles for Years 3–8. Its design keeps human judgement central, but the fact that AI-assisted marking is already acknowledged in system infrastructure marks a step-change in normalisation. ([newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz](https://newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz/smart-overview-and-access/5637300577.p?activeTab=tab%3A3))

### 2) Day of AI Aotearoa and Westlake Girls’ High School: AI literacy at delivery stage

The **24 April 2026** Education Gazette feature shows Day of AI Aotearoa moving beyond concept stage. The nine-school pilot was localised for New Zealand, incorporated te ao Māori values, and produced measurable gains in student understanding and ethical awareness. Westlake Girls’ has also translated this work into a schoolwide AI strategy, policy, and guidelines. ([gazette.education.govt.nz](https://gazette.education.govt.nz/articles/the-day-of-ai-a-teachers-drive-for-responsible-ai-literacy-in-new-zealand-schools/))

### 3) Aotea College and Hobsonville Point Secondary School: assessment authenticity by design

The Ministry’s GenAI case studies remain two of the clearest school governance exemplars. Aotea College built a staff AI group and updated schoolwide assessment policy. Hobsonville Point created checkpoints, version-history checks, verbal verification, and restorative processes, with the Ministry case study reporting a significant drop in inappropriate AI use in assessments. ([web-assets.education.govt.nz](https://web-assets.education.govt.nz/s3fs-public/2025-03/GenAI%20Case%20Study%20Aotea%20College_MAR2025.pdf?utm_source=openai))

### 4) NZQA: scaled AI in national assessment workflows

NZQA’s automated text scoring remains the sector’s most consequential production AI use because it sits inside a nationally significant assessment process and combines scale with human quality assurance. Budget 2026 now signals that NZQA is being funded not just to maintain but to extend this direction through pilots in marking, moderation, and exam development. ([www2.nzqa.govt.nz](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/about-us/news/embracing-ai-in-student-assessments/?utm_source=openai))

### 5) Tertiary innovation with cultural grounding: University of Auckland te reo Māori tools

The University of Auckland’s AI-powered te reo Māori pronunciation project remains one of the strongest examples of culturally grounded educational AI innovation in New Zealand. The project received a **$1 million** Smart Ideas grant and is designed to provide real-time personalised feedback; the University’s **2026–2030** te reo Māori strategy also references AI-powered pronunciation tools as part of broader language revitalisation work. ([auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2025/08/04/AI-maori-pronunciation.html?utm_source=openai))

## Emerging Trends

### 1) The big story is operationalisation, not novelty

Since early April, the clearest movement has been from experimentation to **system design**: assessment platforms, Budget lines, curriculum consultation, qualification architecture, and university governance all now treat AI as something to be structured rather than merely debated. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/our-work/publications/corporate-documents/budget-2026/investing-secondary-achievement))

### 2) AI literacy is becoming a mainstream capability

Day of AI Aotearoa, Kōtui Ako’s AI Digital Literacy offering, Tāhūrangi’s AI teaching resources, and the explicit AI content in Years 9–13 curriculum materials all point in the same direction: AI literacy is becoming a normal part of what schools are expected to address. ([gazette.education.govt.nz](https://gazette.education.govt.nz/articles/the-day-of-ai-a-teachers-drive-for-responsible-ai-literacy-in-new-zealand-schools/))

### 3) Assessment is where the highest-stakes AI adoption is happening

SMART, NZQA’s automated text scoring, and Budget 2026’s new NZQA AI pilots together show that assessment is now the leading edge of formal AI deployment in the education system. Human oversight remains central, but AI is increasingly part of the machinery around scoring, moderation, and assessment operations. ([newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz](https://newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz/smart-overview-and-access/5637300577.p?activeTab=tab%3A3))

### 4) Governance quality is becoming the sector’s main differentiator

The strongest institutions are no longer those merely using AI; they are the ones building explicit rules on privacy, disclosure, oversight, assessment conditions, and cultural legitimacy. That is visible in Ministry guidance, privacy expectations, Otago’s governance framework, and school-level authenticity redesign. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/education-professionals/schools-year-0-13/digital-technology/generative-ai/?utm_source=openai))

## Conclusion

As of **29 May 2026**, AI in education in New Zealand is best understood as **maturing through infrastructure, governance, and curriculum reform**. Classroom use is already widespread, but the bigger current story is that AI is being built into official assessment systems, national qualification planning, curriculum architecture, and tertiary governance. ([educationcounts.govt.nz](https://www.educationcounts.govt.nz/publications/series/teaching-and-learning-international-survey/talis-2024-publications/talis-2024-first-report?utm_source=openai))

The most important change since **2 April 2026** is therefore not a single tool or school example. It is the emergence of a more visible operating model: AI literacy programmes are live, AI is more explicit in curriculum documents, national qualification reform is active, and Budget 2026 has put direct funding behind AI-enabled assessment development. ([gazette.education.govt.nz](https://gazette.education.govt.nz/articles/the-day-of-ai-a-teachers-drive-for-responsible-ai-literacy-in-new-zealand-schools/))

New Zealand is still not moving toward an “AI replaces educators” model. The evidence instead points to a model where AI supports planning, literacy-building, assessment operations, and selected learning innovations, while **human judgement, authenticity, privacy, and Māori data sovereignty** become more—not less—important. ([education.govt.nz](https://www.education.govt.nz/education-professionals/schools-year-0-13/digital-technology/generative-ai/?utm_source=openai))
